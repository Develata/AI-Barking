                                 NATURE OF THE ACTION

       1.      In this lawsuit, the USA TODAY Plaintiffs join the long list of copyright holders

who have filed lawsuits against OpenAI and other AI companies for copyright infringement, many

of which have been consolidated in this Court.

       2.      Together, the USA TODAY Plaintiffs own copyrights in content published by 19

different publications: USA TODAY, The Tennessean, Indy Star, The Bergen Record, The

Enquirer, Asbury Park Press, Democrat & Chronicle, The Knoxville News-Sentinel, Naples Daily

News, The Oklahoman, Milwaukee Journal Sentinel, The Columbus Dispatch, The Arizona

Republic, The Courier-Journal, The Des Moines Register, Detroit Free Press, The Detroit News,

The Palm Beach Post, and Star News (collectively, the “USA TODAY Publications”).

       3.      The USA TODAY Plaintiffs have protected the value of their print and online work.

Each of their publications includes copyright management information as defined in 17 U.S.C.

§ 1202(c). Their registered copyrights are attached as Exhibit A. This material is referred to

collectively as the “USA TODAY Plaintiffs’ content.”

       4.      OpenAI’s large language models (“LLMs”) were trained on copyrighted material

scraped from the internet without authorization—regardless of paywalls or other access

restrictions—and without regard for the rights of publishers and authors. As Sam Altman,

OpenAI’s founder and CEO, candidly acknowledged in testimony before the British House of

Lords, his company’s models depend on copyrighted material:

       Because copyright today covers virtually every sort of human expression—
       including blog posts, photographs, forum posts, scraps of software code,
       and government documents—it would be impossible to train today’s
       leading AI models without using copyrighted materials. Limiting training
       data to public domain books and drawings created more than a century

        5.       A Microsoft executive described OpenAI’s unauthorized use of copyrighted

content as “an astonishing theft of unprecedented proportions” and perhaps the “largest theft of

labor in human history.”2

        6.       OpenAI deliberately chose to use copyrighted works without permission,

disregarding the rights of authors and publishers whose livelihoods depend on respect for their

creative efforts. OpenAI did not merely steal the copyrighted works used to train its models; it did

so using programs designed to strip away Copyright Management Information (“CMI”), which

indicated that the works were protected by valid copyrights.

        7.       OpenAI’s internal communications confirm that it deliberately targeted news

content and knew its models were built to reproduce it. OpenAI’s Co-Founder and President Greg

Brockman told his colleagues that OpenAI’s models were “particularly good at predicting text of

news articles,” “excellent at news,” and “very good at any news task.” In a 2020 presentation,

then-OpenAI research leader Dario Amodei listed “News Generation” among GPT-3’s “Skills.”3

        8.       OpenAI’s models have “memorized” the copyrighted works they ingested. Before

OpenAI implemented litigation-inspired safeguards, its models would reproduce verbatim or near-

verbatim copies of copyrighted content when prompted. And because users demand current

information, OpenAI must continuously feed its models new copyrighted material—ensuring that

its infringement is not a past wrong but an ongoing one.

            9.      OpenAI understood that its models memorize training data. OpenAI’s VP of

Research explained: “We train our networks to memorize the training data — that’s their

objective.” By November 2019, OpenAI internally expressed concern about its models

“accidentally regenerating copyrighted works.” By 2020, OpenAI understood that GPT-3 had

memorized training items and that even a single exposure during training “causes some degree of

memorization,” and recognized that its API “might output existing content verbatim.” By 2021,

OpenAI considered prevention of memorization important “for fair use [compliance] and

minimizing copyright violations in model output.” In June 2022, OpenAI employees

acknowledged that GPT-4 would have “memorized a ton of data and therefore will be insanely

good at regurgitation.”4

            10.     Indeed, the “safeguards” OpenAI implemented were designed not to stop

infringement but to conceal it. OpenAI’s output filters did not suppress output of content from any

entity that had not sued it. A Microsoft executive who raised the issue internally called the

approach an “accidental cover up” because it left “people who have a right over the content having

less visibility into what was used for training.”5

            11.     OpenAI’s violations were willful. OpenAI knew that its models had “memorized”

the content OpenAI trained them on and would reproduce it verbatim or near-verbatim when

prompted. These violations continue today and are committed knowingly and intentionally in

disregard of the rights of those who research, investigate, write, and publish the news that

OpenAI’s products appropriate daily.

                                   JURISDICTION AND VENUE

        17.       The Court has subject matter jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. §§ 1331 and 1338(a)

because this action arises under the Copyright Act of 1976, 17 U.S.C. § 101, et seq., as well as

other federal laws.

        18.       Jurisdiction over Defendants is proper because they have purposely availed

themselves of the privilege of conducting business in New York. A substantial portion of OpenAI’s

widespread infringement and other unlawful conduct alleged herein occurred in New York,

including the distribution and sales of OpenAI’s Generative Pre-trained Transformer (“GPT”)-

based products like ChatGPT, ChatGPT Enterprise, and related application programming interface

(API) tools within New York to New York residents. Furthermore, OpenAI maintains offices and

employs personnel in New York who, upon information and belief, were involved in the creation,

maintenance, or monetization of OpenAI’s widespread infringement and other unlawful conduct

alleged herein.

        19.       Venue is proper under 28 U.S.C. § 1400(a) because Defendants and/or their agents

reside or may be found in this District, and because of the infringing and unlawful activities that

occurred in this District.

        20.       Venue is also proper under 28 U.S.C. § 1391(b)(2) because a substantial part of the

events giving rise to the USA TODAY Plaintiffs’ claims occurred in this District, including the

marketing, sales, and licensing of OpenAI products built on the infringement and other misuse of

the USA TODAY Plaintiffs’ intellectual property within this District. Upon information and belief,

OpenAI has sold subscriptions for ChatGPT products to New York residents, and OpenAI enjoys

a substantial base of monthly active users of ChatGPT products in New York. OpenAI has licensed

its GPT models to New York residents and companies headquartered in New York. For example,

C.     How GenAI Models Work

       87.     In the course of developing its models and products, OpenAI has unlawfully

collected, created, and distributed copies of the USA TODAY Plaintiffs’ content in several ways.

       88.     At the collection stage, OpenAI collects and stores a vast amount of content scraped

from the internet, including content scraped from the USA TODAY Publications’ websites.

OpenAI uses the scraped content for a variety of purposes, including creating datasets used to train

the LLMs.

       89.     At the training stage, OpenAI trains its LLMs on the training datasets by a process

that feeds the data through its LLMs to generate output. Appending the output of an LLM to its

input and feeding it back into the model produces sentences and paragraphs word by word. This is

how ChatGPT products generate responses to user queries, or “prompts.”

       90.     LLMs encode as numbers called “parameters” the information from the training

datasets that they use to make these predictions.

       91.     The process of setting the values for an LLM’s parameters during the training

process involves storing copies of the training data (e.g., a USA TODAY article) in computer




                                                24
           Case 1:26-cv-08892            Document 1          Filed 10/08/26        Page 25 of 79




memory, providing a portion of the article to the model, and adjusting the parameters of the model

so that the model accurately predicts the next word in the article.

        92.      After being trained on a large corpus, models may be further subject to “fine-

tuning” by, for example, performing additional rounds of training using specific types of works to

better mimic their content or style, or providing the models with human feedback to reinforce

desired or suppress undesired behaviors in order to improve the model’s ability to follow

instructions.

        93.      Models trained in this way are known to exhibit a behavior called

“memorization.”23 That is, given the right prompt, LLMs will repeat large portions of materials

they were trained on. This phenomenon shows that LLM parameters encode retrievable copies of

many of those training works.

        94.      Memorization is not a bug in OpenAI’s models—it is the design objective. As

OpenAI’s VP of Research stated, “[w]e train our networks to memorize the training data — that’s

their objective.”24

        95.      Once trained, LLMs may be provided with information specific to a use case or

subject matter in order to “ground” their outputs. This is accomplished through a process called

retrieval augmented generation or “RAG.” For example, an LLM may be asked to generate a text

output based on specific external data, such as a document, provided as context. Using this

method, OpenAI’s LLM search applications: (1) receive an input, such as a question; (2) retrieve

relevant documents related to the input prior to generating a response; (3) combine the original
