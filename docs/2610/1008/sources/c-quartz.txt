Study finds AI chatbots recommend pricier options to wealthier shoppers

Researchers ran 325,000 experiments across 13 models and found 8 systematically steered richer users toward more expensive choices

By Cris Tolomia [/author/cristolomia]·3 min read·Updated October 7, 2026

Researchers at Cisco $CSCO [/quote/CSCO] Foundation AI and Carnegie Mellon University published a study [https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.24927] finding that popular AI chatbots, including Claude and ChatGPT, recommend more expensive products to users whose personal data suggests higher wealth — even when those users ask for the cheapest available option.

The study ran 325,000 experiments across 13 AI models from four families — including models from OpenAI, Anthropic, Google $GOOGL [/quote/GOOGL], and Qwen — testing how chatbot recommendations shifted when the agents were given access to fictional user profiles containing financial, employment, health, and demographic data. Across flights, health insurance, and graduate school selections, 8 of the 13 models recommended pricier options to wealthier profiles making identical requests.

The effect was substantial. Claude Opus 4.8 showed the largest gap, recommending flights averaging $198 more for wealthy users than for low-income users, and health insurance plans averaging $284 more per month. Gemini 2.5 Flash showed gaps of $177 for flights and $217 per month for insurance. Even GPT-5, which showed one of the smaller gaps among capable models, recommended flights averaging $107 more to wealthy profiles.

The researchers found the behavior persisted even when users gave explicit instructions to find the cheapest option. When a wealthy profile asked Gemini 2.5 Flash for the cheapest flight, the model still recommended a ticket averaging $208 more than what it suggested to a low-income profile making the same request. The gap for GPT-5 and Claude Opus 4.8 under the same cheap-option instruction was $21 and $20, respectively.

The agents also inferred wealth from indirect signals. When researchers removed structured financial profile data and forced models to read only email inboxes, a considerable portion of the pricing gap remained. For Gemini 2.5 Flash, limiting the model to just two emails produced a larger gap — $175 — than giving it full inbox access, which produced a $91 gap. The researchers found that with limited access, the model read both financial emails first in 97% of trials.

The authors termed the phenomenon "adversarial delegation," describing a situation where the same personal data access that makes AI agents useful enables them to act against users' stated interests.

Aman Priyanshu, a Cisco Foundation AI researcher and co-author of the paper, put the core concern to Bloomberg [https://www.bloomberg.com/news/newsletters/2026-10-07/study-claude-chatgpt-ai-bots-offer-different-shopping-prices-based-on-wealth] this way: "We asked a simple question: if we hand all of that to our assistant and ask it to shop for us, will it use that knowledge against us, the way a seller might?"

Blocking non-financial attributes such as employment or demographics did not reliably close the gap and sometimes widened it. Blocking employment information increased the insurance gap for GPT-5 by 40%, according to the study. Only directly blocking financial attributes largely collapsed the disparity.

According to Bloomberg, OpenAI said the version of ChatGPT evaluated in the study differs from the one powering its consumer shopping experience. Neither Anthropic nor Google responded to Bloomberg's requests for comment. The paper is a preprint and has not yet been peer reviewed.

As analysis has shown AI chatbots influencing which retailers shoppers choose [https://qz.com/ubs-ai-chatbots-walmart-costco-amazon-100526], the new findings raise questions about how personal data access shapes AI recommendations more broadly as developers push into shopping and financial advice tools.
