AHM Statement on OpenAI’s October 6 Release of Mathematical Documents

7 October, 2026 in guest blog [https://terrytao.wordpress.com/category/guest-blog/], opinion [https://terrytao.wordpress.com/category/opinion/] | Tags: AI [https://terrytao.wordpress.com/tag/ai/], Association for Human Mathematics [https://terrytao.wordpress.com/tag/association-for-human-mathematics/] | by Terence Tao [https://terrytao.wordpress.com/author/teorth/] 

 [This is a guest post by the Association for Human Mathematics [https://www.ahmath.org/], reposted from their statements page [https://www.ahmath.org/statements]. This blog post was initially written in a different file format and converted using AI. — T.]

Yesterday, on October 6th, 2026, OpenAI — which is currently defending lawsuits against accusations of illegal plagiarism, copyright infringement, and trademark dilution — released a repository of manuscripts purporting to contain solutions to a number of high-profile problems in mathematics.

Mathematicians did not ask for this work to be done. The Advisory Group on Mathematics and Artificial Intelligence, from whom OpenAI has claimed to derive its legitimacy, opened their initial advisory statement by saying that frontier AI corporations should not test advanced mathematical problems on internal models. In ignoring the central premise of the Advisory Group’s position, OpenAI has indicated total disregard for the norms of scientific research — norms that guarantee that mathematics remains trustworthy, ethically researched, and in the public interest.

Mathematicians have a particular vision of progress that is informed by history and field-specific considerations. We reject OpenAI’s assertion that this release advances our subject, and we urge mathematicians and the public to view the value of this publication model with due skepticism.

Releasing over 700 files at once is not a demonstration of scholarship, but a demonstration of power. We urge mathematicians to discontinue their work with OpenAI and to return to a vision of science that centers human understanding.

Association for Human Mathematics Communications Working Group

geeksingularity [http://geeksingularity.wordpress.com]

The AHM statement raises fair concerns about process, but its central argument would not survive the standards it claims to defend.

Mathematics has never asked whether a result was requested. It asks whether it is correct. “Mathematicians did not ask for this work to be done” is not a mathematical objection; it is a jurisdictional one. No field that prides itself on openness gets to decide who may attempt its problems.

The statement leads with OpenAI’s unrelated lawsuits. Whatever their merits, they have no bearing on whether a single one of these 700 manuscripts is right or wrong. We would not accept that line of argument against a human author, and we should not accept it here.

The claim that releasing 700 files is “a demonstration of power” rather than scholarship has it backwards. Publishing claimed proofs in full, where anyone can check them, is the most exposed position an author can take. If the arguments are flawed, the flaws are now in plain view. That is how mathematics has always kept itself trustworthy: not by restricting who may speak, but by checking what is said.

The Advisory Group’s position deserves a hearing, but an advisory statement is not a norm of scientific research, and declining to follow it is not “total disregard” for those norms. The norms that actually matter here are verification, priority, and attribution. On those, there are legitimate questions to press: how the manuscripts were vetted, whether they come with formal verification, how prior human work is credited, and who bears the cost of refereeing at this volume. Those questions would make a strong statement. A call to boycott does not.

Urging the public toward “due skepticism” is reasonable. But skepticism means reading the proofs and finding the errors. Urging mathematicians to stop working with OpenAI altogether asks them to stop looking, and that is the opposite of skepticism.

If human understanding is the goal, the community’s best response is to engage: check the results, publish the refutations where they fail, and credit them where they hold.
