Why I agreed to join AGMAI

22 September, 2026 in guest blog [https://terrytao.wordpress.com/category/guest-blog/], opinion [https://terrytao.wordpress.com/category/opinion/] | Tags: AI [https://terrytao.wordpress.com/tag/ai/], Martin Hairer [https://terrytao.wordpress.com/tag/martin-hairer/] | by Terence Tao [https://terrytao.wordpress.com/author/teorth/] 

 [This is a guest post by Martin Hairer [https://www.hairer.org/], cross-posted from Proofs and Prompts [https://proofsandprompts.com/2026/09/22/why-i-agreed-to-join-agmai/]. — T.]

There has been a lot of speculation regarding the “Advisory Group on Mathematics and Artifical Intelligence” agmai.org [https://agmai.org/] since it was announced on Monday. In this short blog post, I would like to explain in a bit more detail how the group works, what we are trying to achieve, and why I agreed to be part of this group.

First of all, let me lay down some facts that seem to have been drowned in a torrent of misinformation. The most important one is that we are genuinely independent of OpenAI and any other of the so-called `frontier’ AI labs. Yes, the original impetus of forming this group was OpenAI’s response to the statement A Severe Misalignment of AI in Mathematics [https://mathandai.org/], but not all members of the group were contacted by OpenAI in the first place, there is nobody besides the nine of us taking part in our conversations, we do not receive any monetary compensation, and our technical support (so far mainly regarding IT, legal, and communications) is being provided by the IAS. We also have not signed any documents placing any constraints whatsoever on what we state in public and whose advice we seek, besides the obvious confidentiality requirements. The other fundamental fact is that our overarching aim is to represent as well as we possibly can the interests of the mathematical community. This is a tall order and we may end up doing a terrible job at it (I certainly hope not!), but I am absolutely certain that all nine of us take this extremely seriously and are acting in good faith.

I am acutely aware of many of the pitfalls of being part of such a group. At a most basic level, the mathematics community consists of people with a very broad range of views and opinions (often strongly held ones!), and the nine of us clearly don’t form a representative sample. As an independent body our role is purely advisory, so the AI labs can choose to simply ignore it. It would also be very naïve to believe that the AI labs won’t try to spin whatever we say in a way that suits their PR machine, which dwarfs anything we could possibly come up with. Finally, given that the unprecedented situation we find ourselves in has the potential of impacting the lives of so many people (I intentionally do not use the word `career’ since for many of us mathematics is so much more than just a career), whatever statement we make and / or advice we give will necessarily upset a sizable fraction of the maths community. So why on earth would I accept to be part of this?

Here are some of the reasons that swayed my mind:

 The recent releases, on privately controlled websites, of high-profile mathematical results combined with abject levels of scholarship and only minimal presentation effort has been undermining the usual standards of scientific practice. If we refuse to answer when being asked how to do better (irrespective of whether that ask is being done in good faith), how could we then complain about this behaviour?

 It is obvious that AI has already had a profound impact on mathematical research and raises numerous questions of correct attribution of ideas, priority, human understanding of ideas, etc. Many aspects of this revolution will be addressed within our community, but it makes no sense of taking a hard `ostrich’ approach of simply ignoring the AI labs and refusing to talk to them on principle.

 It is perfectly legitimate to question OpenAI’s motivations, but the fact is that they have now made an effort (as belated and minimal as it is) to engage with the mathematical community. Turning them down without even trying to engage with them would be the easiest way for them to get a PR win painting the mathematical community as a bunch of out of touch luddites.

 In the `Severe Misalignment’ statement, which has now been endorsed by nearly 8000 mathematicians, we conclude by saying that “These issues must be addressed urgently, in the mathematical community, by the companies developing these technologies, […]”. It would be rather hypocritical of myself to then chicken out at the first opportunity of actually having a chance of setting up some form of communications channel between the mathematical community and the AI labs just because I could get some flack for it. 

Regarding the actual advice we intend to provide, it is a fact that AI companies have in recent months been producing some high profile mathematical results and that their publication and dissemination has been falling far short of acceptable mathematical practice, whichever way that is defined. However, the situation at hand is sufficiently unprecedented that we genuinely haven’t completely made up our minds yet on the best way forward and we fully intend to gather as much feedback as possible, be it through our feedback form [https://agmai.org/input/], discussions with colleagues, the discussions on this blog, etc. We are going through a period of intense turmoil (and this doesn’t just concern mathematics) but I am heartened by the many intense but thoughtful and respectful discussions I have experienced both inside my own institution and at gatherings of mathematicians like the ICM over the past few months. I am convinced that the mathematical community can emerge all the more united and stronger from this experience.

Anonymous

I think we as a mathematics community need to take up this very seriously, and to play our cards very carefully. We do have some significant leverage here, and companies like OpenAI do not hold all the cards in their hands, but need us. While we should be aware of their ways, to put it mildly, we can also enforce good faith on this whole procedure, and cut through whatever their bottom line is to find a protocol that would be fair and reasonable, and which, if they refuse, will reflect on them appropriately. 

Now let us ask what do these companies want from mathematical community, and let us see what is legitimate and what is dishonest about it, with clarity and approaching things in an honest way, with the kind of honesty mathematics teaches us all.

1. Obviously, they want PR about capabilities of their models. That is one of the main reasons they are now putting huge resources into doing math – they are using it as a benchmark to show off.

2. They also want legitimacy. They want their results to be admitted as legitimate, to validate their models. We need to be aware that lean is not perfect. It does contain possible bugs, and only when proofs are accepted and digested by math community they become legitimate.

3. They want to develop their models. Recently, in an interview with Dwarkesh Patel, OpenAI researcher Noam Brown pointed out that while their models are powerful, their abilities are “jagged”, uneven, they lack judgement, they lack direction, ideas need to be supplemented etc. Their own internal researchers, according to him, spend 7-8000 dollars in compute on codex per day and their researchers still have to guide AI. Prompts and ideas supplied in chats by mathematics people are very valuable data for them too. We should always keep that in mind.

4. They say that we cannot and should not slow down their models. Now that is one of the dishonest takes they do. It is not about slowing them down, it is about transparency and assigning credit where credit is due. That goes both ways. Currently, and make no mistake about it, models are not uniformly better than human mathematicians – they are better at some aspects of doing math, mostly combining existing math across fields and groundwork computation. Whether – or if – that will change in near future is unclear. But we should insist on transparency because for a benchmark (which they want) to be meaningful, there needs to be honest reporting on both failures and successes, and an honest account of resources used.

5. To continue on the last point, they do not want full transparency. They want to hide their failures, and to spice up their successes. Sam Altman has said that ChatGPT 5 was already as capable as a maths university professor – which is laughable if anyone remembers interacting with this model a year ago. Our role – as math community – is to evaluate their capabilities in an honest way, so we cannot and must not grant them this lack of transparency.

Crucially, we need to realize a few points. They, as a company, do not have any interest in mathematical problems per se. The only reason they are now piling into mathematics is because they want PR, benchmark, and development of the side abilities mathematics provides. That is also their selling point. Mathematics, except perhaps cryptography and some particular applications, has no immediate use, and hence there is no direct safety issue associated with solving math problems. This also provides a unique advantage. 

There are other unique strengths of our community. Unlike some other branches of science, mathematics has a somewhat unified community, world congress every 4 years, and a clear valuation system that is our great asset. We, and only we, can judge value of the math results that llms provide. Also, mathematical inquiry is at the root of any theoretical scientific endeavor, and the abilities for mathematics translate to other fields, which is essential for AI. 

Most importantly, we can set an example for other fields that AI is aiming at. If we as a community step up, and ask for the benefits of AI in math to be shared in a fair manner, that can set a precedent for other areas. There is great responsibility in this point.

So what should we do, and how can we use our position to contain the unhealthy tendencies of these companies. We need to keep in mind that a lot of people in those companies are researchers themselves and might share some of the values we can appeal to. Also, in their public statements, even leaders of these companies sometimes make statements that make them seem benevolent, but which might not be held sincerely. We should hold them accountable to their own words! 

The committee can make a public statement, offer, not only to OpenAI, but also to Anthropic, and other companies like Meta, xAI, Google DeepMind, and also Chinese AI companies like DeepSeek, Kimi, Qwen, and European ones like Mistral. Now that might be of higher or lesser interest to each of them, but we should set a standard, and increase our leverage by company peer pressure. They need us, and they do have a lot to lose if they refuse our offer. OpenAI is wrong in thinking they call all the shots – they don’t, but neither do we. Therefore, our offer should be reasonable, and fair. We need to demonstrate our wisdom, for the sake of not only our profession, but, by setting precedent, for humanity as well. 

What can we offer? Here is one idea. We can demand that testing of models against problems, where huge amounts of resources are required (far exceeding the subscription that a single mathematician can pay), needs to go through our community. We pick – and suggest – problems to be tried. Either by simple prompts or by other means. The result – either fail or succeed, are published publicly and transparently as well as prompts. In return, we offer to curate and check the solutions, and provide the desired legitimacy.

There is a precedent to this. Terence Tao blog has hosted many collaborative polymath projects, where anyone could participate. Having such a precedent is a huge advantage. This might be a way to accept these works, and in terms of credit sharing it seems pretty fair. Other collaborative reviews have also existed, one of the most notable being quick and slick review of Deolalikar’s P vs NP claimed proof, where anonymous contributors played a crucial role. Also, we might offer to interactively test their models – with huge credit budgets – in a guided way in such projects, as a collective effort. In this way, there is a real way to incorporate testing and benchmarking without the danger of PR stunt capture. If that is what they want, we can offer that. We should hold them to their words, and cut through any attempt at manipulation with directness and honesty.

Another idea might be to organize our own, mathematics llm hubs, that protect IP inside chats. This is perhaps not directly associated with the OpenAI initiative, but there is a need for protective layers. Also, this way we can redistribute compute with discount through this layer, and, very importantly, have scrutability of what went into solving some problems i.e. how much credit to assign to mathematician and how much belongs to the machine. Because otherwise the credit system on which mathematical research relies is going to collapse. This is of course under assumption that AI won’t be able to replace human researchers completely – that, while they are better at some things, they are not able to do everything, including providing insight and ideas, as this has been proven to be the case for art and software engineering, and realistically we can expect it to hold for quite a while. But as far as OpenAI and other companies are concerned, it is essential that we guard our ideas and valuable interactions from being scrapped for free. 

Whatever we do, it is very important to get unified as a community with our core demands, and to make no apologies and no excuses to corporate bullying, while remaining fair to whatever is reasonable in their legitimate interests, and holding them accountable to what they say and not let ourselves be manipulated or bullied.

For those out of the companies that sign up to our offer, we will provide legitimacy, benchmarking and other benefits that they need. To those that keep hostile attitude our response should be equally clear. We should refuse to review or publish their claims for a set period of time (several years, for instance, which is in fact common for reviewing process in mathematics, but is an eternity in their ambitious plans to score quick and dishonest PR victories) or to give any legitimacy to their claims of solved problems, pointing out their unfair practices, unreliability of lean and their refusal to play fair and not be harmful to as noble a human endeavor as mathematics uniquely is.
