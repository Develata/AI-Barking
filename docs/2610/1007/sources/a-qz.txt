An AI app is now writing first-time acne prescriptions in Utah without a doctor
Nolla Health's Nolla Derm app received state approval to issue initial prescriptions autonomously, with physician oversight built in at each stage
By Cris Tolomia·2 min read·Updated October 6, 2026
Add QZ to Google
Anadolu / Getty Images
Nolla Health announced Monday that its Nolla Derm app has become the first AI system in the United States authorized to issue initial prescriptions, starting with acne medication in Utah. The company said Utah residents 18 and older can sign up for the program at $4.99 a month.
The app works by asking patients to complete a 10-to-15-minute intake questionnaire and a face scan. According to Bloomberg, the AI examines the patient's skin, produces a numerical acne severity score, and identifies an appropriate treatment from a set of eight pre-approved topical options. Prescriptions are delivered to a pharmacy or the patient's home. If the AI cannot select a treatment with confidence, the patient is referred to a licensed physician instead.
The program operates in three stages with declining levels of direct physician involvement. During the first stage, which covers the initial 100 patients enrolled, every prescription the AI produces must pass through physician review before being sent to a pharmacy. In the second stage, which encompasses the following 400 patients, the AI sends prescriptions straight to pharmacies while a physician looks them over after the fact on a weekly basis. Once the program moves past those initial 500 patients, physician oversight shifts to a monthly audit covering no fewer than one in ten prescriptions issued, the company said.
Nolla Health received approval through the Utah Office of Artificial Intelligence Policy, a division of the Utah Department of Commerce that authorizes companies to test AI products under relaxed regulations. Under the pilot, prescriptions carry a clinician's name without a human professional writing them, according to Bloomberg. The program is set to run for one year, with the option to renew for two more years at the state's discretion.
The company said licensed clinicians agree with the AI's treatment recommendations in more than 96% of real-world cases, with disagreements limited to minor adjustments such as topical medication strength. The system cannot generate open-ended prescriptions and is restricted to pre-approved topical treatments. Patients with severe acne, pregnancy, or other conditions outside the pilot's scope are referred to a physician.
According to Bloomberg, Zachary Boyd, director of Utah's Office of Artificial Intelligence Policy, said the state carved out a "narrow exception" for Nolla Health, setting aside the state statute that ordinarily restricts prescribing authority to licensed physicians. Boyd added that the office does not anticipate the same controversy that followed an earlier AI prescription pilot in the state because Nolla Health's program is limited to acne medication and is the first company to engage with the Utah Medical Licensing Board before receiving authorization.
The company said the Nolla Derm app has been downloaded by more than 175,000 people across 44 states. Chief Executive Luis Wenus said in a statement that the goal is to help patients receive care "in minutes instead of long waits or going without care altogether."
