   Refactored intracellular domains enhance
   CAR expression and function

   Abstract
   Chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) intracellular domains (ICDs) couple antigen recognition to T-cell acti-
   vation. Here we worked with AI agents to refactor CAR ICDs, proposing designs and refining them as
   measurements accumulated over three rounds. This approach yielded krgr–ζ1–40, a 45-amino-acid ICD
   less than a third the length of the clinical 4-1BB–CD3ζ (BBζ) ICD. Compared with BBζ, krgr–ζ1–40 increased
   CAR surface expression and antigen-driven IFN-γ release, without detectable antigen-independent IFN-γ
   release. krgr–ζ1–40 extended CAR expression and target-cell killing after transient mRNA delivery, sup-
   ported the expression of four CARs from a shared mRNA dose, and enhanced B-cell depletion after lentiviral
   and lipid-nanoparticle delivery. These results show that iterative agent-guided design with experimental
   feedback can yield compact ICDs that enhance CAR expression and sustain CAR T-cell function.




Introduction
Chimeric antigen receptors (CARs) redirect T cells to recognize cell-surface antigens independently of major
histocompatibility complexes (MHCs). The concept originated with chimeric T-cell receptors whose variable
regions were replaced by those of an antibody, which conferred antibody-type specificity on T cells [1]. In parallel,
Irving and Weiss showed that the cytoplasmic domain of the T-cell receptor (TCR) ζ chain is sufficient to couple
a chimeric receptor to TCR signal transduction pathways [2]. Combining these ideas, Eshhar and colleagues fused
a single-chain antibody fragment to the ζ or γ chain, producing single-chain chimeric receptors that triggered
cytokine secretion and target-cell lysis upon antigen binding [3]. Adding a costimulatory domain from CD28 [4,5]
or 4-1BB [6–8] to CD3ζ produced the second-generation CARs used in approved therapies, and the two differ in
signaling, metabolism and persistence [9,10].
Engineering these receptors requires balancing antigen-induced activity with behavior in the absence of antigen.
Antigen-independent (tonic) CAR signaling can promote exhaustion, and 4-1BB and CD28 costimulation have
different effects on exhaustion in tonic-signaling models [11]. Activation strength also depends on the number of
immunoreceptor tyrosine-based activation motifs (ITAMs) in CD3ζ. Mutating two of the three CD3ζ ITAMs lowers
the activation potential of a CD28-based CAR, which preserves memory differentiation and limits exhaustion [12].
Conversely, additional ITAMs increase T-cell proliferation [13,14] and the efficiency of receptor triggering, likely
by bringing multiple ZAP-70 molecules into proximity [15], and the larger number of ITAMs in the TCR–CD3
complex is thought to contribute to the greater antigen sensitivity of the TCR compared with CARs [15,16]. These
studies support the prevailing view that more ITAMs produce stronger activation, so that removing ITAMs trades
acute activity for persistence. They also suggest that the composition of the CAR intracellular domain (ICD) can
be tuned beyond the two clinical designs, CD28–CD3ζ and 4-1BB–CD3ζ.

Efforts to expand this signaling vocabulary have screened libraries of signaling domains for T-cell function, from
randomly assembled domains to pooled and combinatorial designs [17–24]. Pooled screens have tested dozens of
intracellular domains from diverse immune receptors in primary T cells [19–21], shuffled costimulatory domains with
single-cell readouts [22–24] and recombined short signaling motifs to decode their effects on CAR T-cell phenotype
[17]. Others have built CARs from CD3 subunits beyond CD3ζ [25,26], from the ITAM adaptor DAP12 [27] and
from proximal signaling molecules [28,29]. Together, these studies show that the signaling vocabulary of CARs
extends beyond CD28 and 4-1BB. However, they explored which domains to combine, exchange or reorder rather
than how much of the ICD could be removed. The closest test, truncating CD3ζ to a single membrane-proximal
ITAM in a CD28-based CAR, preserved acute effector function and improved tumor control, but was designed
to examine ITAM position and dosage and retained the full CD28 domain [12]. Because shortening the signaling
domain is otherwise expected to reduce function, how far the ICD as a whole can be compacted, and whether
compaction can improve function, remain unknown.
Refactoring in software reorganizes internal structure while preserving observable behavior [30]. In biology, refac-
toring has simplified systems shaped by natural selection, from the overlapping genetic elements of bacteriophage
T7 to G protein-coupled receptor signaling in yeast, so that they can be engineered rationally [31,32]. Applied to
CAR ICDs, refactoring would reorganize signaling motifs into compact domains that preserve the core function of
coupling antigen recognition to T-cell activation. This requires choosing which binding partners to recruit, how
to combine and place their motifs, and which sequences to remove. Unlike the folded proteins of structure-based
design [33–35], signaling domains such as the CD3ζ cytoplasmic domain are largely disordered and act through
short motifs that recruit partners inside cells [17,36,37]. The functional consequences of these design choices must
be established experimentally. AI agents that combine literature search, data analysis and hypothesis generation
to propose and revise experiments [38,39] offer a way to reason over the design space as measurements accumulate.
A compact ICD would also remove most of this disordered sequence, which two mechanistic models implicate in
receptor signaling, with opposite predictions for a compact ICD. In the first, signaling components assemble into
membrane condensates that promote signal transduction [40,41]. In T cells, these condensates have been attributed
mainly to multivalent crosslinking of LAT [40,42], as in other multivalent signaling systems [43]. However, TCR–
ZAP-70 and LAT occupy distinct domains within microclusters, with the TCR–ZAP-70 domain assembling first
[44], and LAT condenses only after a delay following TCR engagement [44,45], so LAT crosslinking does not explain
how the receptor itself clusters. In the broader condensate field, phase separation is often driven by intrinsically
disordered regions (IDRs) themselves, in which interacting stickers, such as aromatic and charged residues, are
separated by solvating spacers [46–48], and in CARs, an engineered CD3ε motif that condenses through cation–π
interactions improved antigen sensitivity and persistence [49]. If the receptor’s disordered tails drive condensation
and condensation drives activation, removing disordered sequence should impair activation. In the second model,
basic-rich stretches (BRS) of the CD3ε and CD3ζ tails bind acidic phospholipids of the inner leaflet [50–57],
inserting ITAM tyrosines into the bilayer and restraining their phosphorylation until antigen engagement releases
them [50,51,55], although disrupting the CD3ζ BRS has also been found to weaken TCR signaling or synapse
accumulation [52,54]. Removing the BRS should instead release tonic signaling. A compact ICD therefore risks
either loss of activation or loss of antigen dependence, and changing disorder, basic residues or motif spacing can
test these competing expectations.
Compactness may also change how many receptors reach the cell surface. CAR function depends on both receptor
and antigen density [58–61], so a compact ICD that raises surface expression could improve function even without
greater signaling per receptor. Transient mRNA delivery, which can generate functional CAR T cells [62,63],
provides a setting in which this relationship can be followed as receptors are lost from the cell surface. Expression
from limited mRNA also matters for in vivo CAR T-cell generation, in which targeted lipid nanoparticles carrying
CAR mRNA [64–66] or targeted lentiviral vectors [67] modify T cells inside the body. With lipid nanoparticles,
an ICD that yields more receptor from less mRNA could lower the dose needed for function.
Here we addressed these questions by refactoring CAR ICDs through successive rounds of design, in which scientists
and AI agents proposed changes to motif composition and sequence context and the resulting measurements
guided the next designs. We asked how far the ICD could be compacted and whether removing its disordered
sequence would cost activation or antigen dependence, as the condensate and lipid-binding models predict. We
also asked whether a compact ICD that yields more surface receptor would improve function after mRNA and
lipid-nanoparticle delivery, and whether experimental feedback could steer design ideas across rounds. Successive
rounds produced shorter ICDs with higher expression and function, without the losses that either model predicts.
Earlier-round measurements also shifted large language model (LLM) predictions toward the design ideas that
worked, although measurement still revealed gains the models missed. These results establish iterative design by
scientists and AI agents as a route to compact CAR ICDs with higher expression and more durable function.

Results
Agent-guided design and screening identify brs–ζ1–40, a compact CAR ICD
CAR intracellular domains are typically assembled from intact cytoplasmic domains of CD3ζ and a costimulatory
receptor such as 4-1BB or CD28, yet T cells use a much larger vocabulary of signaling modules [17,19,20,22,25,68–
71]. To make this vocabulary explicit and to frame the design of compact ICDs, we compiled published evidence
into a signaling map (Fig. 1a). The map links 34 ICD modules, drawn from CD3 chains and other ITAM receptors,
co-receptors, the CD28 family, LAT, the TNF-receptor family and cytokine receptors, through binding partners
and relays to signaling pathways and reported T-cell phenotypes. It draws on 150 interactions from 253 primary
studies, each graded by the strength of its evidence [68,72–75]. Our iterative exploration across rounds 1–3 covered
most modules on the map (Fig. 1a).
The map summarizes established rules of CAR signaling. CD3ζ ITAM number and identity calibrate activation
strength, ligand sensitivity and T-cell fate [12,15,76]. 4-1BB signals through TRAF1–3 and noncanonical NF-κB
[77,78] and recruits THEMIS–SHP-1, which attenuates Lck-dependent priming [79]. CD28 and 4-1BB differ in the
kinetics, magnitude and metabolic consequences of signaling [9,10], and point mutations in CD28 motifs change
persistence and function [80,81]. Membrane-proximal basic residues bind acidic inner-leaflet lipids [51,52]; CD3ε
motifs recruit Lck, PI3K and Csk [82–84]; and a JAK–STAT module can add a cytokine signal [85]. ICD size,
rather than costimulatory identity, determines constitutive CD3ζ phosphorylation [86], and pooled and combi-
natorial libraries show that non-canonical domains and module combinations can change CAR T-cell phenotype
[17,19,20,22]. Grading the evidence behind each rule family by experimental system showed that most support
comes from T cells in vitro and from mouse models, whereas clinical evidence is limited to comparisons of CD28
and 4-1BB costimulation.
We built an agent system that combines LLM reasoning with literature search and computational tools. Scientists
specified design objectives and reference sequences, and tool-using agents searched the literature and proposed
sequence changes. Scientists then reviewed the proposed designs, steered the agents and compared candidate
sequences. After several cycles of review we selected 186 candidates for the round 1 library screen. Representative
designs ranged from 36 to 109 amino acids (aa), compared with 154 for the second-generation 4-1BB–CD3ζ reference
ICD (BBζ; Fig. 1b).
As an initial exploration, we screened the 186 designs as CARs with the D-domain BCMA binder (ddBCMA) [87]
in Jurkat NFAT reporter cells against BCMA-positive and -negative target cells. Of the 186 designs, 38 matched
or exceeded the BCMA-driven signal of BBζ, and 32 of these also gave lower signal with BCMA-negative targets
(Fig. 1c). Grouping the designs by native ITAM count and length, we found that 25 of the 130 designs retaining
only one ITAM supported antigen-driven signaling at or above the level of BBζ (Fig. 1d).
brs–ζ1–40 is a 54-aa ICD joining the membrane-proximal basic clusters and first ITAM of CD3ζ to a CD40 segment
required for TRAF6 binding in native CD40 [88,89]. CD40-derived signaling domains have previously enhanced
CAR T-cell proliferation, survival and persistence [23,90,91]. However, a 19-residue CD40 motif spanning the same
TRAF6-binding site was among the most inhibitory motifs for CAR T-cell proliferation in a pooled screen of CARs
with full-length CD3ζ [17], so whether this segment can act as a costimulatory domain within this scaffold was
unclear. Unexpectedly, brs–ζ1–40 matched the NFAT signal of BBζ with BCMA-positive targets, whereas with
BCMA-negative targets its signal was about one-sixteenth that of BBζ (Fig. 1c).
To test whether the designs transferred to another binder and to primary T cells, we re-screened 78 designs, selected
by ICD length and NFAT activity, as bb2121 BCMA scFv CARs [92] in primary T cells (Fig. 1e). Strikingly, nearly
all compact designs increased surface CAR: 73 of the 78 exceeded BBζ (median, 2.7-fold), and 30 also released more
antigen-driven IFN-γ. In this screen, brs–ζ1–40 displayed 4.1-fold the surface CAR of BBζ and the second-highest
IFN-γ of the 78 designs. Without target cells, its IFN-γ remained below the detection limit. Jurkat NFAT and
primary T-cell IFN-γ ranked the re-screened designs similarly (Spearman ρ = 0.69; Fig. 1f), even though the two
screens differed in binder, target cells and measured functional response. Together, these results identify brs–ζ1–40
as a compact ICD that is highly expressed and antigen responsive in primary T cells.

brs–ζ1–40 increases CAR expression across BCMA binders and retains
antigen-dependent function

(E:T) ratios (Fig. 2a–c). All ten compact ICDs displayed more surface CAR than BBζ, from 2.3- to 17-fold (Fig.
2a).

To determine whether these properties depend on the binder, we tested the same eleven ICDs with the FMC63
anti-CD19 scFv [93] against NALM6 leukemia cells (Fig. 2d–f). Seven of the ten compact designs again displayed
more surface CAR than BBζ, by up to 3.5-fold (Fig. 2d). In contrast, brs–ζ1–40 displayed slightly less CAR
than BBζ with FMC63, whereas krgr–ζ1–4-1bb displayed 2.5-fold more. brs–ζ1–40 released less NALM6-induced
IFN-γ than BBζ, whereas krgr–ζ1–4-1bb released the most in the panel, 3.4-fold BBζ (Figs. 2e and S1f). brs–ζ1–
40 and BBζ killed NALM6 cells with similar potency (Fig. 2f), and krgr–ζ1–4-1bb was among the most potent.
Thus, the expression and cytokine advantage of brs–ζ1–40 seen with bb2121 did not extend to FMC63, whereas
krgr–ζ1–4-1bb was highly expressed and active with both binders.

Because tonic signaling can promote exhaustion [11] and may rise with receptor density [59,94], we next measured
IFN-γ released by the BCMA CAR T cells in the absence of target cells (Fig. 2g). Across effector inputs, every
construct except brs–ζ1–4-1bb–ζ3 remained below the 9.8 pg/mL detection limit, including brs–ζ1–40 despite its
13-fold higher receptor density. These results indicate that the high surface density of brs–ζ1–40 does not lead to
detectable tonic IFN-γ release.
To determine which segments of brs–ζ1–40 drive its signaling, we compared signaling mutants of brs–ζ1–40 and BBζ
in Jurkat NFAT and NF-κB reporter cells stimulated with BCMA-positive target cells (Fig. 2h). Costimulation-null
variants carried alanine substitutions in the CD40-derived QEPQE motif of brs–ζ1–40 or the TRAF-binding motif
of 4-1BB, and ITAM-null variants carried Y→F substitutions in every ITAM. As expected, the costimulation-null
variant of BBζ kept its NFAT signal but lost more than 80% of its NF-κB signal, whereas the ITAM-null variant
lost about 95% of its NFAT signal. brs–ζ1–40 followed a similar pattern: its costimulation-null variant lost more
than 80% of NF-κB signal but, unlike that of BBζ, also about 40% of NFAT signal, and its ITAM-null variant
lost about 95% of NFAT signal. In this experiment, unmodified brs–ζ1–40 gave 3.5-fold higher NFAT and similar
NF-κB signal compared with BBζ, more than the matched NFAT signal of the library screen (Fig. 1c). These
results show that, as in BBζ, NFAT signaling by brs–ζ1–40 depends on its ITAM tyrosines and NF-κB signaling
on its CD40-derived QEPQE motif.
We then asked whether the expression advantage of brs–ζ1–40 extends to other BCMA binder formats represented
among clinical CAR T cells (Fig. 2i). With the biparatopic VHH binder of ciltacabtagene autoleucel, brs–ζ1–40
displayed 2.8-fold more CAR per cell than BBζ at similar CAR-positive fractions. With ddBCMA, it displayed
4.5-fold more, compared with 13-fold with the bb2121 scFv (Fig. 2a). Together, these results show that brs–ζ1–
40 increases surface CAR expression across scFv, VHH and miniprotein BCMA binders while retaining antigen-
dependent killing and signaling that depends on its ITAM and its CD40-derived QEPQE motif.
We also asked whether such designs could have been anticipated from sequence alone. Given each library sequence
and a description of the assay, five general-purpose LLMs predicted NFAT activity poorly. Their predicted prob-
ability that a design would exceed BBζ in NFAT stayed below 0.75 and hardly separated the 38 designs that did
from the rest (mean 0.40 versus 0.34; Fig. 2j).

Experiment-informed agent redesign yields krgr–ζ1–40, a 45-aa ICD with higher
expression and IFN-γ
To determine which features of the round 1 hits could be combined in a shorter ICD, we worked with Claude
Code to design 15 round 2 ICDs from four hits characterized in primary T cells (Fig. 3a). The designs tested
six groups of changes: ablations, costimulatory motif swaps, ITAM swaps, two TRAF-binding motifs, KRGR
anchor transfers and minimal domains. Ablations tested whether individual segments were required, while motif
swaps and anchor transfers tested whether features of different hits could be combined; krgr–ζ1–40 was an anchor
transfer. brs–ζ1–40 had released the most IFN-γ of the four hits, and earlier cross-binder cytokine measurements
motivated transferring the KRGR anchor from krgr–ζ1–4-1bb (Fig. 3a). The Jurkat screen had also shown that
KRGR was required for the activity of krgr–ζ1–4-1bb (Fig. S1b). These observations led us to replace the first 13
residues of the basic-rich segment of brs–ζ1–40 with the four-residue KRGR anchor while retaining its ITAM and
CD40-derived segment (Fig. 3c). The resulting krgr–ζ1–40 was 45 aa long, shorter than either parent (54 and 56
aa) and less than a third the length of BBζ.
To determine whether the KRGR anchor preserved the properties of brs–ζ1–40, we tested the 15 round 2 designs
alongside brs–ζ1–40 and BBζ in a primary T-cell screen (Fig. 3b). Twelve of the 15 round 2 designs displayed
more surface CAR than BBζ, and six released more antigen-driven IFN-γ. krgr–ζ1–40 improved on the surface
expression of brs–ζ1–40 by 1.5-fold and BBζ by 6.2-fold (Fig. 3b, left). Its IFN-γ was the highest of the 15 round
2 designs (Fig. 3b, middle). Without target cells, all round 2 designs and brs–ζ1–40 remained below the detection
limit (Fig. 3b, right). Thus, replacing the 13-residue basic-rich segment of brs–ζ1–40 with the four-residue KRGR
anchor increased both expression and antigen-driven IFN-γ without increasing tonic IFN-γ.
To compare killing, we titrated krgr–ζ1–40, brs–ζ1–40 and BBζ CAR T cells against H929 cells across a range of
E:T ratios (Fig. 3d). Killing curves for krgr–ζ1–40 and brs–ζ1–40 were left-shifted relative to that of BBζ, at 2.0-
and 1.6-fold lower IC50 E:T, respectively, so fewer CAR T cells were needed per target cell for the same killing.

To determine whether krgr–ζ1–40 signals through the same segments as brs–ζ1–40, we tested the signaling mutants
of Fig. 2h (Fig. 3e). Unmodified krgr–ζ1–40 gave 4.7-fold higher NFAT and 8.6-fold higher NF-κB signal than
BBζ, in proportion to its higher surface CAR (Fig. S2). In contrast, brs–ζ1–40 gave about a quarter of the
NF-κB signal per surface CAR of BBζ, which may reflect suboptimal costimulatory signaling (Fig. S2). As with
brs–ζ1–40, NFAT signaling depended on the ITAM tyrosines, with about 95% lost in the ITAM-null variant, and
NF-κB signaling on the CD40-derived motif, with more than 95% lost in the costimulation-null variant, compared
with about 85% for BBζ (Fig. 3e). Together, these results show that the KRGR anchor preserves the ITAM-
and CD40-dependent signaling of brs–ζ1–40 while further increasing expression and antigen-driven IFN-γ. We
therefore carried krgr–ζ1–40 forward as the lead compact ICD.

krgr–ζ1–40 extends CAR expression and function across mRNA, lentiviral and
lipid-nanoparticle delivery
We next measured how krgr–ζ1–40 expression scales with the amount of mRNA delivered and how long expression
and function last after transient delivery (Fig. 4a–e). Surface CAR density increased with the mRNA dose for
all three ICDs, but krgr–ζ1–40 gave the highest density at every dose, 8.4-fold that of BBζ and 2.4-fold that of
brs–ζ1–40 at the highest dose (Figs. 4a and S3). krgr–ζ1–40 matched the density that BBζ reached at the highest
dose with one-eighth of the mRNA.
To follow expression over time, we transfected primary T cells with krgr–ζ1–40 or BBζ CAR mRNA and measured
surface CAR daily for 17 days (Fig. 4b,c). On day 1, krgr–ζ1–40 T cells displayed 12-fold more CAR than BBζ T
cells, and, as expected after transient delivery, expression of both CARs then declined at a similar rate. Because it
started higher, krgr–ζ1–40 remained above 2,000 CAR/cell until day 13, compared with day 4 for BBζ, and above
the 1,000 CAR/cell LLOD through day 17 (Fig. 4b). Thus, after transient delivery, the higher starting expression
of krgr–ζ1–40 kept surface CAR detectable for longer than with BBζ, without slowing its decline.
We next asked whether the longer expression of krgr–ζ1–40 extends its function. Every second day, we co-cultured
the CAR T cells with fresh H929 cells and measured IFN-γ and target viability (Fig. 4d,e). krgr–ζ1–40 T cells
released 3.1-fold more IFN-γ than BBζ T cells on day 1, and IFN-γ declined over time for both CARs (Figs. 4d
and S4a). However, krgr–ζ1–40 T cells still released IFN-γ on day 13, whereas IFN-γ from BBζ T cells fell below
the detection limit from day 7. Similarly, at the highest E:T, krgr–ζ1–40 T cells killed more than 90% of H929
cells through day 13, whereas BBζ T cells had lost most of their killing by day 9 (Fig. 4e). From day 1 to day 7,
the killing IC50 rose 11-fold for BBζ T cells but only 2.5-fold for krgr–ζ1–40 T cells, which still killed half of the
H929 cells through day 13 (Fig. S4b). Together, these results show that krgr–ζ1–40 extends both CAR expression
and function after transient mRNA delivery.
Because krgr–ζ1–40 yielded more receptor per unit of mRNA (Fig. 4a), we next asked whether it supports the
co-expression of several CARs from a shared mRNA dose (Fig. 4f,g). We electroporated T cells with a cocktail of
four CAR mRNAs carrying BCMA, CD19, HER2 or MUC16 binders on either krgr–ζ1–40 or BBζ, each at half the
single-CAR dose, and detected each CAR with its own antigen in a separate well. With krgr–ζ1–40, more than
80% of cells were positive for each CAR, compared with at most a third with BBζ (Fig. 4f), and CAR density was
2.6- to 12.9-fold higher (Fig. 4g). Each CAR delivered alone at the full dose showed a similar advantage, with 4.5-
to 10.1-fold higher density (Fig. S5). These results show that krgr–ζ1–40 supports the co-expression of four CARs
from a single electroporation at half the per-CAR mRNA dose.
To determine whether these advantages extend to persistent function, we transduced T cells with lentiviral vectors
encoding FMC63 anti-CD19 CARs (Fig. 4h,i). krgr–ζ1–40 T cells displayed 6.8-fold more CAR than BBζ T
cells (n = 2 donors; Fig. 4h). We then co-cultured the T cells with Raji lymphoma multilayer 3D aggregates at
low E:T ratios and measured IFN-γ on days 1, 3, 5 and 7 (Fig. 4i). IFN-γ from both CARs peaked on day 3
and then declined steadily through day 7. krgr–ζ1–40 T cells released more IFN-γ than BBζ T cells in 30 of 32
matched E:T and day conditions. By day 7, when both curves had reached the same plateau, krgr–ζ1–40 T cells
reached half-maximal IFN-γ at a 2.9-fold lower E:T than BBζ T cells. Because each T cell must engage more tumor
cells in succession at a lower E:T, this shift suggests that krgr–ζ1–40 T cells stayed active through 2.9-fold more
consecutive encounters with antigen-positive tumor cells.
We next asked whether lentivirally transduced krgr–ζ1–40 T cells also kill target cells more efficiently. In a separate
experiment, krgr–ζ1–40 T cells killed primary human B cells at 2.4-fold lower IC50 E:T than BBζ T cells (Fig. 4j),
and NALM6 leukemia cells at 1.3-fold lower IC50 E:T (Fig. S4c). Thus, krgr–ζ1–40 T cells killed both normal
and leukemic CD19-positive cells at lower E:T.
Finally, we asked whether krgr–ζ1–40 enhances CAR T-cell function when the CAR is delivered directly to unse-
lected blood cells [66]. We added CD3-targeted lipid nanoparticles (tLNPs) carrying CAR mRNA to whole PBMC
cultures from five donors, so that CAR T cells were generated in situ alongside autologous B cells (Fig. 4k,l). One
day later, krgr–ζ1–40 gave more CAR-positive T cells than BBζ at every dose from 12 ng mRNA per 106 cells
upward (Fig. 4k). B-cell depletion increased with dose for both CARs, but krgr–ζ1–40 reached 50% depletion at
a 4.6-fold lower dose than BBζ (Fig. 4l). Together, these results show that krgr–ζ1–40 increases CAR expression
and enhances CAR T-cell function after mRNA, lentiviral and lipid-nanoparticle delivery.

Iterative experimental feedback guides CAR ICD refactoring
Having established krgr–ζ1–40 as the lead compact ICD, we asked whether it could be refined further. In round 3,
we worked with AI agents to design 164 variants of krgr–ζ1–40 and screened them in primary T cells (Fig. S6a,b).
The variants tested three groups of changes: 113 rational edits, 30 single and combined point mutations, and 21
synonymous codon recodes. Rational edits changed the juxtamembrane (JM) segment, the costimulatory motif,
spacers, charge and length; point mutations tested local sequence changes (Fig. 5a); and codon recodes tested
whether expression could be raised through the mRNA sequence alone (Fig. S6e). Surface CAR varied widely
among the 21 recodes, which encode the same protein, so codon choice alone changed CAR expression. Of the 122
ICD variants that passed the expression threshold, 17 released at least as much IFN-γ as krgr–ζ1–40 on the same
plate (Fig. S6b).
Compact ICDs are difficult to design from structure or from training data. Signaling ICDs such as the CD3ζ
cytoplasmic domain are intrinsically disordered [36], so apart from short motifs bound to their partners, such as
a phosphorylated ITAM on the ZAP-70 tandem SH2 domains (PDB 2OQ1) [95] and a CD40 peptide on TRAF6
(PDB 1LB6) [89], they offer little structure to model. Their function is measured mainly in cell-based assays,
and published CAR libraries offer little training data for compact ICDs [17–24]. Machine learning offers several
routes to interpretable sequence design, including libraries of CAR signaling motifs [17], sparse features of protein
language models that map to biological concepts [96,97], and grammars that constrain sequence generation [98].
However, these approaches rely on training data or established rules that remain scarce for ICDs. Design therefore
relies on reasoning about experimental data, which can be biased toward the designer’s prior beliefs when many
choices are defensible [99–103].
To design in this space, we worked with many AI agents in parallel [38,39], which searched the literature and
analyzed our assay data. In each round, we and the agents reviewed the preceding measurements together, proposed
ideas and developed designs with matched controls. Within each idea, designs changed one feature at a time against
matched controls, such as JM charge at fixed length or stepwise trims of ICD length (Fig. S7). The agents wrote
and ran the analysis code, and each proposal came with a written rationale [103]. Each design was assigned to an
idea when it was proposed, and we traced these ideas across rounds 1–3 (Fig. 5a). Round 1 comprised 186 designs
in 14 ideas, and krgr–ζ1–40 became the parent of the 164 round 3 variants.
Current models did not reliably identify which compact ICDs would fail (Fig. S8). Neither Boltz-2 [104] co-fold
confidence nor protein language-model likelihoods (ESM-2 [105] and ProtGPT2 [106]) reliably separated active
from inactive ICDs. We also asked five general-purpose LLMs, three queried in a single call and two run as coding
agents, given the ICD sequences and a description of the assays, for two probabilities per design: that it was active,
P(active), and that it would exceed the round’s comparator, P(better). Each model answered zero-shot and again
with the earlier rounds’ results in the prompt (in context). The LLMs called most designs active, including most
inactive round 3 variants: the specificity of their calls was 0.15–0.34 zero-shot and 0.18–0.51 in context (Fig. S8a),
consistent with the poor calibration of LLM predictions of experimental outcomes [107].
As a retrospective proxy for how much signal the earlier rounds’ data carried about each idea, we measured how far
the models’ predictions moved when those data were added to the prompt, a belief change analogous to Bayesian
surprise [108,109] (Fig. 5b). Each model was run three times per condition. We removed each model’s overall
tendency to call designs active or better, so that ideas were compared by relative belief. Given round 1, the
models moved krgr–ζ1–40 from sixth to third among the 15 round 2 designs for P(active) and from fifth to second
for P(better); in the primary T-cell screen, which tested the 15 round 2 designs alongside 78 round 1 designs, it
released the most IFN-γ of the 93 ICDs (Fig. 3b). Across ideas, the in-context shift followed the outcome. It
correlated with the gap between the observed fraction and the zero-shot prediction (Spearman ρ, 0.52 for P(active)
and 0.35 for P(better)), and of the ideas whose prediction moved by 0.03 or more, 12 of 16 moved toward the
outcome for P(active) and 11 of 14 for P(better). For 3 ideas in each task, however, the outcome lay outside the
in-context range: the models overrated native-brs-jm, foreign-itam and traf6-super for P(active), overrated
plm-ranked-mutant and recruit-pick for P(better) and underrated jm-lysine-charge.

To ask what the LLMs learned in context, we compared both predictions with one readout, IFN-γ relative to each
round’s comparator, for the 125 round 2 and 3 designs scored in both tasks (Fig. 5c). The earlier rounds’ data
raised the correlation of P(better) with the readout for single designs (Spearman ρ, from 0.33 to 0.51) and for the
21 ideas (from 0.40 to 0.63). For P(active), they raised it for ideas (from 0.37 to 0.54) but not for single designs
(from 0.40 to 0.37), and in context P(better) tracked single designs more closely than P(active) did (difference in
ρ, +0.14; 95% interval +0.01 to +0.28). The size of each design’s update also followed the outcome for P(better)
(Spearman ρ, 0.46 across 125 designs; 0.66 in round 2 and 0.44 in round 3): the models raised P(better) for 17 of
the 21 designs that exceeded the comparator and for 43 of the 104 that did not. For P(active), the update hardly
followed the outcome (ρ, 0.16; Fig. S9b). In both tasks, most of this signal lay between ideas; within ideas, ρ was
0.24 for P(better) and 0.06 for P(active). Thus, the earlier rounds’ data improved the models’ predictions for ideas
in both tasks, and for single designs only for P(better).

the KRGR anchor of krgr–ζ1–4-1bb and the ITAM1 and CD40 core of brs–ζ1–40 (Fig. 3a). Given round 1, the

We next examined designs for which the earlier rounds gave a clear signal. Round 1 showed that ICDs with Y1 at
8 or less were inactive and that moving Y1 from 5 to 9 with KRGR restored signaling (Fig. S9a). Given the assay
descriptions, the models already rated a round 2 design with Y1 at 5 unlikely to be active (mean P(active), 0.16
zero-shot), lowered it further given round 1 (0.10), and it was inactive (Fig. S9b). Round 3 moved this boundary
closer to the membrane: a basic JM spacer kept a design active at Y1 = 6, where a neutral spacer did not (Fig.
S9a). Basic JM residues contribute to membrane association and signaling [51,110]; in our compact ICDs, JM
sequence also influenced how close to the membrane an ITAM could remain functional. Natural ITAM receptors
place Y1 at 13–33 residues [111], so with a basic JM segment, compact ICDs can place Y1 closer to the membrane
than natural receptors do. Among designs of the same idea, the models’ in-context predictions tracked activity
when the designs varied JM length and charge (Spearman ρ, 0.75) and in part when they varied linker length
(0.38), but not among the 30 point mutants or the 21 motif swaps (0.01 and −0.19).
The models could also converge on wrong hypotheses. They rated all five designs that restored the native basic-rich
JM segment of CD3ζ at about 0.9, yet two were inactive. Although native CD3ζ BRS motifs support membrane
association and TCR signaling [52,54], krgr–ζ1–40 retained antigen-dependent function after most of these segments
were removed (Fig. 3b,d,e). Every in-context run called the same 21 of the 43 inactive round 3 designs active,
consistent with the correlated errors that LLMs share [112]. Designs that replaced the second YxxL motif of
ITAM1 with the CD28 YMNM motif, proposed in several rounds, expressed but did not signal, as ZAP-70 binding
requires both YxxL motifs [95,113]. Conversely, the CD40 core, which binds TRAF6 in native CD40 through a
motif distinct from those of 4-1BB [88,89], raised IFN-γ in each round: the lead design with the CD40 core released
more IFN-γ than the same ICD with the 4-1BB segment in its place (up to 2.4-fold; Fig. S9c), a gain the models
did not anticipate (the CD40 design was never predicted more than 0.07 above its counterpart). Asked whether
round 3 designs would exceed krgr–ζ1–40, the models also overrated the point mutants and motif swaps, of which
4 of 27 and 1 of 17 did, and underrated the JM lysine-charge designs, of which 5 of 9 did (Fig. 5b). These results
show that the models followed directions that the earlier data made clear but still missed the gain from the CD40
core and overestimated the promise of several design ideas.


Discussion
Here we sought to determine how compact a functional CAR ICD can be. We found that most of a clinical
second-generation ICD could be removed: krgr–ζ1–40 retains a single ITAM and a CD40-derived segment in 45
aa. Our results establish three properties of compact ICDs. First, they increase surface CAR expression, across
designs, binders and delivery methods. Second, they retain antigen-dependent signaling through defined segments,
NFAT through the single ITAM and NF-κB through the CD40-derived motif, with antigen-independent IFN-γ
below detection despite higher receptor density. Third, higher expression translated into more durable and broader
function after mRNA, lentiviral and lipid-nanoparticle delivery.
Why do compact ICDs express so highly? Because the effect was shared across designs, it may reflect a property
of short ICDs more than of a particular sequence. One possibility is that shorter receptors are synthesized, folded
or delivered to the cell surface more efficiently. Alternatively, they may be removed from the surface more slowly,
because constitutive CD3ζ phosphorylation depends on ICD size [86] and a shorter ICD carries fewer lysines,
whose ubiquitination drives CAR downmodulation after antigen engagement [114]. The effect also depended on
the binder, as brs–ζ1–40 did not increase the expression of the FMC63 CAR whereas krgr–ζ1–4-1bb did (Fig. 2d).
Measuring CAR mRNA, protein synthesis and surface half-life across ICDs and binders would distinguish faster
production from slower removal.
Do compact ICDs signal more per receptor, or do they place more receptors on the cell surface? Higher expression
contributes: brs–ζ1–40 lost its IFN-γ advantage with FMC63, where it also lost its expression advantage (Fig.
2d,e), and the NFAT and NF-κB signals of krgr–ζ1–40 rose in proportion to its surface CAR (Figs. 3e and S2).
Signaling per receptor also bears on antigen sensitivity. Because additional ITAMs raise the efficiency with which
ligand binding is converted into a signal [15], and CARs are already far less sensitive to antigen than TCRs [16,115],
a single-ITAM ICD might need more engaged receptors for the same response. However, when we titrated antigen
density [60] at a matched mRNA dose, krgr–ζ1–40 T cells needed about 1.7-fold more BCMA per target cell
than BBζ T cells for half-maximal antigen-specific IFN-γ, and released more antigen-specific IFN-γ from a lower
antigen-independent floor (Fig. S10). Because krgr–ζ1–40 displayed 8.4-fold more CAR at this dose (Fig. 4a),
its higher surface density likely offsets a lower antigen sensitivity per receptor. Measuring ITAM and ZAP-70
phosphorylation in T cells matched for surface CAR would test this directly.
Our results also bear on the two models that implicate the BRS of the disordered CD3ζ tail in receptor signaling.
krgr–ζ1–40 removed most BRS regions from BBζ yet retained antigen-dependent activation (Fig. 3b,d,e). Its
antigen-independent IFN-γ also remained below detection at 6.2-fold the surface CAR of BBζ (Fig. 3b). Losing


                                                         12
most of the BRS therefore impaired neither activation nor antigen dependence, and neither model’s prediction
held. For condensation, LAT crosslinking [40,42,116] does not address the result, because LAT condenses after
and apart from the TCR–ZAP-70 domain [44,45], and CARs can form microclusters without LAT [117]. Instead,
the result runs counter to extending the stickers-and-spacers model of IDR phase separation [46–48] to the ICD,
in which losing charged stickers such as the BRS should impair activation. One possibility is that antigen binding
and the multivalent partners of the ITAM and CD40 motif, ZAP-70 [95] and TRAF6 [89], cluster the receptor
without its disordered tail. For membrane sequestration, in which the BRS hold ITAM tyrosines at the membrane
until antigen engagement [50–56], the KRGR anchor and the basic residues after the ITAM may suffice to hold a
single ITAM there (Fig. S11). Alternatively, antigen-independent CAR signaling may arise mainly from the 4-1BB
domain [118] and additional ITAMs that compact ICDs lack, or from charged patches on the binder [119].
Although the native BRS was dispensable, a short basic segment near the membrane was required. With a basic
JM segment, a single ITAM remained functional closer to the membrane than in natural ITAM receptors or BBζ
(Figs. S9a and S12), and JM segments without lysine or arginine lowered surface CAR. Natural spacing may
instead serve regulation, such as holding ITAMs at the membrane until antigen engagement [120]. KRGR, the
first four residues of 4-1BB, supplies such a segment. A conserved basic juxtamembrane motif anchors the OX40
tail at the membrane by binding anionic lipids, with similar evidence for 4-1BB and CD40 [110] and for CD28
[121], but to our knowledge KRGR had not been used before as a standalone anchor. Because we measured neither
condensates nor basal ITAM phosphorylation, these results argue against a requirement for the BRS rather than
against condensation or membrane sequestration. Imaging CAR and LAT clusters separately and measuring ITAM
phosphorylation across ICDs that differ in BRS content and juxtamembrane spacing, at matched surface density,
would distinguish these possibilities.
How did biological reasoning guide the refactoring? The round 2 designs illustrate the process: measurements from
earlier hits motivated combining a short membrane-proximal anchor with an ITAM and a CD40-derived segment.
Agents helped turn these observations into sequence proposals, and subsequent experiments determined which
combinations worked. Round 3 then tested which changes to the lead preserved or impaired function, allowing us
to trace ideas across designs and rounds (Fig. 5a). Because current models did not reliably predict which designs
would work (Figs. S8 and S11a–e), we relied on the screens to choose among designs. Experimental feedback
guides design in a similar way in directed evolution, antibody design and agent-guided experimentation [122–126].
Given the earlier rounds’ results, the LLMs’ predictions tracked which ideas worked, and tracked which designs
worked only when the models were asked whether a design would exceed the comparator (Fig. 5c), consistent with
LLMs judging candidates more reliably against one another than on an absolute scale [127]. Whether agent-guided
design yields better designs per round than expert or model-based design, and whether it reduces the number of
rounds needed, remain to be tested against a baseline matched for experimental budget and data [128], which
could also separate the contributions of literature retrieval, biological reasoning and earlier measurements.
Several aspects of our work merit further investigation. The Y1 and JM constraints identified here require prospec-
tive tests on new designs. Several experiments used T cells from few donors. Our successive-day challenges
measured sustained function rather than serial killing by the same cells, and we did not measure exhaustion, which
tonic signaling promotes [11] and rest can reverse [129]. Whether the CD40-derived segment recruits TRAF6 in a
CAR, how many cells co-express all four CARs and how compact-ICD CAR T cells perform in vivo remain to be
determined.
In summary, successive rounds of agent-guided rational design and experimental feedback identified a 45-aa CAR
ICD that increases expression without detectable antigen-independent IFN-γ, extends function after transient
mRNA delivery and enhances function after lentiviral and lipid-nanoparticle delivery. The screens also exposed
sequence constraints of compact ICDs, such as how close to the membrane the first ITAM tyrosine can sit. Because
krgr–ζ1–40 yielded more receptor per unit of mRNA and occupies less of the payload, compact ICDs may lower
the dose needed to generate CAR T cells in vivo [64–67] and enable more complex CAR therapies, such as CAR
cocktails.

CAR constructs and mRNA synthesis
Each CAR comprised the CD8α signal peptide, a FLAG (DYKDDDDK) tag, the antigen-binding domain, the CD8α
hinge and transmembrane domain, and the ICD. The BCMA binders were the D-domain binder ddBCMA [87] in
the Jurkat screen and Fig. 2i, the bb2121 scFv [92] in primary T cells, and the biparatopic VHH of ciltacabtagene
autoleucel in Fig. 2i. CD19, HER2 and MUC16 CARs carried the FMC63 scFv [93], a trastuzumab-derived scFv
and an anti-MUC16 scFv from Generate Biomedicines, respectively. ICD coding sequences were codon-optimized
for human expression with CodonTransformer [130] and DNA Chisel [131]. DNA templates were synthesized as
linear double-stranded DNA by Integrated DNA Technologies, as eBlocks for the compact-ICD CARs with the
ddBCMA binder and as gBlocks for all other constructs, and were transcribed without linearization. The constructs
carried proprietary in-house 5′ and 3′ UTRs.

Lentiviral transduction

cells from the same donor served as controls.

CD3-targeted lipid nanoparticles
CD3-targeted lipid nanoparticles (tLNPs) encapsulating FMC63 CAR mRNA with the BBζ or krgr–ζ1–40 ICD were

were added to whole PBMCs, CAR T cells were generated in the same culture as the autologous B cells they

Agent-guided sequence design
We used AI agents with access to computational tools to propose compact CAR ICDs from scientist-defined
objectives and reference sequences. In round 1, we used Claude Opus 4.6, Claude Sonnet 4.6, GPT-5.2, Kimi
K2.5 and MiniMax M2.5. brs–ζ1–40 was proposed by Claude Opus 4.6 in round 1. We prompted the agents to
shorten CD3ζ, vary ITAM number and identity, incorporate costimulatory motifs, or jointly redesign activation
and costimulatory elements (Supplementary materials, “Round 1 design prompts”).
The agents had access to literature search, protein annotation, sequence analysis, Python execution, structure
prediction and visual assessment of predicted structures. The computational tools ran as serverless functions on
Modal.
In rounds 2 and 3, we worked with Claude Code (Opus 4.7 and Opus 4.8, respectively) to interpret experimental
measurements and refine the designs. We used literature evidence and results from earlier rounds to define design
objectives, revise candidate sequences and develop matched controls.
We organized the designs of each round by idea. An idea was a design question with a written rationale, for example
whether the KRGR·KQGQ JM segment acts through charge or through spacing. Each design was assigned to one
idea when it was proposed, and the agents gave each idea a short code name. Because no screen could cover all the
sequences that an idea allows, the designs within an idea were chosen as contrasts that one plate could interpret.
Each design changed one feature relative to the same-plate comparator, and the set included charge-matched
pairs at fixed length, residue-by-residue trims, re-tests of earlier designs beside matched controls and tyrosine-null
calibrators. For some designs, the rationale also stated the expected direction of the result. Fig. S7 shows six
ideas organized in this way, including one whose readouts did not separate its designs.

Evaluation of agent-generated design hypotheses
We evaluated the agents’ design hypotheses retrospectively against the screens (Figs. 5b,c, S8 and S9).
Design data. The benchmark comprised 339 unique ICDs with activity calls: 186 from round 1 (Jurkat NFAT;
active at 0.25 or more of BBζ), 15 from round 2, screened in primary T cells alongside the 78 re-screened round 1
designs, which thus also received round 2 calls (IFN-γ, active at 0.5 or more of the same-plate BBζ), and 138 new
round 3 ICDs (H929 killing, active at 0.5 or more of krgr–ζ1–40). Constructs encoding the same protein took the
majority call; plate controls were excluded, as was krgr–ζ1–40 from round 3, where it served as the reference. For
P(better), a design exceeded the comparator when its IFN-γ was at least that of the comparator on the same plate
(round 2, BBζ, 6 of 15 designs; round 3, krgr–ζ1–40, 16 of the 117 designs with IFN-γ).
Predictions. Five LLMs, each run three times on the same task text (three queried directly and two run as
coding agents), gave the probability that each ICD was active, P(active), and, in a separate task, that its readout
exceeded that of the round’s comparator, P(better), zero-shot and in context, that is, given the measured ICDs of
the earlier rounds (round 1 for round 2; rounds 1 and 2 for round 3). To compare ideas by relative rather than
absolute belief, we removed each model’s offset: for every model, round and condition, one shift on the log-odds
scale set the model’s mean prediction over the round’s test ICDs to the active fraction of the earlier rounds’ labeled
ICDs (those given in the in-context prompt), so that no test label was used. Because one shift is applied to all of
a model’s predictions in a round and condition, it leaves each model’s ranking of the designs, and so its AUROC,
unchanged. It removes the models’ overall overprediction of activity (in round 3, a mean zero-shot prediction of
0.78 against an observed active fraction of 0.66), which would otherwise dominate the comparison of zero-shot and
in-context predictions. For ensemble predictions, probabilities were averaged over runs within a model, and the
five models were combined with equal weight.
Metrics. AUROC and specificity were computed per run against the measured calls, with 95% intervals from
1,000 bootstrap resamples of ICDs (Fig. S8). Specificity was computed for each model’s own calls and, independent
of its calling threshold, at the highest threshold on its predicted probability that still called at least 90% of active
ICDs active, with ties at the threshold called active. For each idea, we compared the mean prediction over its
designs, zero-shot and in context, with the fraction of its designs observed active or above the comparator (Fig.
5b). An in-context prediction supported the outcome when its mean lay at least 0.05 closer to the observed fraction
than the zero-shot mean. An idea was underrated or overrated when, treating its designs as independent trials
with the in-context probabilities, a count as extreme as the one observed had a probability below 0.10, with the
observed fraction above or below the in-context mean. The in-context shift of an idea was compared with the gap
between its observed fraction and its zero-shot mean by Spearman correlation, and among ideas that moved by
0.03 or more, by the share that moved toward the outcome.

Finally, the Spearman correlation between the ensemble prediction and IFN-γ relative to each round’s comparator
was computed per design and per idea (the mean over its designs, with ratios capped at 2), for both predictions
on the designs scored in both tasks, with intervals from 2,000 bootstrap resamples of designs or ideas (Fig. 5c).
For ideas with at least four designs, the correlation of in-context P(active) with the activity readout was computed
among the designs of one idea (Fig. S9b).

Agentic digital infrastructure
We built an electronic lab notebook system to store all our sequences, protocols, and experimental data. Claude
Code serves as the primary interface, with dedicated graphical interfaces for tasks that demand consistent visual
interaction, such as plate map visualization and flow cytometry gating.
Routine procedures were written as shared agent skills instead of fixed application code. For example, coding
agents performed codon optimization from a shared skill that defined the procedure and adapted it to individual
sequences and constraints, without each edge case being encoded in advance [137,138].
We use minimal data models to store only core information and leverage agents to infer many relationships and
details at runtime (Fig. S13). For example, after each experiment, users upload raw data to a shared filesystem,
where agents use plate maps to link measurements to designed variants and experimental conditions for analysis
and plotting. Typically, we discuss results and make decisions with comprehensive analyses and plots in hand
within minutes of instrument readout.
The PostgreSQL database and file storage ran on Supabase.
