Amazon tells its engineers to stop 'tokenmaxxing' on AI, focus on long-term outcomes

An Amazon memo published Tuesday encouraged its software engineers to stop "tokenmaxxing" and start being more judicious about how they are using AI to accomplish tasks this week.

The memo, circulated within Amazon last month but made public on Tuesday, argued that AI token usage and code generation are "deeply flawed" metrics for measuring productivity.

“We believe that it is best to measure, and be measured by, long-term outcomes,” the company wrote.

“We’re just seeing the beginning of AI’s effect on software engineering,” the company wrote. “While we have a vision for what the future looks like, there’s a lot that’s still emerging. We are certain that whatever future best practices are, AI will be at the center of software engineering practice.”

Major companies have struggled to square the massive cost of AI usage while the benefits of using the technology are often hard to measure at a large scale.

Anthropic discloses breaches, security-related training pause in IPO filing

Artificial intelligence giant Anthropic's IPO filing [https://www.foxnews.com/live-news/ai-news-anthropic-claude-openai-chatgpt-10-27-26] is drawing scrutiny over what it leaves out about the potential impact of AI on American workers, despite the company's repeated warnings that the technology could disrupt white-collar employment.

Axios senior AI reporter Madison Mills pointed out Tuesday that Anthropic's filing with the Securities and Exchange Commission discusses the reputational and regulatory risks associated with concerns about AI replacing workers but does not explicitly address whether widespread job losses could hurt its own business.

"For all the warnings about AI replacing knowledge workers, Anthropic's IPO filing barely addresses job displacement, and says nothing about what mass unemployment could mean for its own business," Mills wrote on X.

Mills noted that the word "jobs" appears just five times in the filing, with three references focused on how public concerns about AI's impact on employment could damage Anthropic's reputation, invite regulation or slow adoption of its technology.

The company estimates that knowledge work represents more than $40 trillion in global economic value, positioning AI as a tool to boost productivity and economic growth rather than projecting how widespread adoption could affect employment.

Fox News' Stephen Sorace contributed to this report.

The apparent omission is notable given Anthropic's own research into workplace disruption. In a report published earlier this month, the company found that its share of junior technical hires had declined as AI agents increasingly took over coding and other tasks.

Anthropic study finds professional analysts largely present AI judgements as their own

Getty Images

Anthropic released [https://www.foxbusiness.com/markets/anthropic-files-sec-paperwork-ai-giant-prepares-major-ipo] a study on Tuesday finding that a large majority of professional statistical analysts deferred to AI when crunching data and presented the AI's judgement as their own.

The study tasked 50 professional analysts with using Claude code to scrutinize a farming cooperative and recommend whether an advisory service it was running was effective and should be expanded.

The analysts were required to make four choices between two reasonable options during the process, but Anthropic did not inform them that the Claude code they were using would be biased in favor of one of those options based on a coin flip.

"The agent was told to make the best honest case for its lean, to change its mind if the evidence demanded it, and never to mislead," Anthropic wrote.

"Across the four analytical choices, analysts went with their agent’s lean 86% of the time. If the agent’s input made no difference, we’d expect about 50%. When analysts had already expressed their own preference before the agent weighed in, they switched away from it 60% of the time if the agent disagreed, compared to 6% if it agreed," it continued.

"Afterward, when asked to explain their choices, 77% of analysts used the agents’ own arguments—sometimes word for word—but only 15% credited the agent as the source of those arguments. Analysts who followed the agents’ recommendations closely were about as confident in their final recommendation as the rest, and roughly as likely to call it the product of their own judgment," Anthropic added.
