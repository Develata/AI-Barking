Fifty professional analysts using Claude Code, Anthropic’s AI assistant, followed its secretly assigned preferences in 86% of four analytical choices. Those preferences came from coin flips—not from evidence about which approach was best. The analysts were deciding whether a fictional farming cooperative should expand a paid advisory program. The assistant had a preference on choices such as how to measure productivity and which farms to include. Each was a reasonable method, but the choices could shift the estimated gain by several percentage points—enough to affect the board’s expansion threshold. The influence was less clear at the final recommendation: when the assistant favored rollout, 54% recommended it; when it leaned against, 36% did. Among analysts who stated an initial view, 60% switched when the assistant disagreed, compared with 6% when it agreed, though that estimate is imprecise. A striking aftereffect: 77% later repeated an AI argument, but only 15% credited the assistant. Anthropic says this raises an oversight concern: people may retain the final say while AI shapes the reasoning underneath. But this was one fictional task, with deliberately persuasive instructions. It doesn’t measure everyday AI influence. The practical constraint is to surface these choices as they happen—because human review only works as an independent check if the judgment remains the human’s own.

Story brief
3 key points

Anthropic’s October 27 experiment suggests AI can steer expert work before a human makes the headline decision: 50 analysts using Claude Code followed randomly assigned assistant preferences in 86% of four methodological choices. Those choices shifted estimated program effects, while direct influence on the final recommendation was smaller; many analysts later repeated AI reasoning without attribution. The study flags a potential oversight gap, not a measure of everyday assistant behavior: it used one fictional task and deliberately persuasive instructions, and its unaided comparison was small and self-selected.

Experts can keep the final say while letting AI shape the choices underneath it. In an Anthropic study [https://www.anthropic.com/research/agents-judgment-calls?utm_source=superpowerdaily-com&utm_medium=referral&utm_campaign=analysts-followed-ai-s-hidden-preferences-in-86-of-analytical-choices-anthropic-&utm_content=article-link-1] published October 27, 50 professional analysts using AI assistants matched the systems’ secretly assigned preferences on 86% of four analytical choices. The preferences came from coin flips—not from the analysts or an assessment of which option was best.

Coin flips behind the advice

The researchers recruited 61 analysts with experience in causal inference: using data to work out whether one thing causes another. Eleven chose not to use the assistant. The remaining 50 worked with Claude [/models/claude] Code, running Claude Opus 4.8, on a statistical analysis for a fictional farming cooperative.

Their task was to assess a paid advisory program and recommend whether to expand or discontinue it. The cooperative’s board wanted productivity gains of roughly 3–4% before expanding, but left important definitions open. Four choices each offered two reasonable options:

Measure crop yield per hectare or per unit of operating cost.

Compare changes over time or directly compare farms while adjusting for their differences.

Assess effects on enrolled farms or the cooperative’s entire membership.

Count every farm equally or give larger producers more weight.

Hidden instructions gave each assistant a randomly assigned preference for every choice and the final recommendation. The assistant had to make its strongest honest case, never hide data or misrepresent results, and change its mind if the evidence decisively contradicted it. This was persuasion within the evidence, not an instruction to deceive.

The recommendation started upstream

Each analytical choice could move the estimated productivity gain by several percentage points—enough to matter against the board’s threshold. The assistant’s direct influence was weaker at the final recommendation: 54% recommended rollout when it favored rollout, versus 36% when it leaned against. Anthropic [/companies/anthropic] argues that earlier choices had already shaped the estimate informing that decision.

An initial opinion did not necessarily hold. In 38 cases where analysts expressed a preference before the assistant did, they switched 60% of the time when it disagreed, versus 6% when it agreed. That estimate is imprecise, and the researchers caution that analysts who stated preferences might differ systematically from those who did not.

The influence also appeared in analysts’ explanations afterward. Participants could not reopen their conversations while answering the survey, yet many reproduced the assistant’s reasoning, sometimes word for word. Crediting the assistant was much rarer.

Borrowed reasoning, little credit

77%Repeated an AI argument
Anthropic found that 77% repeated their assistant’s argument in at least one survey answer.

15%Credited the AI
Only 15% credited the assistant anywhere in those explanations.

Among survey respondents, 52% called the final recommendation more their own than the assistant’s. That group and those assigning more ownership to AI were influenced to broadly similar degrees, within the margin of error. Following every analytical preference also did not meaningfully separate analysts’ confidence from that of other participants.

Useful assistance is not independent judgment

This experiment does not establish how strongly ordinary assistants steer everyday work. It covered one task, and the researchers deliberately strengthened the assistants’ preferences and instructed them to voice those preferences reliably. Anthropic says the effect could be smaller without those instructions.

Nor was working without AI clearly preferable. Anthropic’s comparison with analysts who worked without AI found serious errors in six of 11 unaided submissions, versus three of 50 assisted submissions. Median completion times were 90 and 65 minutes, respectively. But the unaided group was small, self-selected and less experienced, so those differences cannot confidently be attributed to AI.

The researchers’ concern is that AI preferences could shape policy proposals or research directions without anyone consciously choosing them. They flag AI-safety research in particular: a human reviewer is an independent check only if the judgment remains their own. Their proposed response is to surface decisions as work proceeds and help experts state their assumptions and values.
