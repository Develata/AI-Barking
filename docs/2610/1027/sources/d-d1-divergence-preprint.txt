    Towards a best-in-class IgE disruptor for food allergy
          with Claude-enabled antibody design
               Ezra Erives∗ , Emilio Ferrara∗ , Sean Michael, Nikhil Mallipeddi, Bowen Jing‡
                  ∗ These authors contributed equally   ‡ Correspondence to: bowen@divergencelabs.com




                                                        Abstract

      Food allergy affects an estimated 220–250 million people worldwide, for whom the constant
      risk of a severe reaction weighs heavily on quality of life. Allergic reactions are triggered by the
      cross-linking of allergen-specific IgE bound to its high-affinity receptor, FcεRI, on mast cells and
      basophils, causing degranulation, inflammation and, in severe cases, anaphylaxis. Omalizumab
      (Xolair), the sole FDA-approved biologic for food allergy, neutralizes circulating IgE; however,
      it does not effectively remove IgE from FcεRI, where it can persist for weeks and where even a
      small residual amount can sustain sensitivity. Here we describe an agentic computational design
      campaign with Claude toward a disruptive inhibitor antibody that physically removes bound
      IgE from FcεRI. Guided by our research team, Claude pursued a biparatopic molecule that pairs
      a disruptor arm, which displaces IgE from FcεRI, with a non-competing anchor arm that holds
      the molecule on receptor-bound IgE. Of 91 experimentally characterized disruptor designs,
      20 showed activity against receptor-bound IgE by competitive bio-layer interferometry, and
      in a dose-titration assay three variants removed more IgE than omalizumab at therapeutically
      relevant concentrations (25–400 nM). De novo designs for the anchor arm bound IgE at diverse
      epitopes with apparent affinities of 3.4–235 nM. These subcomponents provide the starting
      point for further characterization toward a best-in-class IgE disruptor.



1    Introduction
Food allergy is an atopic disease characterized by the overproduction of IgE reactive against otherwise
harmless environmental or food antigens. Allergen-induced cross-linking of IgE bound to the high-affinity
receptor FcεRI of mast cells and basophils releases preformed and newly synthesized inflammatory medi-
ators, resulting in classical symptoms ranging from moderate urticaria to severe, fatal anaphylaxis (Fig-
ure 2b) [1]. In the United States the disease affects up to 8% of children and about 10% of adults, roughly
33 million people in all, with about 40% of affected children reactive to more than one food [2–4]. Until re-
cently, the only option for food allergy patients was strict allergen avoidance and self-injectable epinephrine
for accidental exposures [5].
The fundamental pathway of food allergy is the interaction of allergen-specific IgE with the high-affinity
receptor FcεRI of mast cells and basophils, one of the strongest protein-receptor interactions in human
immunology. IgE binds FcεRIα with an affinity near 10−10 M and dissociates so slowly that mast cells
and basophils stay fully sensitized whether or not allergen is present [6–8], supported by continuous IgE
production by B cells [9–11]. While free IgE has a serum half-life of about two days, receptor-bound
IgE persists in human skin for weeks to months [9, 12–14]. Additionally, mast cells and basophils are
exquisitely sensitive to IgE cross-linking. For reference, a basophil carries some 105 FcεRI, yet only a few
thousand allergen-specific IgE molecules, one or two per cent of the receptor pool, give a half-maximal
response, and only a few hundred are enough to initiate secretion [15–17]. Therefore, food allergen

Over the past decade, the management and treatment of food allergy have been revolutionized. Most no-
tably, in 2024 omalizumab (Xolair), a humanized anti-IgE antibody, became the first FDA-approved medi-
cation to reduce allergic reactions to multiple foods [5, 18, 19]. In the OUtMATCH trial, 16 to 20 weeks of
omalizumab allowed 67% of patients with multi-food allergy to tolerate 600 mg of peanut protein, against
7% on placebo [18]. This is a major advance for the treatment of food allergy, yet it is far from completely
addressing the disease. Only 42% of treated patients completed the full 6,044 mg cumulative challenge for
two of their foods and 24% for all three. Furthermore, 14% could not consume even 30 mg of peanut after
the full 16 to 20 weeks of treatment [18]. The treatment regimen is also burdensome. Because omalizumab
neutralizes IgE stoichiometrically, the dose is set by body weight and total IgE. Patients must take 75 to
600 mg of drug by injection every two or four weeks, with no established dosing for patients whose body
weight and/or total IgE lie outside the approved label, excluding a significant population. Furthermore,
because omalizumab does not alter IgE production, free IgE returns after discontinuation of drug, requiring
patients to be treated indefinitely [5, 19]. Omalizumab therefore protects against accidental exposure, but
patients are advised to continue avoiding their allergens and must carry epinephrine [5, 19].
Omalizumab nonetheless represents a breakthrough in the treatment of food allergy and validates IgE as
the central mediator of the disease. Its limitations derive from the biology of the IgE:FcεRIα interaction
and from the development goals set for omalizumab in the 1990s (Figure 2) [20, 21].

IgE exists in two forms, free in the circulation and bound to FcεRI on the cell surface, and at therapeutically
relevant doses omalizumab neutralizes only the free form (Figure 2c). Because IgE triggers reactions
through clustering of the bound form, neutralizing free IgE disarms the cell only as fast as its existing
complexes turn over: basophils lose most of their surface IgE within about ten days, but FcεRI-positive
mast cells in skin are unchanged after one week and reduced only after ten [22–24]. The medicine with
the greatest potential for patients with food allergy would therefore not only neutralize free IgE but also
remove the IgE already bound to effector cells (Figure 2).

2   Neutralizing and disruptive IgE inhibitors
Omalizumab neutralizes free IgE by binding to the IgE Cε3 chain at two symmetrically related epitopes, and
in a manner which both directly overlaps with the bound conformation of FcεRIα, and which additionally
prevents the folded-back conformation of the Cε2-domain believed to stabilize the IgE:FcεRIα complex
(Figure 2a) [20, 21, 25, 26]. For these same reasons, it was originally thought omalizumab was unable to
bind IgE once it is already bound to FcεRIα. However, subsequent studies of omalizumab after its initial
development discovered that it could in fact disrupt IgE:FcεRIα complexes, but only at concentrations far
above those reached in patients. In this case, transient partial dissociation on the part of FcεRIα allows
omalizumab to occupy its partially overlapping epitope on Cε3, thereby preventing rebinding to FcεRIα
(Figure 2e) [25]. Indeed, at micromolar concentrations omalizumab has been shown to accelerate the
dissociation of pre-formed complexes and to remove IgE from basophils over hours. Omalizumab’s 50%
disruption capacity (DC50) is ∼200 µM (SPR) for bound IgE disruption compared to KD ≈ 0.2 nM for free
IgE inhibition [25, 27].

Orthosteric disruptive inhibitors In parallel to this work, designed ankyrin repeat protein (DARPin)
E2_79 was discovered and found to inhibit mediator release more effectively than omalizumab in cellular
assays [28, 29]. In 2012, a fluorescence assay developed to classify IgE inhibitors demonstrated that E2_79
disrupted not as a classical competitor of the FcεRIα epitope, but rather through a modeled steric overlap
of only 400 Å3 in comparison to omalizumab’s modeled value of 1200 Å3 , suggesting that large overlap
may not be necessary and may even be counter-productive to facilitating disruption [8, 25, 30]. Subse-
quently, E2_79 was shown to remove surface IgE from allergic-donor basophils within minutes, to abolish
IgE-dependent activation, and to protect mice carrying human FcεRIα from passive cutaneous anaphy-
laxis [27]. The DARPins that revealed facilitated dissociation, however, made a difficult starting point for
a medicine, limited by immunogenicity, developability, and no approved precedent for the scaffold [27, 31].
Based on this work, the academic groups behind the DARPins turned to engineering omalizumab itself to
establish disruptive potency [32, 33]. They evolved variants that strip humanized mast cells at a few
hundred nanomolar, and a cryo-EM structure of a variant bound to a partially dissociated complex con-
firmed steric facilitated dissociation as the mechanism of disruption [32]. Elbow-flexible descendants of
these variants desensitize mice within 36 hours of a single dose, where omalizumab matches vehicle [33].
Excellergy, a biotechnology company founded on this work, has now entered phase 1 clinical trials [33, 34].

Allosteric disruptive inhibitors Concurrently, the biotechnology company Ablynx isolated single-domain
antibodies (VHHs) against IgE from an unbiased llama immunization campaign, and found several that

not only blocked IgE binding to FcεRI but also displaced receptor-bound IgE from cells [35–37]. The
mechanism of one of these VHHs, sdAb026, was resolved later: it binds the CD23 (FcεRII) site at the
Cε3–Cε4 junction and traps the closed conformation that cannot bind FcεRIα, representing a second route
to disruption that acts from a distal epitope rather than at the interface [38]. In cellular assays, sdAb026
strips IgE from basophils within minutes.

Biparatopics Together, the orthosteric and allosteric disruptive lineages have established two distinct
mechanisms for driving disruption of IgE:FcεRIα complexes: steric facilitated dissociation, in which a
binder inserts at a transiently exposed sub-site of the receptor interface and competes the receptor off,
and allosteric conformational trapping, in which a binder at the distal CD23 (FcεRII) site locks Cε3 in the
closed conformation that cannot hold the receptor [8, 38]. In both cases, and regardless of modality, such
monovalent disruptors require concentrations of high hundreds of nanomolar to micromolar on cells to
mediate disruption. To significantly improve disruptive potency, biparatopic constructs have been gener-
ated by combining a monovalent disruptor arm with a second anchor arm against a non-competing epitope.
Fusing E2_79 to a DARPin that recognizes receptor-bound IgE at a distal Cε3–Cε4 surface improved effi-
ciency about 100-fold [27, 39]. Furthermore, the knob-in-hole IgG1 Fc-fusion, KIH_E07_79, brought the
half-maximal disruptive concentration on whole-blood basophils to 2 nM, roughly 500-fold the potency of
E2_79, and cleared IgE from basophils and tissue mast cells within nine hours in vivo [30, 39, 40]. Bi-
paratopic VHHs have similarly demonstrated significant disruptive potency gains by pairing sdAb026 with
non-competing anchoring VHHs [41]. Biparatopic constructs exhibit improved disruption because of an
avidity effect for the IgE:FcεRIα complex. The non-competing arm binds receptor-bound IgE and anchors
the molecule to the IgE:FcεRIα complex. This, in turn, holds the disruptive arm at a high local concentra-
tion, ready to act whenever the complex transiently adopts the state it recognizes (Figure 2d) [27, 39, 41].

Anaphylactogenicity The same biparatopic mechanism whose avid cis-binding of a single receptor-bound
IgE molecule enables significantly higher disruptive potency also carries the risk of trans-binding of two
distinct receptor-bound IgE molecules, which can lead to cross-linking and anaphylaxis [32]. As a clini-
cal example, the development of omalizumab variant HAE1 was discontinued after adverse events in two
participants during a phase 2 trial. Although the nature of the events has not yet been disclosed, cellular
assays demonstrated HAE1 could activate basophils from some donors, attributed to longer dwell time on
intact IgE:FcεRIα complexes [32]. The flexible binding geometry of biparatopics demands that significant
consideration be taken so as to avoid this unwanted cross-linking. While initial experimental validation
suggested that KIH_E07_79, for example, was non-activating [39, 40], subsequent testing in additional
donors reported a high degree of anaphylactogenic activity [41]. Further characterization has strongly
implicated the arm geometry, linking moiety (tandem or Fc-fusion) and orientation, as well as the affinity
and kinetics of the arms themselves, in determining the anaphylactogenicity [39–41]. Hence, a truly best-
in-class disruptive IgE inhibitor must engage receptor-bound IgE strongly enough to disrupt the complex,
yet be physically unable to bridge two IgE molecules on the same cell.

3   A computational design campaign toward a best-in-class IgE inhibitor
Potent and safe disruption of bound IgE calls for precise control over molecular geometry. Conventional an-
tibody discovery via display or immunization provides little control of the binding epitope or pose, making
complex therapeutic mechanisms such as IgE disruption challenging to target. On the other hand, biomolec-
ular generative models can specify or predict the geometry before any molecule is made, enabling rational
optimization or selection. We hypothesized that a computational design campaign leveraging modern
biomolecular generative models, directed by Claude [42], and informed by prior literature and rational
epitope and pose selection could accelerate the path towards a best-in-class IgE inhibitor: one able to in-
hibit free IgE with high affinity, disrupt IgE:FcεRI on allergic effector cells with potency at therapeutically
relevant doses, and do so without cross-linking and activating effector cells.
We set out to design a biparatopic antibody with two functional VHH arms, one that disrupts the IgE:FcεRIα
interaction and one that anchors the molecule to receptor-bound IgE at a geometrically compatible epitope.

3.1                                     Claude-steered decomposition and orchestration of the design task
The design campaign was largely driven by interaction with Claude. First, in conjunction with our research
team, Claude orchestrated high-level design planning and decomposed the stated objective into distinct
work phases. Second, it proceeded with near-autonomous execution of these work phases, supported
by our research team throughout, as Claude has recently done for de novo binder design against diverse
targets [42]. In total, the campaign consisted of a total of 22 distinct phases of autonomous execution, of
which 15 were primarily devoted toward computational design. We provide a high-level summary of the
workflow in Figure 3.
To initiate the design campaign, Claude performed an extensive review of characterized disruptive ap-
proaches from both academic and preclinical work, and grouped these into two salient mechanistic classes:
First, orthosteric disruptors, including the monovalent and biparatopic lineages extending DARPin E2_79
[8, 27], affinity-evolved, disruption-selected omalizumab variants [32, 33, 39], and even omalizumab it-
self at sufficiently high concentration [25, 26], act by exploiting small, transiently freed steric overlap with
FcεRIα to gain occupancy and subsequently drive dissociation. Second, allosteric disruptors act as CD23
function homologs [44], displaying selective affinity for the non-receptor-competent closed conformation of


                                                                                                                                                                                                          6
IgE Fc Cε3–Cε4, and which includes examples such as sdAb026 [38]. Claude then proceeded to analyze the
amenability of each class to computational redesign and eventual inclusion within a biparatopic construct,
identifying key functional and structural considerations for each class critical to facilitating disruption and
whose accurate recapitulation would be required on the part of the computational model employed.
Toward this end, Claude identified experimentally derived, publicly available data to be used as a means
of directly and quantitatively gauging function-specific modeling performance. For example, Claude per-
formed structure prediction on a number of campaign-relevant resolved structures, including both the or-
thosterically disrupted intermediate state induced by the clone 7 omalizumab variant (PDB 7SHT) [32, 45],
and the disruptive binding mode of sdAb026 (PDB 5NQW) [38, 43], observing uniformly high accuracy
over both classes in identifying the open/closedness of Cε3–Cε4, but displaying higher confidence for the
allosteric disruptors. Such observations, the existence of an experimentally validated disruptor in VHH
sdAb026, and the existence of a solved structure by which to guide redesign, resulted in a final judgment
to emulate the allosteric rather than orthosteric disruption mechanism.1
Finally, to better inform the choice of co-folding models for use in downstream optimization, Claude iden-
tified experimentally validated loss-of-function mutants of sdAb026 [38], and used these as the basis of a
calibration mechanism by which to select two distinct co-folding models whose confidence most accurately
recreated the disruptive rank-ordering of wild type against the disclosed mutants. Claude further clarified
the functional specification of the desired biparatopic into respective designs for both arms and a necessary
linking moiety. Together with key structural or functional insights, it decomposed the design campaign into
subsequent phases. The overall flow of this campaign-planning stage is summarized in Figure 3, left.

3.2    Disruptor redesign
To initiate disruptor redesign, Claude began by analyzing the interface geometry of the resolved sdAb026
binding mode to IgE Cε3–Cε4 in PDB 5NQW [38, 43], identifying three scopes within which to conduct
targeted, structurally localized redesign. These scopes include a Cε4, receptor-distal interface, mediated by
both part of the CDR3 loop and a collection of framework regions (Cε4 interface scope), a Cε3, receptor-
proximal interface mediated by CDR1 and CDR3 (Cε3 interface scope), and a third scope consisting of the
interface-distal CDR2 loop (CDR2 scope). A fourth scope consisting of CDR1 and CDR3 residues which do
not participate in the interface as contacts but which can be inferred to play supporting, interface-stabilizing
roles, was advocated for by Claude and carried through the initial inverse-folding and tempered-redesign
rounds; however, we opted to drop it from further campaign phases so as to diversify resources away from
the binding interface toward the distal CDR2 scope instead. More details on the choice of scopes can be
found in Appendix A.1, and a brief visual overview is included in Figure 3.
Claude then elected to split subsequent disruptor redesign efforts into six distinct sub-phases, each con-
sisting of a choice of redesign scope and redesign paradigm. For both the Cε3 interface and Cε4 interface
scopes, Claude opted to perform targeted mutagenesis so as to obtain double, triple, and quadruple mu-
tants with superior disruptive effect. To do so, two primary methods were employed: inverse folding of
randomly sampled sub-scopes [46], as well as tempered redesign, a purpose-built, gradient-based muta-
genesis approach not requiring prior specification of mutation scope. Claude enabled real-time conceptual-
ization, implementation, and subsequent validation and deployment of this key methodological extension,
and more broadly meticulously tailored design workflows (both open and internal) to campaign-specific
contexts. Finally, both inverse folding and gradient-based redesign were utilized against the CDR2 scope.
At the completion of the round, Claude refolded all designs using the scoring models nominated in Sec-
tion 3.1, ranked by 10-seed mean binder-pair ipTM, and nominated thirteen distinct designs from each of
the four Cε3 interface and Cε4 interface design scopes, accompanied by an additional forty designs from
the CDR2 scope, for subsequent experimental validation. A more detailed overview of the round phasing
can be found in Figure 3 (middle column), and a summary of results is reported in Figure 4. We refer the
reader to Section 3.2 for a more extensive treatment of each phase mentioned above, and to Appendix C
for further details on tempered redesign.

3.3       De novo anchor design
The anchor arm was designed de novo against two surfaces that remain exposed on receptor-bound IgE
(Figure 2d). The first is the Cε2 domain pair, the epitope class of the VHH anchors that improved sdAb026
in biparatopic format [41]. The second is the Cε3–Cε4 surface recognized by E3_53, the anchor of the
first biparatopic DARPin [27, 39]. Claude first refolded the published Cε2-binding VHHs [41] with the
selected co-folding critics to check whether the models placed them on a consistent epitope, and used the
consensus footprint as the first target.
Anchors were designed by backpropagation through the structure predictor from a random start, over all
three CDRs, on twelve clinical VHH frameworks (Table 2). Three phases of design were run on Cε2: one
against the consensus epitope, one against randomly drawn epitope patches across the domain, and one
without epitope conditioning (Appendix B.2). On Cε3–Cε4, two phases were run: the first with randomly
drawn epitope patches as for Cε2 and with an additional avoidance loss penalizing contact with the FcεRIα
and sdAb026 footprints, as well as a subsequent, completely unconditioned run without epitope condition-
ing. Both rounds were subsequently followed up by an iterative refinement scheme based on inverse folding
[46–49]. In total, 23,600 unique designs were scored in 1,310 H100-hours, and 57 were nominated, 42
on Cε2 and 15 on Cε3–Cε4 (Figure 4, Table 3). We refer the reader to Appendix B for a more technical
discussion, to Figure 3 (right column) for a broad overview of the round, and to Figure 4 for results for the
round regarding plate nomination for subsequent experimental validation.

3.4    Biparatopic assembly
In the final phase of the design campaign, we asked Claude to use the computational results characterizing
the disruptive potency and binding affinity of the designs from Section 3.2 and Section 3.3, together with
model predictions of pose and binding geometry to assemble promising biparatopics. As discussed in
Section 2, the choice of linking moiety varies among precedented disruptors, and within a larger design
space consisting of both single-chain tandem linkers, such as the (G4 S)2 linker found in biparatopic DARPin
bi53_79 [27], and bulkier, knob-in-hole (KIH), IgG fusion constructs [39, 41]. To further narrow the
design mandate, we requested that Claude limit its search to encompass only tandem (G4 S)k linkers for
k ∈ {3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8}. Despite the limited search space, the dimer symmetry of IgE Fc, combined with the
labile nature of Cε2, ensures that geometric characterization of the likely cis-binding mode of a putative
biparatopic remains a difficult task.
A long and flexible linker is a serious liability for an IgE-targeting biparatopic. The desired biparatopic effect
by which an anchor arm binding raises the effective local disruptor arm concentration so as to increase the
disruptive potency of the molecule relies on a shorter, rather than longer, linker. The role of linker length,
and of linker and arm geometry more generally, is widely implicated as playing a dominant role beyond
affinity and kinetics in the anaphylactogenic liability of a particular construct [39, 41]. Indeed, a longer
linker and more flexible geometry can accommodate trans binding of distinct receptor-bound molecules,
in turn leading to stable bridging, activation, and eventually, anaphylaxis.
With this context present, Claude proceeded to split the task in two: First, Claude conducted a systematic
enumeration of potential IgE Fc Cε2–Cε4 conformations with bound FcεRIα by identifying all deposited
structures in the PDB with these chains present (2Y7Q, 7SHT, 8YWA). For each putative pair of anchor and
disruptor, all structures were enumerated along with all combinations of symmetrically related binding sites
for both anchor and disruptor arm, so as to yield a total of twelve crude, candidate cis-binding geometries.
Second, Claude derived an algorithm which, for each fixed geometry and choice of k ∈ {3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8},
attempts to construct a placement of the alpha carbons of (G4 S)k tandem linker residues so as to yield an
unobstructed path between the pair of complementary VHH termini. For the latter, minor intervention on
our part was required to ensure that pathological linker geometries (i.e., threading the “donut hole” of the
open conformation of IgE Cε3–Cε4) were excluded, and that certain steric clashes were properly accounted
for. The combined procedure then nominates the shortest such k for which an unobstructed linker could
be constructed, along with the corresponding construction. One such linker construction is provided in
Figure 4 (bottom right).

4     Results
We nominated designs from both the disruptor and anchor design campaigns for experimental validation
and assessed their functional activity via bio-layer interferometry (BLI). For the disruptor arm, we per-
formed a multi-phase, competitive BLI experiment to measure the extent to which the designed antibody
in solution can displace IgE bound to an immobilized soluble FcεRIα ectodomain, mimicking the physio-
logical context of FcεRI on mast cells and basophils. For the anchor arm, we performed standard kinetic
characterization of free IgE binding to immobilized VHH.

4.1    Designed disruptor variants displace IgE from bound FcεRIα
We first nominated 91 disruptor designs for experimental screening, alongside the parent sdAb026 and
single alanine mutants of its Cε4-facing CDR3 previously reported to remove functional activity [38], at
a single concentration. Twenty designs removed IgE faster than every negative control. We classified
these 20 sequences as positive hits for disruptive activity. Six hits appeared to improve upon the activity
of sdAb026. Hits originated from every design scope and method, albeit at different rates (Figure 5D).
Redesign of CDR2 was by far the most productive route, accounting for most of the hits and most of the
designs that appeared to exceed the disruptive activity of the parent. However, activity did not depend
on staying close to the parent sequence, and functional activity was retained for sequences differing by
as many as eight substitutions (Figure 5B), demonstrating the ability of biomolecular models to navigate

4.2   Epitope-diverse anchor arm hits
We next nominated and experimentally tested 57 anchor arm designs via BLI, with the VHH immobilized
on the probe. Three variants exhibited cleanly resolved kinetic fits, for a hit rate of approximately 5%
(Figure 6A). The hits derived from two of the three epitope-specific design blocks: the side of Cε2 distal
to the consensus binding site of variants B1, E1, and F4 [41]; and the Cε3–Cε4 surface. Somewhat
surprisingly, no designs targeting the consensus epitope of variants B1, E1, and F4 exhibited binding.
Corroborating recent literature [42], the critic scores do not perfectly anticipate which designs would
bind; anchor design 1 was the lowest-ranked of the 19 nominees targeting the distal Cε2 surface, whereas
anchor designs 2 and 3, were second-best within second of the 15 nominees targeting the Cε3–Cε4 surface.
Plotting the association rate against the off-rate (Figure 6C) reveals the three hits associate at similar
rates, so their range of apparent affinities, from 3.4 to 235 nM, arises almost entirely from their off-rates.
We caveat, however, that these affinity measurements are potentially confounded by avidity; because IgE
presents each Fc domain twice, binding events to adjacent immobilized VHHs may not be independent.
Curiously, though, anchor designs 1 and 3 appear to occupy a near-symmetric, single binding site at the in-
tersection of the two IgE monomers (Figure 6A). Experimental characterization of avidity will thus provide
an unusual mechanism by which to directly probe the accuracy of the predicted binding pose.

5   Discussion and future work
In this report, we demonstrated that computational design can produce two functional arms of a biparatopic
as the first key step towards a best-in-class IgE disruptive inhibitor. Our parallel design campaigns produced
(a) a disruptor arm that removes IgE from FcεRI, and (b) an anchor arm that binds to IgE at a noncom-
petitive epitope. Through Claude agentic orchestration of biomolecular generative models [42], both were
obtained within weeks rather than the months expected of traditional screening workflows.
Starting from sdAb026, a single-domain antibody with previously reported disruptive activity [38], we
obtained 20 variants with measurable disruptive activity. These variants were up to eight substitutions from
the parent and represent a diverse functional family different from one another by as many as ten residues
(out of 31 CDR residues). These findings mirror similar results in the use of biomolecular foundation models
to design functional sequences far from original starting points [50–52]. We will continue to explore
agent-orchestrated computational design campaigns [42] to venture further from known sequence space,
steering towards other properties of interest in addition to preserving function. While we did not measure
any variants with improved functional activity over sdAb026 at controlled doses, diversification of existing
functional sequences provides multiple starting points for independent optimization for properties beyond
functional potency, such as developability, biparatopic compatibility, and cross-reactivity. This represents a
significant reduction in time and cost over the custom library-scale screening protocols previously required
for such diversification [32, 33].
In combination with our anchor designs, we expect biparatopic construction (as tandem VHH or as VHH-
Fc) to provide a significant boost to potency and disruptive efficacy. Indeed, the parental disruptor sdAb026
was reported to exhibit disruption at therapeutically relevant concentrations only when combined with an
anchoring VHH [35, 37, 41]. Similarly, the orthosteric disruptor DARPin E2_79 experienced dramatically
improved potency after conversion to a biparatopic format [27, 39]. In this light, we are encouraged to
observe improved activity over omalizumab even when our designs are expressed as monovalent constructs.
A key limitation of the disruptor design methodology pursued here is the reliance on an existing sequence
(and in fact, docked structure) of a molecular species with the desired functional activity. Future work
will explore if Claude can employ reasoning strategies to design functional sequences when no reference
sequence is available; or even more ambitiously, when the structural specification of the desired function
(in this case, inducing disruption by biasing IgE Fc toward a closed conformation) is entirely unavailable.
The subcomponents generated here by Claude will provide an excellent basis for the development of can-
didate biparatopics for further characterization towards a best-in-class IgE disruptive inhibitor. Following
biophysical validation of free and bound IgE inhibition, candidates will be tested in basophil and mast-cell
assays for disruption, activation, and protection against allergen challenge across donors, followed by fur-
ther lead optimization and functional, pharmacokinetic, and pharmacodynamic studies in animals. A key
risk will be to avoid anaphylactogenicity via receptor cross-linking; we anticipate that geometric reasoning
and analysis of biparatopic formats with Claude will prove highly useful in this next stage of discovery.
Looking beyond IgE, the strategies and methods developed in this work create a powerful foundation
for additional campaigns of rationally designed antibodies capable of more complex biological functions,
opening the door for best-in-class medicines across human diseases.

Determination of design scope After analyzing the binding mode of sdAb026 in PDB 5NQW, Claude
nominated the Cε3 interface, Cε4 interface, and free scopes detailed in Figure 7. As reported in the main
text, the free scope was eventually dropped in favor of a fourth, interface-distal CDR2 scope so as to
increase the diversity and extent of the explored optimization space. We emphasize that chronologically,
full redesign was initially carried out against the three non-CDR2 scopes before the conceptualization of
the fourth, complementary CDR2 scope reported in Figure 3. Claude then further identified the first three,
interface-proximal scopes as being candidates for targeted mutagenesis (edit distance 2-4) rather than
full scope redesign, utilizing both inverse folding (Appendix A.2) and a gradient-based mutagenic design
scheme developed for precisely this purpose, as described in Appendix A.3.

Inverse Folding with Manually Constructed Scopes In light of poor critic confidence on the part of
manually calibrated biased inverse folding, Claude elected instead to manually enumerate all double-,
triple-, and a select number of quadruple mutants of sdAb026 until a predetermined budget of N = 750
was obtained, and subsequently inverse fold at T = 0.4 with SolMPNN. Critic confidence with respect to
both nominated critics, edit distance, and DockQ are visualized in Figure 10. In particular, we report both
mean critic binder-pair ipTM (that is, the average ipTM between the design and the respective IgE dimer
chains), and the sub-interface DockQ mean [53, 54], which we define as the average DockQ of the variant
with respect to both Cε3 and Cε4 interfaces.

B.3       Computational design against Cε3–Cε4
Linker- and epitope-related filtering procedure The flexible positioning of IgE Cε2 relative to Cε3–Cε4
makes it hard to gauge the geometric compatibility of an anchor placed on the latter with a disruptor on the
former on tandem or Fc-fusion linker geometry, as doing so would require more precise modeling of domain
rearrangement and linker geometry to accommodate cis binding of the resulting biparatopic. In contrast,
the comparatively rigid Cε3–Cε4 varies largely between the open and closed conformations described in
Section 2, so that direct screening for linker compatibility is more straightforward. For this reason, Claude
formulated a screening algorithm for determining the compatibility of a choice of anchor with a C-terminal
IgG1 fusion, as in [41], and together with disruptor sdAb026 (via PDB 5NQW). The procedure involves
first performing a rigid alignment of both the anchor arm as well as one of the two symmetrically related
sdAb026 chains in 5NQW to PDB 2Y7Q. Then, for each of linker lengths (G4 S)1 (n = 4) and (G4 S)2 (n = 8),
we construct circular shells centered at the respective VHH N-termini, and with inner and outer radii equal
to 0.8 × L and 1.2 × L for L ≜ 0.8 × 3.8 × n. A 1 Åcircular grid is then constructed about the midpoint
of the N-termini, and a grid search is performed for apices which satisfy strict clash criteria. Namely, the
shortest clash-free paths are identified via Dijkstra’s shortest-path algorithm on a 1.5Å cubic lattice, with
lattice points within 3 Åof IgE Fc, receptor, or arm chains excluded. A final check is applied by screening
for clashes involving a rigid-body cylindrical approximation of the IgG1 Fc. If such a non-clashing path
exists, the anchor is said to be linker-compatible. We additionally apply a secondary, epitope-related filter
removing any design which overlaps with the respective residue footprints of sdAb026 or FcεRIα.

Epitope scanning against Cε3–Cε4 In line with de novo design against Cε2, Claude similarly pursued
design against Cε3–Cε4 with an identical epitope scanning procedure to the one described in Appendix B.2,
and with a similar repulsive avoidance loss intended to exclude residues within the footprints of sdAb026
and FcεRIα. A total of 1584 design trajectories were run, after which the top-50 designs by mean critic
ipTM passing both linker compatibility and epitope filters were selected for further refinement in the same
manner as described in the Cε2 epitope scanning phase, significantly diversifying the set of high-confidence,
filter-passing designs, and as shown in Figure 13 (top-right, middle-left).

C.1                    Targeted mutagenesis via tempered redesign
Our design platform consists of a gradient-based design model similar to that disclosed in Candido et al.
[55], and which natively supports the redesign of particular residue subsets starting from scratch. In con-
junction with Claude, we reasoned that by modifying the annealed temperature schedule to instead start
at a low, near-zero temperature before rising to a predetermined apex, and then cooling back down to the
starting temperature, we could realize a gradient-based mutagenic design scheme. In particular, recall the

D     Experimental methods
Protein expression and purification. VHH designs were expressed by cell-free protein synthesis (Gen-
Script CFXpress PLUS E. coli CFPS kit, cat. RP-D00006-1) from 125 ng of linear DNA template carrying
a T7 promoter, ribosome-binding site, the VHH with a C-terminal Twin-Strep tag, a stop codon and a T7
terminator, in the kit’s reaction buffer according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For the screens, reac-
tions were used unpurified after dilution in PBS or Gator K buffer. For purification, variants were captured
from the reactions on MagStrep Strep-Tactin XT beads (IBA Lifesciences, cat. 2-5090-010), washed with
Buffer W (100 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA) and eluted with Buffer BXT (Buffer W
with 50 mM biotin). Protein concentrations were measured on a Qubit Flex fluorometer.

Bio-layer interferometry. All measurements were made on a Gator Plus instrument (Gator Bio) at 30 ◦ C
with shaking at 1000 rpm. Unless otherwise noted, all dilutions and buffer steps used Gator K buffer.

Competitive disruption assay, screen. Soluble His-tagged FcεRIα ectodomain (Sino 13193-H08H-100)
was loaded onto anti-His XT probes (Gator Bio) for 90 s, followed by 30 s of buffer. IgE (Abcam AB65866-
1008) reconstituted to 100 nM was then bound to the receptor for 120 s, followed by 30 s of buffer. The
probe was then moved for 120 s into the cell-free expression reaction of each design (diluted 3× in PBS).

Competitive disruption assay, purified variants. Eight active designs, alongside sdAb026 and Y112A,
were purified as above. They were tested at 25, 50, 100, 200 and 400 nM with omalizumab (MedChem-

30, 90, 120, 150 and 180 s, followed by a 600 s exposure to the purified test article.
