                                                          Abstract
            Lab-in-the-loop systems, in which a model proposes molecules, automated assays measure their prop-
        erties and the results retrain the model, are a popular approach to antibody lead optimization. These
        systems optimize for a specification defined with metrics computed from instrument signals, so their
        success depends on how well those metrics serve as proxies for the properties a drug candidate needs.
        When a metric is a poor proxy, neither a better model nor more data will fix it, and identifying the problem
        usually requires expert review. We report two campaigns run on BigHat Biosciences’ Milliner platform,
        one on an anti-albumin nanobody (VHH) and one on an anti-mesothelin single-chain variable fragment
        (scFv), in which a research team and a general-purpose AI agent, Claude, brought scientific reasoning
        into the loop. Over seven rounds and about 1,010 design variants we built libraries from antibody lineage,
        structure and biophysics, and found from the raw curves that on each scaffold one thermal metric did not
        measure what its name implied. The libraries were built as controlled comparisons, so the results also show
        what each mutation contributes to each property. We fit a physical model of the curves, used it to choose
        metrics suited to each scaffold, and changed the experimental protocol, varying protein concentration and
        heating rate, to test the model against pre-registered predictions. Both leads improve on their parents in
        affinity, thermal stability and aggregation while improving or maintaining yield and purity.


1. Introduction

Therapeutic antibodies have transformed the treatment of cancer and autoimmune disease (Carter and
Lazar, 2018). An antibody drug candidate must be potent, manufacturable at scale, stable in storage, and
safe in patients. Lead optimization is the step that takes a therapeutic program from an identified starting
molecule to IND-enabling studies (i.e., the formal safety work required before a first trial in humans).
The longer and more expensive the lead optimization step is, the fewer clinical candidates a program has
to choose from, and ultimately the fewer programs are ready to proceed to the clinic. Because physical
experiments are slow and expensive, a lead-optimization campaign can make only a few hundred high-fidelity
measurements. Each experiment should therefore teach as much as possible, which rules out brute-force or
random search over the vast space of possible mutations. A popular alternative is a closed lab-in-the-loop
system, in which a model proposes molecules, the lab measures them, and the results retrain the model.
This formulation is widely shared, from machine-learning-guided directed evolution (Wu et al., 2019) to
Bayesian optimization and generative design of biomolecules (Romero et al., 2013; Stanton et al., 2022;
Khan et al., 2023; Gruver et al., 2023; Li et al., 2023; Rapp et al., 2024).
Every lab-in-the-loop system needs a formal specification of the molecule to produce, made up of objective
functions (i.e., reward functions) and constraint functions, metrics that say which molecules are admissible
and preferable for a program. Most of what a program cares about cannot be measured directly during lead
optimization, so the specification is written in terms of in vitro readouts that serve as proxies, for example
a thermal stability readout taken over a few hours as a proxy for years of shelf life. The specification is
usually chosen before the first round and then held fixed, so the whole campaign rests on the alignment
between these proxy metrics and the program’s true goals. How good that alignment is depends on how
each metric is made. An instrument does not report an intrinsic property of a molecule, it records a signal,
and an analysis turns that signal into a metric based on a set of assumptions. For example, to measure how
tightly an antibody binds its target, we use a sensor to follow the two coming together and letting go, and if
a simple physics model fits the resulting curve well, its parameters serve as binding metrics (Figure 1a).

To a lab-in-the-loop system, both of these metrics are simply labels to predict and improve. If one of them
turns out to be a poor proxy, neither a better predictive model nor more data will fix it, and the problem
can only be found by going back to the raw signal and the analysis that produced the metric. That means
inspecting individual instrument traces, understanding what the prior analysis assumed, and testing a
better model of the assay, which is usually the job of a biophysicist or experimental biologist who knows
both the instrument and the molecule. This is not the role typically envisioned for AI in lead optimization.
Even recent agentic campaigns have used general-purpose models chiefly to orchestrate specialized design
tools under a predefined protocol (Claude Science and Shanehsazzadeh, 2026), leaving what to measure
and how to read it to people. In this report we ask what a research team and a general-purpose AI agent
can accomplish when they work together on live optimization campaigns.
We report two lead-optimization campaigns run between June and September 2026 on BigHat Biosciences’
Milliner platform, which carried out every experiment, one on an anti-albumin VHH (the single variable
domain of a camelid heavy-chain antibody) and the second on an anti-mesothelin scFv (the two variable
domains of an antibody joined by a linker), over seven rounds and about 1,010 design variants (1,232
wells). A lab-in-the-loop campaign typically builds each library with specialized machine learning models,
trained on earlier rounds to propose the designs predicted to score best (Frey et al., 2025). In this work a
human researcher and Claude, working in Claude Code, took a different approach to both library design
and measurement. To design libraries they reasoned as antibody engineers would, tracing each parent’s
lineage through the patent and structural literature, running small experiments to find which positions
mattered, and proposing mutations that tested specific hypotheses about binding and stability. To interpret
the results they asked what developability means apart from any one assay, and which intrinsic properties
of the molecule the thermal metrics were meant to estimate. Reading the raw curves with that question
in mind, they found that on the VHH the aggregation onset sat near 40 °C regardless of the melt, while
on the scFv the reported melt marked the sample precipitating. They then built a physical model of the
curves, replaced those metrics with an aggregation rate on the VHH and a solution collapse temperature on
the scFv, and predicted how each metric should respond to changes in protein concentration and heating

specification as closely as possible (i.e., that minimize their divergence from it) in a small number of rounds.

From metrics to labels. The reported 𝑇𝑚    app
                                               is a summary statistic of the fluorescence signal, the steepest
point of a curve, while 𝑇𝑚 is a parameter of the physical model. The statistic estimates the parameter only
if the protein unfolds in two states, reversibly, and at equilibrium (Greenfield, 2006; Kotov et al., 2021).

with protein concentration or heating rate. An optimization loop sees neither the signal nor the physics,
only a label, which we write 𝑦𝑖 = 𝑓(𝑥𝑖 ; 𝜔) + 𝜖𝑖 for sequence 𝑥𝑖 under protocol 𝜔 (e.g., instrument, sample

different quantity, a problem known as misspecification. In lead optimization every design stays near 𝑥0 , so
the distribution of sequences changes relatively little from one round to the next. If a label reflects a stable
property of the sequence, a model trained on one round should therefore predict the next, and a failure to
do so suggests a problem with the label rather than with the model.


3. Related work

Model-based optimization. Machine learning entered protein engineering through directed evolution,
where models trained on measured variants choose what to test next (Romero et al., 2013; Wu et al.,
2019; Yang et al., 2019). Generative methods followed, both for de novo design (Dauparas et al., 2022;
Watson et al., 2023) and for optimization loops that pair a generative model with a discriminative model
that ranks variants (Stanton et al., 2022; Gruver et al., 2023). For antibodies, models have predicted
antigen specificity (Mason et al., 2021), proposed mutations from language models alone (Hie et al., 2024),
co-optimized affinity and non-specific binding (Makowski et al., 2022), and run a loop across four targets
and eleven lead antibodies (Frey et al., 2025). The model may not even be the main constraint, since
general-purpose language models can be trained to solve constrained biophysical sequence optimization
tasks that specialized solvers also solve, leaving the solver an advantage mainly in efficiency (Chen et al.,
2025). Objectives come from the program’s target candidate profile (i.e., the properties a candidate must
have, which can be revised as the program learns), and computational guidelines drawn from clinical-stage
antibodies offer one screen for developability (Raybould et al., 2019). Most of this work treats reported
assay values as correct labels. We also tested whether those values measured the intended property.
Agents in scientific campaigns. Recent work hands whole campaigns to general-purpose agents. Such
systems have designed nanobodies against SARS-CoV-2 (Swanson et al., 2025), generated and triaged
hypotheses across biomedical problems (Gottweis et al., 2026), executed diverse biomedical research tasks
(Huang et al., 2026), and automated the production of research reports (Lu et al., 2026). Closest to this work,
an agent given a written protocol and open-source design tools ran de novo binder design campaigns against
sixteen targets, with no human input during optimization (Claude Science and Shanehsazzadeh, 2026), and
a related system supplies campaign-level control for high-throughput binder design (Jeon et al., 2026). The
former leaves open questions, since the agent primarily orchestrates pre-existing tools, mini-proteins are
not yet a proven therapeutic protein modality, biologists wrote the protocol and configured the experiments,
and binding is only one of several properties that decide a discovery program. A recent position paper
argues that agents are not built for autonomous discovery, in part because problem selection depends
on human judgment and training corpora omit the tacit knowledge of laboratory practice, and endorses
agents as co-scientists, with a person retaining at least one of problem specification, experimental work
or interpretation (Bisht et al., 2026). Our campaigns fit that model. Researchers provided the molecule
specifications and ran the experiments, and the agent did much of the analysis and interpretation.
Developability metrics as surrogate objectives. Jain et al. (2017) ran a dozen biophysical assays on
137 antibodies in advanced clinical stages and found that the number of “red flags” a molecule carries
declines with advancement toward approval, consistent with selection for developability (i.e., whether a
molecule can be manufactured, stored and delivered as a drug). Such assays yield metrics that stand in
for the properties a program cares about, as melting temperature does in our campaigns. Clinical trials
face the same problem with surrogate endpoints (i.e., measurements that stand in for a clinical outcome).
The strictest criterion asks a surrogate to capture the whole relationship between the intervention and the
endpoint (Prentice, 1989), which is rarely verifiable from the data the surrogate was collected on (Buyse and
Molenberghs, 1998). In practice it is enough that effects on the surrogate predict effects on the endpoint,
but correlation alone does not establish this, because an intervention can move a surrogate through a
mechanism independent of the process of interest (Fleming and DeMets, 1996). Thermal stability assays are

4. Setup

Platform. Both campaigns ran on BigHat Biosciences’ automated Milliner platform, which expresses
variants cell-free (i.e., without growing cells) and measures binding, thermal stability, aggregation, purity
and yield at high throughput. All experimental and computational work sits in one system of record,
Reccy, which an agent can reach through a Model Context Protocol (MCP) connector, a standard interface
between a model and external tools. Raw instrument traces are stored beside the summary metrics and the
quality-control flags. The analysis in Section 6 relies on this access to the raw traces. Appendix A gives the
methods for each assay.
Campaigns. The VHH campaign started from Alb8, a 115-residue anti-albumin domain from Ablynx
(Ablynx, 2006) (Figure C1), and the scFv campaign from a 259-residue anti-mesothelin fragment derived
from Medarex clone 7B1 (Medarex, 2009), both supplied by BigHat with baseline measurements. Between
29 June and 4 September 2026 we ran four design rounds on the VHH and three on the scFv. Each round
tested 176 wells of design variants, and results typically returned in 9 to 10 days. The AI agent was Claude
in Claude Code, primarily Claude Fable 5, directed by S.S.
Specifications. The campaign specifications cover affinity, 𝑇𝑚app
                                                                  , 𝑇agg and yield (Table 1, which also shows
where each lead finished). The supplied baselines and the in-campaign parent measurements differ, most of
all for the scFv’s affinity, and Table 1 gives both.
Campaign journal. We kept an append-only journal of every design, analysis, finding and decision, with
an author on each record (387 records through the lead decisions). Predictions for the later design rounds
were written to file before the plates ran.


5. Designing by biophysical reasoning

In both campaigns we designed libraries as antibody engineers and biophysicists might, with each part
of a library testing a stated hypothesis about what a part of the molecule was doing. The agent proposed
hypotheses and mutations to test them, and the research team set the design strategy, reviewed the
designs and chose which went on each plate. Mechanistic reasoning is powerful but can slip into post-hoc
rationalization, since more than one mechanism can often explain a result. We therefore built libraries as
designed experiments and read them with a simple statistical model.

Substitutions as chemical questions. Amino acids differ in known ways, so a well-chosen substitution
asks a precise question about a position, and a scan repeats that question across a region. On the scFv
we asked whether eight tyrosines in CDR-H3 contributed through their aromatic ring or their hydroxyl,
replacing each with phenylalanine (ring, no hydroxyl) and with serine (hydroxyl, no ring). All 8 phenylalanine

chain facing an acidic patch on albumin. In both campaigns, alanine scans of the CDRs and the Vernier-zone

the sample falls out of solution (Section 6), and we conjecture that these help the variable domains stay

Combining modules and balancing trade-offs. Mutations that act through separate mechanisms can
be treated as modules and combined, so that a gain from one module offsets a cost from another. On the
scFv, reversions to germline that improved stability also cost binding, and in round 1 designs carrying
them as a module bound in 0 of 7 cases without G230Y and Y238A, against 14 of 15 with them (G230Y
tightened binding and Y238A offset its cost in aggregation). In the same round, a simple control combining

including added disulfide bonds that did not improve stability, and it became the starting point of the final
round. Further stabilizing mutations cost off-rate, so the scFv lead took two of the milder ones, T187Y and
V196A (Figure 3d, Appendix C.6).
Checking the reasoning. We tested these hypotheses with combinatorial libraries, in which each mutation
is either present or absent, so that each design is a point on a Boolean lattice. If only a few mutations carry
a function, a small sample of the lattice is enough to find them (Poelwijk et al., 2019). We fit these samples
with Bayesian linear regression in the Walsh basis, the Fourier basis of Boolean functions (Poelwijk et al.,
2016), which gives each mutation or combination of mutations its own coefficient and uncertainty (Appendix
C.3). On the scFv, a sample of 63 of the 256 combinations of the eight somatic mutations showed that only
three carried binding, and the effects at the other five were consistent with zero (Figure 3e). In the final
round, a full factorial (i.e., all combinations) of stabilizing mutations at four of those positions tested them
directly, and A182D, V196F and T187Y added 8.1, 4.2 and 2.0 °C of collapse temperature (Appendix C.6).
On the VHH, a full factorial over six mutations in the final round showed that E1A carried the off-rate gain,
N16G and T77N the melt, and E1A, S30R and V93R slowed aggregation, with effects that added (Figure 3c,
Appendix C.5). We tested computational tools the same way, and on the scFv co-folding scores did not
separate binders from decoys designed for another target (Appendix F.2). On the VHH, off-rate could be
predicted from one round to the next, while the reported thermal metrics could not (Section 6).

before the plates ran (Table E1). BigHat reconfigured the platform to run it, doubling the synthesis scale.


7. Results

Both leads improve on their parents in affinity, thermal stability and aggregation, hold expression and purity,
and pass the revised specifications (Table 1, Figure 6, Appendix H). We take each molecule in turn, from its
parent’s curves through the test to the lead.
Two processes on the VHH. As the VHH parent heats, two separate things happen (Figure 4b). From
about 40 °C the scattering climbs while the fluorescence barely changes, so particles are forming while
nearly every molecule is still folded. Only near 62 °C does the fluorescence shift, as the protein unfolds in a
real, reproducible melt. The melt is an equilibrium, so it depends only on the temperature the sample has
reached. The aggregation is kinetic, so the amount of protein aggregated depends on how long the sample
has been warm and how much protein is present.
In the final round the onset sat near 40 °C regardless of the melt (median 40.2 °C, 𝜌 = −0.11 with the melt,
𝑛 = 129 wells), while molecules with the same onset differed more than tenfold in 𝜅𝑁 . On a ramp, 𝑇agg is a
time disguised as a temperature, marking when the signal first leaves its baseline and not how fast it grows
afterward. It was a poor proxy for aggregation on this scaffold and could not rank designs. We therefore
used p𝑘agg for aggregation and the identified melt for stability.
In the test, the identified melt moved about 0.1 °C at half the concentration (half load) and at the faster
ramp, and p𝑘agg moved 0.03 log units at half load, as predicted (Figure 5c). The onset rose at the faster
ramp but moved the wrong way at half load (Figure 5d), so a replicated load series is needed.
The VHH lead carries eight mutations from Alb8 (16 V-gene differences from the human germline IGHV3-23,
against 13 for Alb8) and passes the original 𝐾𝐷 , melt and yield constraints, with its 𝑇agg right-censored
above the ramp maximum of 85 °C (Appendix D.3). Off-rate is 0.90 log slower than the parent’s on two wells,

The frontier. Each campaign ended with a set of trade-offs, and the leads are best read as points on that
frontier (Figure 6). We nominated the designs measured cleanly on every axis, which were not always the
designs that went furthest on one axis. On the scFv, one design keeps close to the parent’s affinity (3.3
nM against 3.7 nM) and collapses at 81.2 °C, 25 °C above the parent, on three wells. At the other end, a
design collapses at 77.0 °C with p𝑘off ≥ 4.3, on a single well. The lead (0.63 nM, 74.2 °C) sits just inside
this frontier, as one of two designs with 𝐾𝐷 measured inside the constraint on two wells, and the more
stable of the two, with the tighter replicates. On the VHH, the lead has the slowest resolved off-rate of
the round, the tightest design reads 0.85 nM on three wells (12 times tighter than the parent), one design
reaches a 75.7 °C melt at 2.3 nM on one well, and another holds a 70.6 °C melt at 1.2 nM with p𝑘agg ≥ 3.0.
The affinity end of the scFv frontier is a lower bound (the base design of Figure 3d reads 𝐾𝐷 ≤ 0.25 nM
on nine wells, about 15 times tighter than the parent), so that campaign reached at least this far, and the
long-window kinetics plate in Section 8 would resolve it. Both populations improved, with binder rates,
counting controls, of 48, 58, 93, 99% on the VHH and 47, 44, 93% on the scFv (Figure H2).


8. Discussion

A general-purpose agent, working with a team of researchers, optimized two antibody leads without relying
on a specialized design model, by reasoning about both the molecules and how they were being measured.
The agent proposed most of the mechanisms and analyses, while the research team set the overall design
strategy and the trade-off between exploration and exploitation, and the researcher working with the agent
reviewed designs and pressed it to read the raw curves and reason about the biophysics directly (Appendix
G). In the researcher’s account the agent was often hasty in analysis, leaned on models where reasoning
directly was available, and would not have stopped to look at the curves without prompting. Once prompted,



                                                       10
Reasoning in the loop: antibody lead optimization by probing mechanism and measurement



however, it read every curve on both scaffolds, built and compared candidate models, and registered its
predictions within days, work that would have taken the human researcher weeks.
This work points to a tension at the center of autonomous science. Optimization needs a fixed target,
but science needs the freedom to change the metrics of success. Machine learning for molecular design
usually takes the specification as an input and concentrates on search, while these campaigns treated it as
a working hypothesis about what each assay measures, to be tested as a campaign runs. Here that meant
asking which intrinsic property of the molecule each thermal metric was meant to estimate, and checking
the answer against the raw curves. A specification that can change can also drift toward whatever the data
favor, so each change needs a record of what changed and why, predictions written down in advance, and
a test that could fail. The journal and the perturbation plates served that purpose here, and any agent
revising its own objective will likely need the same.
Questioning a metric during a campaign depends on the lab as much as on the agent. Three properties
of BigHat’s platform made it possible: raw instrument traces stored beside every derived metric and
quality-control flag, a single system of record an agent could query directly, and protocols that could be
changed between rounds. As more experimental platforms open their raw data and protocols to agents, we
expect more assay development to move inside the campaign, and we believe that prospect justifies the
work of rebuilding experimental data infrastructure around AI agents.
Limits and next steps. The closest affinity competitors to the scFv lead sit at or beyond the BLI assay’s
limit of resolution, as do 51 of the 113 final-round scFv designs, so a long-window kinetics plate with
the shortlists and parents, or a more sensitive platform such as surface plasmon resonance, is the next
step for ranking them. Lead values rest on one or two replicates, and the VHH’s aggregation value is
one well, so confirmation runs of both leads come first. A melt that holds across concentrations does not
by itself rule out aggregation (Goyal et al., 2014), and an isothermal hold with size exclusion before and
after would test it directly. Stress testing and expression in a mammalian system would extend these
results toward development, and a condition that resolves the scFv’s unfolding midpoint would complete
its thermal picture. Size analysis of scFv samples held just below the collapse could also show whether
variable domains pair across molecules on the way to aggregation. Testing a small set of designs spanning
the range of p𝑘agg at high protein concentration would show whether the metric can serve as a low-material
screen for high-concentration behavior.
Looking forward, two views of AI in molecular design now sit side by side. In one, a general-purpose model
orchestrates specialized tools that do the design, and in the other, taken in these campaigns, the general
model reasons about the molecule directly. We see the choice between them as a question of resources
more than capability. Scaling a general model, extending a specialized one and generating data in the lab
are all ways of turning compute and skilled labor into better molecules, and the right mix depends on what
a team has and the value of the application. Specialized models keep their own advantage, since they are
modular and can be tuned to a particular data distribution far faster than a frontier model can change,
and for a valuable enough problem that speed should justify the investment. Agents also shift this balance
by supplying the labor custom models require, so models with likelihoods crafted for a specific assay and
dataset once again become practical, as the censored regressions and physical models in these campaigns
suggest. In a world with abundant intelligence, we can afford to investigate nature more deeply than ever
before.

A.3 Assay methods. BigHat ran every assay, and the protocols below are theirs.
Binding. BLI measurements were made on Sartorius Octet instruments. For the VHH molecules, target
protein was biotinylated in-house, checked by QC, and captured on streptavidin tips, and select anti-albumin

against a commercial His-tagged antigen. Assays used tip-loading thresholds, positive and end-to-end
controls, and buffer-blank referencing, with double referencing in some runs. Fitting parameters were
fixed in the method protocols at project start. Kinetics data were uploaded to Reccy and passed through
BigHat’s QC pipelines, and protocol deviations were flagged in the data.
Yield. Protein yield was assessed by measuring absorbance at 260nm and 280nm using a SpectraMax iD5
spectrophotometer. Sequence-derived molar extinction coefficients and volume-defined path lengths were
then combined to determine protein concentration using the Beer-Lambert Law.
Assembly. Capillary electrophoresis was performed to assess protein assembly on the Revvity LabChip GX
II Touch microfluidic capillary electrophoresis SDS system. Samples were loaded into a HT Protein Express
LabChip according to the manufacturer’s protocol. A manufacturer-provided protein ladder was used to
benchmark protein size and validate protein assembly.

system from 25 °C to 85 °C using a thermal ramp rate of 0.3 °C/min. To determine melting temperature

Appendix C. Library design and the lattice method

C.1 Lineage. The agent identified both parents on the first day. The VHH matched Ablynx’s Alb8 at all
115 positions, which brought with it a patent family, a published successor set of six framework and CDR1
substitutions (Alb23; Ablynx, 2012), and a crystal structure of a family member’s binding mode (PDB 8OI2;
De Felice et al., 2024). The scFv was traced through a patent search to Medarex clone 7B1, from which it
differs at three CDR-H3 positions, four VH framework positions and one VL framework position, with no
structure available. Positions are numbered sequentially along each expressed construct (the scFv runs
VL, linker, VH). CDRs follow the Kabat definition, except that VHH CDR1 spans residues 26–35 to include
the Chothia loop. The scFv’s human germlines were assigned with ANARCI (Dunbar and Deane, 2016),
and IGHV3-23 is the human reference for the VHH. Both parents came from lineages whose affinity had
been engineered once already, and both bound more weakly than the best members of those lineages;
the agent treated the lineage as the first library: on the VHH the six Alb23 substitutions and six other
family members (Alb1, Alb3–6 and Alb10; Ablynx, 2006) went on the round-0 plate as single and combined
variants; on the scFv the three CDR-H3 differences from 7B1 became a five-well ancestral-reversion block.
Every well in the VHH lineage blocks bound, the best of those six read 1.42 nM in round 0 against a parent
in the 10 nM range, and the largest single affinity lever of the campaign, E1A, came from Alb1. All five
ancestral-reversion wells on the scFv bound, three of them beyond the assay’s dissociation floor, and one
reversion, G230Y, took the parent beyond that floor in every subsequent round. The harder problem was
thermal: zero of 176 scFv round-0 designs reached the reported 𝑇agg constraint of 65 °C, and on the VHH
the two thermal constraints at 70 °C sat about 8 °C above the parent’s melting temperature and 25–30 °C
above its aggregation onset. An 8 °C gain in melting temperature is a large ask for a 115-residue domain; a
25–30 °C gain in aggregation onset is larger than any gain we found published for this scaffold, and the
size of that gap was the first sign that the 𝑇agg metric was not reporting the fold (Appendix D).
C.2 Blocks. Each plate was assembled from four kinds of block (Figure C2). Anchors are known molecules,
lineage members, literature variants and the parent, that serve as centroids around which to explore, and
as the reference wells every other measurement on the plate is compared against. Scans change one
position at a time over a region, so that every well is a main effect on the parent background; the round-0
alanine scans of the scFv CDRs found two packing repairs, Q91A in CDR-L3 and S245A at the VH J-stem,
each worth 6–7 °C on the collapse temperature as a fitted module effect (single scan wells read 5.3–7.7
°C), and a tyrosine-to-phenylalanine scan established that the paratope needed an aromatic ring but not a
hydroxyl at certain positions (8 of 8 binders against 1 of 8 for tyrosine-to-serine). Hypothesis blocks test a
stated hypothesis in a few wells: a two-well control that combined the scFv’s affinity reversions with its two
packing repairs, later called P, beat every hypothesis-designed variant in round 1 and became the putative
lead. Lattice samples and factorials sample the combinatorial space over a set of mutations and are read by
the regression of C.3. Round 0 of each campaign was mostly scans; the VHH’s rounds 1–3 were mostly

Appendix E. The designed perturbation test

E.1 Why the protocol had to change. A model of the traces makes predictions that the standard protocol
cannot test, because every capillary is run at one concentration and one ramp rate. The model of Appendix
D says which metrics are thermodynamic and which are kinetic. A thermodynamic midpoint does not
depend on how much protein is in the capillary or how fast it is heated; an aggregation rate is first order in
concentration, so 𝜅𝑁 halves at half load and 𝑘agg = 𝜅𝑁 /𝑐 does not move; and a kinetic collapse, a threshold
crossed on a ramp, moves later at lower concentration and at a faster ramp. On the VHH that separates the
melt (invariant) from the scattering onset (moves). On the scFv it says that every thermal metric should
move, by predictable amounts, and that the reported 𝑇𝑚  app
                                                            , if it were a melt, should not.
E.2 What was run. For the final round of each campaign the agent proposed running each design at two
loadings (neat and one-half) on one plate and at two ramp rates on the other, as designed perturbations.
BigHat proposed a slower second ramp; the agent asked for a faster one, 1.0 °C/min, on the reasoning
that a fast ramp outruns aggregation and might expose the unfolding transition before the collapse, and
BigHat agreed. An isothermal hold was deferred to mammalian material because cell-free material is below
1 mg/mL, and a one-quarter loading was too dilute to run. The synthesis scale was doubled to supply the
extra capillaries. A protocol record (2026-08-12) and a prediction file (2026-08-19) wrote down, before the
plates ran, which metrics should move, which should not, and by how much (Table E1).

Appendix F. Specialized models in full

F.1 The test. The agent ran specialized models inside the campaigns, against pre-registered predictions.
Every score below is prospective unless marked: trained on earlier rounds, scored on the next round’s data
against a prediction file written before the plate ran. Rank correlations are Spearman ρ with 90% bootstrap
intervals; Table F1 gives the register.
F.2 Structure and sequence models. Structure co-folding (ESMFold2, Candido et al., 2026; Protenix,
ByteDance AML AI4Science Team, 2025; Boltz-2, Passaro et al., 2025) did not separate binders from
non-binders on either scaffold: on the VHH two of three models ranked binders below non-binders, on the
scFv the interface confidence (ipTM; Evans et al., 2021) gave an AUROC of 0.13 against decoys designed
for another target, and the four tightest scFv binders on the plate had the lowest median ipTM of any
class. An in-sample binder-versus-non-binder AUROC of 0.72 at a wider mesothelin crop fell to 0.48 on
the next two rounds. The scores tracked distance from the parent. ProteinMPNN (Dauparas et al., 2022)
redesigns of the scFv CDR-H3 gave three non-binders in three wells, and its per-position preferences were
right at two positions and wrong at two, including a probability of 1.00 for the glycine whose reversion,
G230Y, is the campaign’s largest affinity lever. Antibody language-model naturalness (IgBert; Kenlay et al.,
2024) was anti-correlated with binding on the scFv (AUROC 0.28 over 359 designs, retrospective, masked
marginals on the parent), because the most natural sequences on the plate are germline reversions that do
not bind; language-model embeddings did no better than one-hot encodings. Published thermostability
predictors were checked against their own validation and rejected; the one run, NanoMelt (Ramon et al.,
2025), dropped nothing from 89 designs and predicted +6.5 °C where +1 °C was measured.
F.3 Discriminative models. Two discriminative models were carried through both campaigns: an
observation-only Gaussian process on a sequence kernel, and a 16,000-parameter convolutional network
trained as a five-seed ensemble (Figure F1a, b). The GP transferred off-rate rank across rounds at ρ 0.59
(DR0 → DR1, n = 74) and 0.54 (DR0+DR1 → DR2, n = 131) on the VHH with calibrated 95% intervals
(coverage 0.96 and 0.99), against 0.10 for counting mutations from the parent; a Tobit-EM variant that won
in-round cross-validation was the worse arm prospectively (coverage 0.45, bias −0.9 log) and was retired.
The CNN matched the GP on off-rate (0.60 and 0.56) and beat it on the scFv collapse temperature, 0.67
[0.55, 0.77] against 0.45 [0.31, 0.59] (n = 116). On the VHH DR3 designs never measured before, scored

generative proposal model was trained. The correlation between predicted and realized utility inside the
selected set was ρ −0.04 [−0.22, +0.15] (n = 84; Figure F1d), and neither model alone did better (−0.10
and +0.01); the top and bottom halves by predicted utility were indistinguishable on every objective. The
set as a whole was good, at the 0.95 quantile of everything measured before, and the record attributes
that to the anchor frame and the additive levers moving the block, which the models had learned, rather
than to the selection within it, which they had not. The lattice sample is the set on which the models had
the most data and the tightest design, so it is the fairest test the campaigns offer; a composite of three
weak, mutually anti-correlated rankers would be difficult for any acquisition function. This is not the full
LaMBO-2 loop (Gruver et al., 2023), which also trains a guided generative model to propose candidates; it
is the discriminative and acquisition half of it.

Appendix I. Limits in full

These are the limits the lead decisions record, with the reviews’ corrections, and the limits of the report’s
argument.
I.1 The assay floor. A single 100 nM BLI injection with a 1:1 fit resolves an off-rate only if the signal
falls by more than about 5% over the dissociation window; slower off-rates are recorded as a bound at the
window’s floor (Figure I1c). The VHH’s window was 899 s in every round, so off-rates slower than p𝑘off
4.24 are unresolved; no measured DR3 design exceeds 3.39, and the lead’s 𝐾𝐷 margin over the constraint
is 0.14 log (Figure I1a). BigHat offered a longer dissociation window in July, and the 2400 s window in
the protocol record of 2026-08-12 was run on the scFv’s final round. There 51 of 113 designs, P among
them, are at its floor, reported as p𝑘off 4.3 (the 5% rule alone would give 4.67; the campaign’s value is
the more conservative bound of record); their ordering replicates only weakly across plates (over the 17
designs at the floor on both plates, Spearman +0.23 on the measured signal drop and +0.53 on the fitter’s
extrapolated off-rates), and the lead was chosen over P because it is measured (Figure I1b). The earlier
rounds’ floor of 5.0 is the Octet fitter’s own limit, not a window rule. A single long-window plate with the
shortlists and parents on it would settle both rankings. The VHH DR3 run also carries an unresolved offset

I.3 Next experiments. The campaigns measured cell-free material on the standard assay set. The next
experiments are stress testing (hold, freeze-thaw and heat), HIC, a polyreactivity panel, DSC, charge-variant
analysis and expression in a mammalian system. Polyreactivity is of particular interest for the VHH lead,
since its mutations add about five positive charges (E1A, S30R, S31R, V93R, S103R) and positive charge

midpoint would complete its thermal picture. The parent VHH is on an older codon set than the designs, so
a matched comparison would use one codon set. Confirmation runs of both leads would test decisions made
on the data as measured.
I.4 Limits of the argument. The comparison between designed blocks and learned acquisition (Appendix
F) is within one campaign. We ran no comparison library designed another way, so we cannot say how
much of the gain came from the selection. The division of labor (Section 8, Appendix G) rests on a journal
that records what was written down, and the researcher’s habitual corrections are his account. And two
scaffolds on one platform with one partner is the whole sample.
