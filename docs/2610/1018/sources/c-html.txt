Abstract

How can we build an agent that thinks beyond human language, yet still acts through it in the external environment? Modern LLM agents still reason and interact through text, while latent reasoning offers an efficient internal medium but lacks native tool access and reasoning transparency.
We bridge these two directions with Hybrid Reasoning Agents (HybridAgents), which dynamically navigate between latent reasoning and text-based interaction throughout a unified generation process.
Architecturally, a lightweight HybridLink enables seamless transitions between text and latent modes, while the agent’s continuous working memory preserves lossless hybrid context across multiple turns.
These designs preserve tool-integrated agentic capabilities while making latent thoughts directly inspectable.
Building on this architecture, we further develop dedicated post-training algorithms for hybrid reasoning: HybridSFT stabilizes tool-integrated hybrid generation by teaching the backbone to coordinate with HybridLink, then HybridRL scales reasoning by learning when to think in latent space and when to act through text.
We evaluate HybridAgent at 3 model scales across 11 benchmarks covering mathematics, science, medicine, search, and agentic reasoning. Compared with frontier LLM Agents and agentic frameworks spanning both text-based and latent methods, HybridAgent consistently improves performance by 10.4%, while delivering 35.5%–54.7% token reduction, 1.8×\times–2.6×\times faster inference, and 2.7×\times–3.7×\times cost savings.

1 Introduction

Frontier intelligence no longer scales through the model’s reasoning alone, but depends increasingly on its ability to interact with the external world (Team, 2026 [#bib.bib44]; Kwa et al., 2026 [#bib.bib21]; Sun et al., 2026 [#bib.bib14]).
To solve a hard problem, an LLM agent must reach beyond the model itself by continually invoking specialized tools, observing feedback from the environment, and adapting its next move (Qu et al., 2025 [#bib.bib17]; Silver and Sutton, 2025 [#bib.bib20]; Feng et al., 2026 [#bib.bib19]).
Beneath this expanding repertoire of agent harnesses lies a single lingua franca: text, the common medium of both the agent’s autoregressive generation and its environment interaction (Sumers et al., 2024 [#bib.bib18]; Zhang et al., 2026b [#bib.bib16]).
All intermediate reasoning trajectories are generated token by token as text before the agent can act on the environment.

A separate line of work on latent reasoning points in a different direction (Zhu et al., 2025b [#bib.bib13]; Chen et al., 2025 [#bib.bib31]).
By unfolding computation directly in continuous hidden space, latent models bypass the need to decode and verbalize every intermediate step, providing more efficient internal reasoning within the model’s “own language" (Hao et al., 2024 [#bib.bib28]; Wang et al., 2025 [#bib.bib40]; Yu et al., 2026 [#bib.bib41]).
However, today’s latent reasoners remain largely sealed behind the model boundary.
Unlike decoded text, continuous latent vectors (i) lack native pathways for tool invocation across multi-turn agentic reasoning (Chang et al., 2026 [#bib.bib22]), and (ii) do not readily expose human-interpretable semantics, leaving them difficult to supervise during training (Chen et al., 2026 [#bib.bib15]) and to audit or debug at inference (Korbak et al., 2025 [#bib.bib24]).
These constraints keep latent reasoning models from operating as capable agents in their own right.

Taken together, agentic interaction and efficient inner reasoning have so far required a different medium each, leaving the two lines of work apart in the development of next-generation agents.
We believe this division can be closed by building a new type of agent that combines both strengths to think beyond text while still acting through it. We call this unified design HybridAgent, one that dynamically navigates between latent reasoning and text-based interaction within a single generation.

Building such an agent is hard, since it needs to handle both dense continuous vectors and sparse discrete tokens as hybrid inputs, and determine when to transition between text and latent modes to balance agentic actions and reasoning efficiency.
To offset the hardness, we first revisit the model architecture and extend its autoregressive generation interface to support hybrid inputs.
At the heart of this design is HybridLink, a lightweight residual adapter that bridges latent thoughts from the last-layer hidden space back into the input embedding space and enables sequence-wise recurrence within latent mode.
Then, we realize multi-turn hybrid generation through the agent’s own working memory, carrying complete hybrid trajectories of text, latent thoughts, and tool observations across turns.
Surprisingly, we find that HybridAgent can render its thoughts in latent mode as human-readable pseudo-text, making latent reasoning transparent and directly inspectable rather than opaque.

To further unlock the potential of hybrid reasoning, we equip HybridAgent with dedicated post-training algorithms.
HybridSFT first stabilizes tool-integrated hybrid generation through activation and self-conditioned training, which adapts the backbone to reason over latent thoughts produced by its own HybridLink, enabling reliable coordination between the two components and closing the train-inference mismatch.
Then, HybridRL scales agentic reasoning capabilities by training HybridAgent to adaptively balance latent reasoning and text-based interaction. To realize this, we enable policy optimization over hybrid trajectories through hybrid policy factorization and exact rollout replay.

We provide theoretical grounding for HybridAgent through complementary analyses of multi-turn context continuation and hybrid policy optimization. From an architectural standpoint, we show that continuous working memory losslessly preserves the entire hybrid context across different turns. From a learning perspective, the hybrid policy factorization and exact latent-context replay in HybridRL provide a well-founded basis for policy updates over complete hybrid trajectories.

Empirically, we evaluate HybridAgent on 11 benchmarks spanning mathematics, science, medicine, knowledge-intensive search, and challenging agentic reasoning.
We instantiate and train HybridAgent with Qwen3-Base models (Yang et al., 2025a [#bib.bib29]) across 4B, 8B, and 14B scales.
Compared with frontier LLM agents and agentic frameworks spanning both text-based and latent reasoning, HybridAgent achieves an average performance improvement of 10.4% and exhibits strong test-time scalability. In terms of efficiency, HybridAgent reduces token usage by 35.5%–54.7%, delivers 1.8×\times–2.6×\times inference speedup, and lowers computational cost by 2.7×\times–3.7×\times.
Additional in-depth analyses of training dynamics, reasoning auditability, and agentic tool-use reliability reveal a clear division of labor between the two reasoning modes: HybridAgent shifts more internal reasoning into semantically rich and interpretable latent thoughts, while reserving text for external inspection and tool interaction.

3 Building a Hybrid Reasoning Agent

We introduce HybridAgent, a hybrid framework that dynamically navigates between explicit text and continuous latent reasoning within a single unified generation process.
As illustrated in Figure 1 [#S0.F1], the architecture preserves an LLM agent’s autoregressive generation and tool-calling interfaces while accommodating both text and latent inputs.
Below, we first present the hybrid interface for seamless text-latent mode transition (Section 3.1 [#S3.SS1]), followed by multi-turn hybrid generation (Section 3.2 [#S3.SS2]).

3.1 Hybrid Mode Interface

Existing latent reasoning models typically feed the last-layer hidden state hth_{t} directly back as the next-step input et+1e_{t+1}.
This design is not well suited to hybrid generation, as substituting hth_{t} for a token embedding creates a representation mismatch with the pretrained input space, making transitions between continuous latent states and discrete tokens difficult.
To support both latent and text modes in a shared input interface, we add a lightweight adapter HybridLink to work alongside the backbone.

HybridLink as a Bridge. An LLM agent’s last-layer hidden states naturally encode the semantics of its current generation, so the HybridLink ℛ\mathcal{R} is designed to carry this information from the hidden representation space back into the input embedding space.
Formally, given any last-layer hidden state hth_{t}, HybridLink simulates its corresponding next-step input embedding as:

et+1=ℛ⁡(ht)=ht+W2​σ​(W1​ht),\displaystyle e_{t+1}=\mathcal{R}(h_{t})=h_{t}+W_{2}\,\sigma\!\left(W_{1}h_{t}\right),

(4)

where W1,W2W_{1},W_{2} are standard linear projections, σ\sigma is an activation function (e.g., GELU).
As the residual connection largely preserves the original semantics of hth_{t}, HybridLink only needs to align the distributional difference rather than learn the entire projection from scratch.
Accordingly, the link module is lightweight, with approximately 2​dh22d_{h}^{2} parameters (e.g., ∼\sim10M parameters for a 4B LLM).

Dynamic Text-Latent Mode Transition.
Next, we describe how HybridAgent transitions between text and latent modes during generation, together with the entry and exit decisions for each mode.

In text mode, HybridAgent behaves exactly as a standard LLM, sampling the next token from the vocabulary at each step. The process continues until the agent generates one special token <latent>, which serves as the entry anchor for the latent mode. For the next forward pass, the agent uses HybridLink for latent reasoning in place of the LM head and token embedding layer.

In latent mode, each step’s last-layer hidden state is passed through HybridLink via Eq. 4 [#S3.E4], allowing HybridAgent to continue its generation flow entirely in latent space without token decoding and re-embedding.
This sequence-wise recurrent computation over continuous representations extends the agent’s effective reasoning depth, enabling more expressive internal reasoning.
The latent reasoning span terminates when the semantics of the last-layer hidden state hth_{t} align with the exit anchor </latent>. Specifically, at any latent step tt, the latent mode closes by satisfying the condition:

arg​maxv∈𝒱cos(ℛ(ht),Emb(v))=</latent>.\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\;\cos\bigl(\mathcal{R}(h_{t}),\,\operatorname{Emb}(v)\bigr)=\texttt{</latent>}.

(5)

HybridAgent then returns to its text mode, and this transition continues during generation.
At each step, the agent adaptively decides whether to remain in its current mode or switch, allowing the length and frequency of text and latent spans to emerge dynamically rather than follow preset configurations.

Interpretable Latent Mode. During hybrid generation, a useful property of HybridAgent is that its latent mode remains decoding-free yet directly interpretable.
To inspect any latent reasoning span, one can easily recover its semantics by mapping each latent step produced by HybridLink to its nearest token embedding, as in Eq. 5 [#S3.E5]. We illustrate this interpretability property clearly in Figure 2 [#S3.F2].

3.2 Multi-Turn Hybrid Generation over Working Memory

In standard LLM agents, each turn’s generated content and tool outputs are appended to the textual context and re-encoded as the next turn’s input prefix.
Hybrid trajectories, however, interleave text tokens with latent vectors and can’t be reconstructed through conventional tokenization alone. Instead, HybridAgent carries the complete context across turns through its continuous working memory.

Figure 3: Multi-Turn Hybrid Generation over HybridAgent’s Own Working Memory. HybridAgent accumulates key-value (KV) entries from text, latent thoughts, and tool observations in continuous working memory, directly reusing and updating it across turns to preserve the full hybrid context.

Cross-Turn Working Memory.
Recall that a single-turn hybrid trajectory τ\tau in Eq. 3 [#S2.E3] consists of model-generated text, latent thoughts, and external tool interactions.
During input construction, all contents are mapped into the same input space through either the input embedding layer or HybridLink.
This shared space allows the Transformer to process all three components uniformly and collect their KV entries throughout generation.
We leverage these accumulated KV entries as the HybridAgent’s continuous working memory to achieve multi-turn hybrid generation.

As illustrated in Figure 3 [#S3.F3], HybridAgent first initializes its working memory ℳ\mathcal{M} from the input context xx and then accumulates the KV entries generated throughout the first-turn trajectory τ(1)\tau^{(1)}, i.e.,

ℳ(0)\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{(0)}
=[K≤|x|,V≤|x|],\displaystyle=\bigl[K_{\leq|x|},\,V_{\leq|x|}\bigr],

(6)

ℳ(1)\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{(1)}
=ℳ(0)⊕[K≤t,V≤t],t=1,…,|τ(1)|.\displaystyle=\mathcal{M}^{(0)}\oplus\bigl[K_{\leq t},V_{\leq t}\bigr],\quad t=1,\dots,|\tau^{(1)}|.

Here, ℳ(1)\mathcal{M}^{(1)} preserves the complete first-turn hybrid context in positional order across all Transformer layers.
For each subsequent turn, the agent directly resumes generation from the current working memory and updates it by appending newly generated KV entries at each step.
This process continues across the sequence of hybrid trajectories {τ(1),τ(2),…,τ(n)}\{\tau^{(1)},\tau^{(2)},\dots,\tau^{(n)}\} until the final answer is produced.
We further provide a formal justification for this continuation process in Appendix A [#A1].
Overall, multi-turn hybrid generation proceeds over one continuous working memory flow, preserving the complete hybrid context across turns without requiring intermediate token-space reconstruction.

4 Learning to Perform Hybrid Reasoning

With the overall framework in place, we now turn to training.
As HybridAgent leaves the backbone architecture intact, no pre-training from scratch is required. Instead, we adapt the standard agentic post-training pipeline of supervised fine-tuning and reinforcement learning to the hybrid setting. The two training stages serve complementary roles: SFT teaches the agent to coordinate with HybridLink and instantiate reliable tool use for stable hybrid generation (Section 4.1 [#S4.SS1]), while RL further scales reasoning by learning when to think in latent space and when to act through text (Section 4.2 [#S4.SS2]).

4.1 Stabilize Generation via Hybrid Supervised Fine-tuning

As a first step toward stabilizing hybrid reasoning in HybridAgent, we design the entire HybridSFT stage through a hybrid data curation pipeline and a staged training procedure.

Hybrid Data Recipe. To curate high-quality training data, we filter existing text-based agentic SFT datasets from multiple sources and convert them into hybrid trajectories through automatic annotation.
We assign reasoning steps to latent mode based on both LLM Experts’ judgments and target models’ own prediction entropy, while retaining a small set of key steps for tool interactions and external debugging/auditing in text mode (see Appendix E [#A5] for data curation details).

Format and HybridLink Activation.
As a warm start, we adapt the backbone to the hybrid data format and initialize the HybridLink.
For format activation, we perform standard text SFT to teach the backbone when to enter and exit latent mode by emitting the two special anchor tokens.
We then freeze the backbone and train HybridLink to align last-layer hidden states at latent positions with their target input embeddings. For a training instance (x,y)∈𝒟SFT(x,y)\in\mathcal{D}_{\text{SFT}}, the activation objectives are:

ℒBackbone=CE⁡(fθ​(x),y),\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{Backbone}}=\mathrm{CE}\bigl(f_{\theta}(x),\,y\bigr),

(7)

ℒHybridLink =λcos(1−cos(ℛ(hlat),Emb(ylat)))+λmse∥ℛ(hlat)−Emb(ylat)∥22,\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{HybridLink }}=\lambda_{\cos}\Bigl(1-\cos\bigl(\mathcal{R}(h_{\text{lat}}),\,\operatorname{Emb}(y_{\text{lat}})\bigr)\Bigr)+\lambda_{\mathrm{mse}}\bigl\lVert\mathcal{R}(h_{\text{lat}})-\operatorname{Emb}(y_{\text{lat}})\bigr\rVert_{2}^{2},

where hlath_{\mathrm{lat}} is the last-layer hidden states at latent positions, ylaty_{\mathrm{lat}} is the corresponding ground-truth text tokens, and λcos,λmse\lambda_{\cos},\lambda_{\mathrm{mse}} balance directional and magnitude alignment, respectively.

Self-Conditioned HybridSFT.
The activation provides a well-aligned initialization, but the backbone has not yet learned to reason over latent thoughts produced by its own HybridLink (see Appendix B.1 [#A2.SS1] for details on this train-inference mismatch).
We therefore adopt a two-pass self-conditioned training scheme as illustrated in Figure 4 [#S4.F4].
In the first pass, HybridAgent is teacher-forced on the ground-truth hybrid sequence. HybridLink maps the generated last-layer hidden states at latent positions one step ahead to form latent inputs for the next pass.
In the second pass, HybridAgent processes the same sequence but replaces the ground-truth latent embeddings with its own latent inputs. We jointly supervise text and latent positions on the second pass as:

ℒHybridSFT=ℒBackbone+ℒHybridLink ,\mathcal{L}_{\text{HybridSFT}}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{Backbone}}+\mathcal{L}_{\text{HybridLink }},

(8)

where the CE loss ℒBackbone\mathcal{L}_{\text{Backbone}} and regression loss ℒHybridLink \mathcal{L}_{\text{HybridLink }} are applied only at text and latent positions, respectively.
Overall, HybridSFT trains the agent to coordinate its backbone with HybridLink for coherent reasoning across text and latent modes, supporting stable hybrid generation at inference.

A HybridAgent cannot straightforwardly adopt popular RL algorithms such as PPO (Schulman et al., 2017 [#bib.bib27]) or GRPO (Shao et al., 2024 [#bib.bib7]), as they assume every generated position carries a token-level likelihood.
Specifically, at each position tt, standard RL computes the per-token importance ratio as:

ρt=πθ​(yt∣y<t)πθold​(yt∣y<t),\displaystyle\rho_{t}=\frac{\pi_{\theta}\bigl(y_{t}\mid y_{<t}\bigr)}{\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}\bigl(y_{t}\mid y_{<t}\bigr)},

(9)

where πθ,πθold\pi_{\theta},\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}} denote the current and rollout policies.
At latent positions, however, HybridAgent generates continuous vectors rather than sampled tokens, leaving no token-level likelihood. Thus, the importance ratio ρt\rho_{t} is undefined.
We address this incompatibility with two simple components.

Hybrid Policy Factorization.
Recall that, in a hybrid trajectory, latent thoughts are computed entirely in the continuous latent space through HybridLink, and tool responses are the masked observations from the environment.
Thus, policy sampling during rollout is confined to model-generated text positions.
To justify why policy optimization needs only to operate over these sampled positions, we formalize the hybrid policy factorization below with detailed theoretical analyses.

Proposition 4.1 (Hybrid Policy Factorization).

Let τ\tau be a hybrid trajectory generated by the policy πθ\pi_{\theta} (induced by fθf_{\theta}) with fixed HybridLink ℛ\mathcal{R}. Then the hybrid policy factorizes as:

πθ​(τ∣x)=∏t∈𝒯πθ​(yt∣x,τ<t),\displaystyle\pi_{\theta}(\tau\mid x)\;=\;\prod_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\pi_{\theta}\bigl(y_{t}\mid x,\,\tau_{<t}\bigr),

(10)

where 𝒯\mathcal{T} is the set of sampled text positions of trajectory τ\tau.

The proof and additional theoretical justifications are provided in Appendix C [#A3].
Proposition 4.1 [#S4.Thmtheorem1] shows that likelihood-ratio updates over sampled text positions are sufficient to optimize the full hybrid trajectory, making the importance ratio in Eq. 9 [#S4.E9] well-defined for the HybridAgent policy.

Policy Update via Exact Rollout Replay.
Policy optimization generally involves rebuilding a differentiable computation graph for the rollouts.
Applying this to HybridAgent further requires recomputing sampled text-token likelihoods conditioned on the same preceding latent thoughts used during rollout.
We satisfy this requirement via exact latent-context replay.
During hybrid rollout, we record latent thoughts alongside sampled text tokens and tool observations.
During policy updates, we replay the full hybrid trajectory in embedding space with a single differentiable forward pass, keeping the recorded latent inputs unchanged while recomputing the sampled likelihoods in Eq. 10 [#S4.E10].

Algorithm 1 [#alg1] outlines the full HybridRL training procedure. We keep the HybridLink fixed to preserve deterministic latent transitions.
For the reward design, we simply formulate it as:

r⁡(τ)=rcorrect+rformat+rtool,r(\tau)=r_{\mathrm{correct}}+r_{\mathrm{format}}+r_{\mathrm{tool}},

(11)

where rcorrectr_{\mathrm{correct}} measures answer correctness, rformatr_{\mathrm{format}} penalizes invalid formatting, and rtoolr_{\mathrm{tool}} encourages multi-tool use.
We impose no explicit reward for mode usage, allowing HybridAgent to adaptively balance text and latent generation and discover effective hybrid reasoning strategies during RL.

For baseline comparisons, we evaluate HybridAgent against

(ii) Latent Reasoning Methods, including SoftThinking (Zhang et al., 2025 [#bib.bib2]), Coconut (Hao et al., 2024 [#bib.bib28]), and SoftCoT (Xu et al., 2025b [#bib.bib6]);
(iii) Frontier LLM Agents, including GPT-5.6-Luna (OpenAI, 2026 [#bib.bib48]), Kimi-K3 (Team, 2026 [#bib.bib44]), GLM-5.3-Flash (GLM-5-Team and others, 2026 [#bib.bib47]), DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash (DeepSeek-AI, 2026 [#bib.bib46]), QwQ-32B (Team, 2025 [#bib.bib45]);

Additional baseline details are provided in Appendix H.2 [#A8.SS2].

Models and Training Setups. We instantiate HybridAgent with Qwen3-Base backbones (Yang et al., 2025a [#bib.bib29]) at three scales of 4B, 8B, and 14B. We then apply HybridSFT with 54K hybrid trajectories curated through our automatic annotation pipeline, followed by HybridRL with 12K deep reasoning examples (Yu et al., 2025 [#bib.bib10]) and 1K agentic search examples (Dong et al., 2025b [#bib.bib43]). During RL, we set rollout batches of 512 trajectories (64×864\times 8), followed by one optimization pass over 8 minibatches.

Implementation Details. HybridAgent interacts with external environments primarily through two types of tools: a Python interpreter within sandbox environments and a live search engine (e.g., Tavily). We cap tool usage at 10 calls per trajectory and retrieve the top 10 snippets for each search query.
As current serving engines do not support multi-turn hybrid generation,
we extend the training and inference infrastructure to enable text, latent, and tool-calling transitions and persistent KV working-memory transfer.
RL rollouts use a sampling temperature of 1.0, and evaluation uses temperature 0.6, top-p 0.95, and top-k 20.
We conduct experiments on H100 GPUs using five independent seeds.
Additional training/inference details and hyperparameter setups are provided in Appendix H.3 [#A8.SS3].

5.1 Scaling Performance via Hybrid Reasoning

Main Results.
Tables 1 [#S5.T1] and 2 [#S5.T2] report the overall performance of HybridAgent across 11 benchmarks spanning math, science, medicine, and knowledge search domains.
On challenging agentic reasoning benchmarks, HybridAgent delivers competitive performance over larger frontier LLM agents and specialized tool-use frameworks, with HybridAgent (14B) improving over the strongest agentic baseline by an average of 17.4%.

Together, HybridAgent combines the strengths of text-based agentic tool use with faster and more expressive latent reasoning, allowing the agent to interact externally while achieving deeper thinking depth internally.

5.2 Efficiency Advantage on Hybrid Generation

Beyond performance effectiveness, a key architectural advantage of HybridAgent is that latent mode enables efficient generation without costly explicit token decoding.
We evaluate this property empirically along three complementary dimensions: (i) token throughput, (ii) wall-clock inference time, and (iii) actual computation cost.
As shown in Figures 6 [#S5.F6] and 7 [#S5.F7], HybridAgent adaptively balances its hybrid reasoning across text and latent modes by using 2.7×2.7\times fewer text tokens, reducing total generation steps by up to 54%, and achieving 2.1×2.1\times faster inference than text-based baselines.
Together with the cost analyses in Table 9 [#A7.T9] (see Appendix G.2 [#A7.SS2]), HybridAgent generates more concise and refined reasoning trajectories, reducing inference overhead and cost.

Self-Conditioning Enables Effective and Interpretable HybridSFT.
To understand why self-conditioned training is necessary for HybridAgent during SFT, we compare HybridSFT against variants with only format and HybridLink activations as well as latent SFT baselines like Coconut, from both performance and interpretability perspectives.
As shown in Table 8 [#S6.F8], although all SFT variants improve the original shared Qwen3-14B-Base backbone, activation alone remains insufficient. Self-conditioned supervision further improves performance by an average of 30.5% over HybridLink activation, bringing HybridSFT to parity with standard text SFT across all three math benchmarks.

We further probe the semantics of the latent thoughts produced by each method over 200 questions spanning all downstream domains.
Figure 8 [#S6.F8] reveals a clear progression, where HybridLink activation first makes latent contents more interpretable, and the additional self-conditioned training sharpens the latent readability and semantic informativeness.
We then take a closer look to examine individual test instances through detailed case studies in Appendix J [#A10].
Specifically, methods with low faithfulness and specificity generally produce largely unreadable artifacts of the embedding geometry, whereas the complete HybridSFT yields clear and semantically coherent latent readouts.
Overall, these analyses reveal that self-conditioned training is essential to HybridSFT for making the latent reasoning traces in HybridAgent fully transparent and directly interpretable.

Does HybridAgent Causally Rely on its Latent Mode Thoughts?
To examine whether latent thoughts are functionally used during hybrid reasoning, we intervene on the latent vectors generated by 8B and 14B HybridAgent on the MATH500 downstream task. We perturb their content, ordering, and reasoning duration through 4 different types of controlled interventions, including (i) noise perturbation, (ii) latent replacement, (iii) latent permutation, and (iv) latent computation restriction. Table 5 [#S6.T5] details the intervention setups and reports performance under each scenario.

As shown in the table, disrupting latent thoughts consistently degrades performance across both scales. Specifically, cross-problem and random-token replacement reduce accuracy by 44.9-47.0% and 49.4-51.3%, while disrupting their ordering or restricting each latent span to one step leads to 13.4-15.0% degradation. Notably, cross-problem replacement causes substantially greater degradation than even stronger Gaussian perturbations, suggesting that performance depends on the semantic content of latent thoughts rather than perturbation magnitude alone.
These analysis results on causal interventions indicate that HybridAgent functionally relies on the semantics, ordering, and sufficient reasoning depth of its latent thoughts during hybrid reasoning.

Does HybridAgent Text-Mode Preserve Agentic Tool Use?
In Figure 10 [#S6.F10], we analyze agentic tool-calling capabilities by comparing HybridAgent with normal LLM agents trained on GRPO, examining whether hybrid reasoning preserves reliable external interactions through its text mode.
Across 8B and 14B scales, HybridAgent achieves higher valid tool-call rates while matching the 99.7% execution success of valid calls, showing that hybrid reasoning preserves a reliable text-mode tool interface. More importantly, the gains extend beyond call validity, as HybridAgent improves final-answer accuracy by 2.2%-44.1% on questions where tools are invoked. The 4.5%-7.7% higher useful tool-call rate further indicates that HybridAgent not only invokes tools correctly, but also retrieves evidence that more directly contributes to solving the task.
These findings suggest that HybridAgent preserves the reliable and effective agentic tool use of standard LLM agents through its text mode, while exposing fewer text tokens with interleaved latent reasoning.

Does HybridAgent Text-Mode Preserve External Auditability?
A natural question of hybrid reasoning is whether shifting computation into latent mode might potentially compromise the external auditability provided by text. To examine this, we evaluate HybridAgent against matched text agents on MATH500 and AIME25/26, covering 560 problems under identical setups. For each question, an external observer sees only the exposed text steps, tool calls, and tool outputs, while latent spans remain hidden behind their boundary markers. We evaluate four complementary dimensions following commonly adopted evaluation protocols.

• 

Monitoring: Measure whether a blind external monitor can predict rollout correctness from the input problem and visible trace generated by each agent, following Baker et al. (2025) [#bib.bib52]. We report AUROC using GPT-4o-mini (Hurst et al., 2024 [#bib.bib56]) as a weak monitor, which relies primarily on the visible trace rather than solving the problem independently, and GPT-5.6-Luna (OpenAI, 2026 [#bib.bib48]) as a strong monitor with more powerful task-solving capability.

• 

Debugging: Following first-error localization (Zheng et al., 2025a [#bib.bib53]), a judge model (GPT-5.6-Luna) is deployed to identify the first erroneous visible step from a failed rollout and produces a corrective hint, with the diagnosis verified against the reference ground-truth solution.

• 

Correction: Each failed problem is re-attempted using the corrective hint generated during debugging, and compared against a generic correctness-only feedback baseline (Huang et al., 2024 [#bib.bib54]) that simply tells the agent its previous answer was incorrect and asks it to retry.

• 

Rationale Sufficiency: Following sufficiency evaluation in DeYoung et al. (2020) [#bib.bib55], a weak reader (GPT-4o-mini) predicts the agent’s own answer from the exposed text steps alone after removing the final answer, measuring whether the visible trace retains sufficient decision-relevant information.

7 Related Work

Latent Reasoning Beyond Text.
Recent works have explored applying intermediate reasoning beyond discrete language tokens into continuous hidden spaces (Hao et al., 2024 [#bib.bib28]; Zhu et al., 2025a [#bib.bib57]; Yu et al., 2026 [#bib.bib41]), allowing models to perform internal computation without explicitly verbalizing every reasoning step (Geiping et al., 2026 [#bib.bib59]; Shen et al., 2026 [#bib.bib58]; Zhu et al., 2025b [#bib.bib13]).
Existing approaches broadly realize this idea by inputting last-layer hidden states (Saunshi et al., 2025 [#bib.bib62]; Tack et al., 2026 [#bib.bib61]), compressing textual chains of thought into latent representations (Cheng and Van Durme, 2024 [#bib.bib64]; Zhang et al., 2026a [#bib.bib60]), or directly optimizing continuous reasoning trajectories (Tan et al., 2026 [#bib.bib63]; Chen et al., 2024 [#bib.bib65]).
These methods improve reasoning efficiency and depth but make direct inspection and external interaction difficult (Korbak et al., 2025 [#bib.bib24]; Chen et al., 2026 [#bib.bib15]).
HybridAgent builds on this direction by integrating directly inspectable latent reasoning as a native built-in mode within the agentic generation process, while preserving direct text-based auditing and tool interaction.

Agentic Reasoning and Tool Use.
Frontier LLM agents (Team, 2026 [#bib.bib44]; GLM-5-Team and others, 2026 [#bib.bib47]; DeepSeek-AI, 2026 [#bib.bib46]) extend reasoning beyond static generation by interacting with external environments through tools (Shen, 2024 [#bib.bib66]; Qu et al., 2025 [#bib.bib17]), such as search engines (Jin et al., 2025 [#bib.bib30]; Zheng et al., 2025b [#bib.bib71]), code interpreters (Feng et al., 2026 [#bib.bib19]; Bianchi et al., 2026 [#bib.bib67]), and APIs (Qin et al., 2024 [#bib.bib69]; Patil et al., 2025 [#bib.bib68]).
A growing line of work further improves these capabilities through post-training over multi-turn tool-use trajectories (Dong et al., 2025a [#bib.bib8]; Qian et al., 2025 [#bib.bib70]), enabling agents to plan, act, observe, and revise their reasoning (Su et al., 2025 [#bib.bib42]; Li et al., 2025c [#bib.bib33]).
Despite this progress, existing agentic frameworks remain text-centric for both model-side reasoning and environment-side interaction, incurring large generation overhead from verbalizing every intermediate step (Cuadron et al., 2025 [#bib.bib72]; Xu et al., 2025a [#bib.bib73]).
HybridAgent decouples these roles by interleaving continuous thoughts for internal reasoning and retaining text only where explicit interaction and monitoring are required.

We discuss additional related work and its relation to HybridAgent in Appendix D [#A4].

8 Discussion and Future Directions

Implications for Frontier Agent Safety and Secure Deployment.
Frontier labs (e.g., OpenAI, Anthropic) provide API-based access to reasoning models while hiding the chain-of-thought from customers to protect intellectual property and limit information leakage, yet Panfilov et al. (2026) [#bib.bib1] show that the encrypted traces returned to clients can be replayed into a sibling model and transcribed verbatim.
HybridAgent places proprietary reasoning on a different footing. Latent thoughts exist only as backbone hidden states that are readable solely through the provider-owned HybridLink (Section 3 [#S3]), so no text exists to leak.
The same property aids monitoring. Because the latent trace is held by the provider rather than the client, providers can assess the safety of reasoning and actions over the full readout, including the portion users never see, while the agent benefits from faster inner thinking. Text mode is then reserved for what users legitimately need, such as tool calls, key decisions, and the final answer, preserving an auditable trace without exposing the full reasoning.

Future Directions on Hybrid Agent Harness and Efficient Post-Training.
Future work can build a new type of agent harness around HybridAgent, where latent working memory is a first-class component. Context management can then operate on KV entries directly, workflows can schedule steps by mode, keeping tool calls and human-facing checkpoints in text while routine computation stays latent, and one agent’s hybrid trajectory can serve as its message to another. Hybrid generation can also make post-training more efficient. Because latent thoughts remain auditable during rollout, an external critic can assign step-level credit to latent spans, branch promising trajectories, or terminate ones whose latent reasoning has already gone wrong, before any text or tool call is produced.

9 Conclusion

We introduce HybridAgent, a unified hybrid reasoning framework that combines efficient latent reasoning with text-based inspection and tool interaction.
HybridAgent enables multi-turn hybrid generation through both architectural designs and dedicated post-training algorithms on SFT and RL stages.
Across 11 benchmarks and 3 model scales, HybridAgent achieves strong performance against advanced LLM agents and agentic frameworks spanning text-based and latent reasoning methods, while substantially reducing inference time, token usage, and computational cost.
Overall, HybridAgent provides a scalable and efficient framework for next-generation agents that can reason beyond human language while retaining agentic interaction in environments and external inspectability.

Appendix B Design and Analyses of Hybrid Supervised Fine-tuning

We expand on the method description of HybridSFT (Section 4.1 [#S4.SS1]), providing additional design justifications and implementation details for the overall training scheme.

B.1 Why Self-Conditioned Training?

Figure 11: Train-Inference Mismatch in Latent Reasoning. We illustrate the exposure bias that arises when teacher-forced training meets free-running inference. Left: During activation, the backbone consumes ground-truth embeddings Emb⁡(y)\mathrm{Emb}(y) at latent positions, while HybridLink outputs ℛ⁡(h)\mathcal{R}(h) are only trained to match their targets and never fed back as inputs. Right: At inference, every latent input is the model’s own ℛ⁡(h)\mathcal{R}(h), so deviations from the target embeddings accumulate across the span.

The format and HybridLink activation provide a well-aligned initialization, but both are teacher-forced (Figure 11 [#A2.F11], left). At every latent position, the backbone consumes the ground-truth embedding Emb⁡(y)\mathrm{Emb}(y), and HybridLink is trained only to match its output ℛ⁡(h)\mathcal{R}(h) to that target; the prediction itself is never fed back as an input. At inference, the agent instead runs free (Figure 11 [#A2.F11], right). Once it enters latent mode, every subsequent input is its own ℛ⁡(h)\mathcal{R}(h), which neither the backbone nor HybridLink has been conditioned on. This train-inference mismatch is akin to exposure bias (Ranzato et al., 2015 [#bib.bib5]; Bengio et al., 2015 [#bib.bib4]), a standard issue in autoregressive sequence models that are trained on ground-truth prefixes but consume their own predictions at inference.

It is worth noting that exposure bias is present in both text and latent modes, since both feed the model’s own outputs back as inputs.
In text mode (as in standard LLM SFT), each fed-back input is sampled and re-embedded, and thus always corresponds to a token in the discrete vocabulary. A prediction error, where the sampled token differs from the ground-truth target, only changes which token is fed back; the input representation itself never leaves the set seen in training, so errors cannot compound in the input space.
Latent mode, in contrast, has no such projection. ℛ⁡(h)\mathcal{R}(h) is continuous and fed back unquantized, so even a small deviation from the target embedding becomes an input the backbone has never seen in training. This deviation propagates to the next hidden state and hence the next latent input, compounding over the tens to hundreds of steps in a latent span and gradually eroding the semantic coherence of the latent trace.

Self-conditioned HybridSFT closes this gap by training both components on the model’s own generated latent inputs. As in Figure 4 [#S4.F4], the teacher-forced first pass applies HybridLink to the last-layer hidden state at each latent position, yielding the model’s own prediction of the next latent input. The second pass then replaces the ground-truth latent embeddings with these predictions and trains the backbone and HybridLink jointly, so both parts learn under the same latent inputs they encounter at inference.

Under this training scheme, the backbone learns to read, and HybridLink learns to produce, latent inputs under the same conditions they encounter at inference. Thus, HybridAgent is trained as an integrated whole rather than two separately activated components.

B.2 Learning When to Enter and Exit Latent Mode

As stated in Section 3 [#S3], HybridAgent transitions between modes through two special tokens, the entry anchor <latent> and the exit anchor </latent>. Within a latent span, these anchors are the only positions supervised by ℒBackbone\mathcal{L}_{\text{Backbone}}, while every other latent position is supervised by ℒHybridLink \mathcal{L}_{\text{HybridLink }} alone. The two transitions are not symmetric, however. Entering latent mode is decided in text mode, where the backbone samples the next token from the vocabulary as usual, whereas exiting must be decided from a continuous latent state through the readout in Eq. 5 [#S3.E5]. HybridSFT therefore treats the entry anchor as an ordinary token and gives the exit anchor dedicated supervision.

Entry as a Text-Mode Decision.
Since entering is a text mode decision, <latent> is simply a cross-entropy target, and the backbone learns from the preceding context when to switch. The anchor position then serves as the first latent step, with its last-layer hidden state passed through HybridLink to produce the next latent input, so the span begins without any additional mechanism.

Exit as a Latent-Mode Decision. Exiting takes place in latent mode, where no token is sampled. By Eq. 5 [#S3.E5], a span closes when the HybridLink’s output ℛ⁡(h)\mathcal{R}(h) is nearest to Emb⁡(</latent>)\mathrm{Emb}(\texttt{</latent>}), a decision depending on both components, where the backbone produces a hidden state that encodes the exit and HybridLink maps it onto the anchor embedding. HybridSFT therefore supervises the last latent step of each span from both sides.
On the HybridLink side, the regression target at this step is Emb⁡(</latent>)\mathrm{Emb}(\texttt{</latent>}), given slightly more weight than the span interior so that the single exit signal is not diluted. On the backbone side, </latent> is also a cross-entropy target predicted from the same hidden state, so the backbone itself learns to signal the exit.

Appendix D Extended Related Work

Adaptive Reasoning on Text and Latent Space.
Beyond fixed-length chains of thought, recent work lets LLMs adapt how long and in which medium they reason (Bigverdi et al., 2024 [#bib.bib82]; Lv et al., 2026 [#bib.bib81]; Yang et al., 2026 [#bib.bib75]; Ye et al., 2026 [#bib.bib80]), for example by learning when to stop latent reasoning or deciding whether explicit deliberation is needed (Ning et al., 2025 [#bib.bib77]).
Several methods interleave latent reasoning with text decoding (Yue et al., 2025 [#bib.bib74]), using confidence or entropy signals (Xu et al., 2026 [#bib.bib78]) to shift into continuous representations at uncertain steps and explore alternative reasoning paths (Fu and Luo, 2026 [#bib.bib79]; Shi et al., 2026 [#bib.bib76]).
However, existing methods largely operate within one reasoning pass or switch modes only at single-turn granularity, and their latent states remain largely opaque to inspect directly. Orthogonal to this line, HybridAgent uses latent mode for efficient deeper reasoning, carries latent context across multiple turns through continuous working memory, keeps latent thoughts directly readable, and preserves the agentic tool-use abilities for long-horizon tasks.

Hybrid Communication among Agents.
A recent line of work in multi-agent systems (MAS) parallel to HybridAgent studies hybrid-protocol communication, in which each inter-agent message combines two media. A latent channel carries dense intermediate signals such as hidden states or KV entries, while a text channel carries concise commitments that remain inspectable.
Early instances augment each natural-language message with the sender’s internal state trajectory (Tang et al., 2025 [#bib.bib84]), and later work formalizes the dual-channel design by routing elaborate reasoning through latent vectors while keeping answers and decisions in text (Mou et al., 2026 [#bib.bib85]; Dai et al., 2026 [#bib.bib87]).
More recent works take the same view from the deployment side, pairing every latent payload with a visible text commitment wherever oversight is required (Liu, 2026 [#bib.bib86]) and evaluating the security of latent transfer alongside visible text (Brito and Baquero, 2026 [#bib.bib83]). Complementary to these inter-agent protocols in the MAS setting, HybridAgent decides the medium at the level of each generation step within a single agent, switching between latent reasoning and text-based tool interaction over multi-turn trajectories.

KV-Cache Reuse.
Reusing key–value (KV) entries across generation steps is standard practice for efficient serving (Kwon et al., 2023 [#bib.bib39]; Zheng et al., 2024 [#bib.bib88]), and recent work extends it across requests, caching shared prefixes and prompt modules (Gim et al., 2024 [#bib.bib89]; Ye et al., 2024 [#bib.bib90]; Juravsky et al., 2024 [#bib.bib91]) and composing independently encoded caches so that repeated context need not be prefilled again (Yang et al., 2025b [#bib.bib92]; Ma et al., 2025 [#bib.bib93]; Hu et al., 2025 [#bib.bib94]). These methods treat the KV cache as an optimization over a context that could in principle be re-encoded from text. HybridAgent likewise builds on the backbone’s own KV entries, using them as a unified working memory that stores and propagates both latent and text information across multi-turn hybrid generation.

Appendix E Hybrid Data Curation

Figure 12: Hybrid Data Curation Pipeline. We convert text-based demonstrations into multi-turn hybrid trajectories using complementary signals from LLM judgments and model entropy to assign reasoning modes, followed by a visibility audit that preserves decision-critical steps in text mode.

We curate the hybrid training data by converting existing multi-turn agentic demonstrations (Yu et al., 2025 [#bib.bib10]; Dong et al., 2025b [#bib.bib43]) into 54k hybrid trajectories for text and latent supervision.
Specifically, an automatic annotation pipeline is designed to assign each source CoT step to either text or latent mode, based on complementary signals from external LLM judges and the target model’s entropy-based confidence, with no human labeling needed.
Accordingly, high-confidence reasoning steps that the model can handle reliably are assigned as latent targets, while audit-critical reasoning and tool-interaction steps are retained as text targets. We describe the detailed curation pipeline below.

Step Segmentation.
We first split each source trajectory into atomic reasoning steps using a deterministic procedure, recording their exact character offsets. These offsets allow subsequent annotations to identify and group steps while preserving the original content and ordering.

Mode Assignment.
We then leverage external LLM experts (GPT-6.1-Sol/GPT-5.6-Luna) to assign each reasoning step to text or latent mode, with tool interactions and key steps needed for external inspection retained in text mode (See Prompts in Appendix I [#A9]). Meanwhile, we complement the external selection with the target model’s prediction entropy. We teacher-force the original textual trajectory through a frozen base model and compute the mean token-level entropy for each step ss:

Hs=−1|ℐs|∑t∈ℐs∑v∈𝒱fθ(v∣x,τ<t)logfθ(v∣x,τ<t),H_{s}=-\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{s}|}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{I}_{s}}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}f_{\theta}(v\mid x,\tau_{<t})\log f_{\theta}(v\mid x,\tau_{<t}),

(18)

where fθ​(v∣x,τ<t)f_{\theta}(v\mid x,\tau_{<t}) denotes the frozen base model’s next-token probability after the vocabulary projection and softmax. Here, xx is the input question, τ<t\tau_{<t} contains preceding reasoning and tool observations, ℐs\mathcal{I}_{s} indexes the tokens in step ss, and 𝒱\mathcal{V} is the vocabulary. Low-entropy steps become latent candidates, while high-entropy steps remain in text mode. Initial mode assignments combining the two complementary signals are then refined through the visibility audit below.

Visibility Audit.
An independent LLM critic next examines the visible version of each annotated trajectory, with candidate latent spans hidden. The critic assesses whether the remaining text and tool interactions provide sufficient information to follow the agent’s decisions. When decision-critical information is missing, the corresponding source steps are restored to text mode. This audit helps preserve the key information needed for external debugging and inspection.

Hybrid Trajectory Construction.
We merge adjacent latent-assigned steps into continuous spans and programmatically insert the special anchors <latent> and </latent>. Tool calls and observations remain in text form. Finally, we verify that removing the inserted anchors recovers the original trajectory byte for byte. This consistency check ensures that the pipeline annotates reasoning modes without rewriting or altering the source demonstrations.

Appendix H Experiment Setups

H.1 Benchmark and Evaluation Details

We evaluate HybridAgent on 11 benchmarks spanning mathematical, knowledge-intensive, and agentic reasoning.
Together, these benchmarks assess complementary capabilities in multi-step problem solving, specialized knowledge application, evidence integration, and tool-assisted information seeking. We follow the setup of Dong et al. (2025b) [#bib.bib43] and describe each benchmark in detail below.

(i) Mathematical Reasoning Tasks

AIME 25 (math-ai, 2025 [#bib.bib34]):
AIME 25 comprises 30 problems from the 2025 American Invitational Mathematics Examination, covering algebra, geometry, number theory, and combinatorics.
Its integer-answer problems evaluate competition-level mathematical reasoning, requiring models to combine mathematical insights with precise multi-step calculations.

AIME 26 (MathArena, 2026 [#bib.bib35]):
AIME 26 contains 30 problems from the 2026 American Invitational Mathematics Examination, following the same competition format as AIME 25.
It provides a complementary assessment of mathematical problem solving across a separate annual problem set, testing sustained reasoning and accurate numerical derivation.

MATH500 (HuggingFaceH4, 2023 [#bib.bib36]):
MATH500 is a 500-problem subset of the MATH test set, spanning 7 mathematical subjects and 5 difficulty levels.
It evaluates math reasoning across diverse problem types, including symbolic manipulation, geometric deduction, and numerical computation.

(ii) Knowledge-Intensive Reasoning Tasks

GPQA-Diamond (Rein et al., 2023 [#bib.bib37]):
GPQA-Diamond is a curated subset of 198 expert-written multiple-choice questions in biology, physics, and chemistry.
Selected through expert and non-expert validation, these questions assess advanced scientific knowledge and reasoning that remain challenging for non-experts even with web access.

MedQA (Yang et al., 2024 [#bib.bib38]):
MedQA is a multiple-choice question-answering benchmark derived from professional medical licensing examinations. The dataset contains 300 questions in total.
Its questions assess medical knowledge and clinical reasoning, requiring models to interpret patient information and identify appropriate diagnoses, investigations, or treatments.

MuSiQue (Trivedi et al., 2022 [#bib.bib9]):
MuSiQue is a multi-hop question-answering benchmark constructed by composing single-hop questions into connected reasoning chains of two to four hops.
It evaluates the ability to combine evidence across multiple passages, with question construction designed to reduce shortcuts that bypass intermediate reasoning steps. Following Dong et al. (2025b) [#bib.bib43], we use 200 test-set questions for downstream evaluation across all methods.

(iii) Agentic Reasoning Tasks

General AI Assistants (GAIA) (Mialon et al., 2024 [#bib.bib26]):
GAIA evaluates general-purpose assistants on real-world questions involving reasoning, web browsing, information processing, and external tool use.
Its three difficulty levels reflect increasingly demanding combinations of reasoning steps and tool interactions, assessing the ability to coordinate these capabilities toward a verifiable answer.

Humanity’s Last Exam (HLE) (Center for AI Safety et al., 2026 [#bib.bib25]):
HLE comprises expert-authored questions across a broad range of academic disciplines, including mathematics, natural sciences, and the humanities.
Its multiple-choice and short-answer problems assess advanced subject knowledge and reasoning on questions designed to resist quick answers through direct web retrieval.

WebWalker (Wu et al., 2025 [#bib.bib11]):
WebWalkerQA, referred to as WebWalker in our evaluations, is an agentic search benchmark requiring exploration of website subpages and integration of information from one or multiple sources.
Its easy, medium, and hard categories reflect increasing traversal depth, testing the ability to locate and synthesize evidence beyond surface-level search results.

SealQA (Pham et al., 2026 [#bib.bib23]):
SealQA evaluates search-augmented question answering on factual questions for which retrieved information may be noisy, conflicting, or unhelpful.
We evaluate on Seal-0 and Seal-Hard, which test factual accuracy and reasoning under challenging retrieval conditions.
Seal-0 targets questions selected for near-zero accuracy among the non-reasoning models used during benchmark construction; Seal-Hard includes a broader collection of difficult questions.

DeepSearchQA (DSQA) (Gupta et al., 2026 [#bib.bib12]):
DeepSearchQA evaluates multi-step web research through tasks that require assembling complete answer sets from information distributed across sources.
It tests search planning, evidence aggregation, entity disambiguation, and the ability to determine when sufficient information has been collected.
We report both full-correct rate and F1 score to assess complete task success and the balance between answer precision and coverage.

H.2 Baseline Details

To evaluate the effectiveness and efficiency of HybridAgent, we compare with 4 categories of methods, including (i) Text-based Agentic Methods, (ii) Latent Reasoning Methods, (iii) Frontier LLM Agents, and (iv) Agentic Frameworks. We describe the implementation details of each baseline below.

(i) Text-based Agentic Methods

Standard Text SFT: We apply standard SFT using the same training data and backbone models as HybridAgent. During evaluation, the models can invoke tools and incorporate the returned observations into subsequent reasoning. We use this baseline mostly to compare with the HybridSFT.

Agentic RL (GRPO/ARPO): We apply commonly adopted agentic RL methods after standard text SFT to further train the text agent baselines. GRPO (Shao et al., 2024 [#bib.bib7]) optimizes the policy using group-normalized rewards from sampled responses without requiring a separate critic. ARPO (Dong et al., 2025b [#bib.bib43]): ARPO combines entropy-guided adaptive rollouts with step-level advantage attribution to improve multi-turn tool use. We train these methods using the same RL training data and backbone models as HybridAgent, primarily to evaluate the effectiveness of HybridRL.

(ii) Latent Reasoning Methods

Coconut (Hao et al., 2024 [#bib.bib28]): Coconut performs continuous latent reasoning by directly feeding the model’s last hidden state back as the next input embedding. A staged training curriculum progressively replaces textual reasoning steps with latent thoughts, teaching the model to reason in continuous space before generating a textual answer.

SoftCoT (Xu et al., 2025b [#bib.bib6]): SoftCoT uses a lightweight frozen assistant model to generate question-dependent soft thought representations, which a trainable projection maps into the target LLM’s representation space. These representations guide subsequent generation, enabling latent reasoning while keeping the assistant and target LLM frozen.

SoftThinking (Zhang et al., 2025 [#bib.bib2]): SoftThinking is a context-based method that represents intermediate reasoning as probability-weighted mixtures of vocabulary embeddings. Feeding these hidden continuous representations back into the model preserves alternatives from the next-token distribution.

We implement all latent reasoning baselines using the same backbone models and, where applicable, training settings and budgets as HybridAgent to ensure a fair comparison.

(iii) Frontier LLM Agents

To evaluate HybridAgent in a broader context, we compare it with current leading closed-source and open-source LLM agents, including GPT-5.6-Luna (OpenAI, 2026 [#bib.bib48]), Kimi-K3 (Team, 2026 [#bib.bib44]), GLM-5.3-Flash (GLM-5-Team and others, 2026 [#bib.bib47]), DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash (DeepSeek-AI, 2026 [#bib.bib46]), and QwQ-32B (Team, 2025 [#bib.bib45]), to assess both performance and inference efficiency of HybridAgent.

(iv) Agentic Frameworks

We further compare HybridAgent with established agentic frameworks that integrate multi-step reasoning with external tool calling and environmental interactions. We apply Qwen3-14B as the backbone for all these frameworks, with the same tool access and usage budgets as HybridAgent.

ReAct (Yao et al., 2023 [#bib.bib49]): ReAct interleaves natural-language reasoning with actions and observations from an external environment. This interaction loop allows an agent to gather information, revise its reasoning, and determine subsequent actions based on tool feedback.

Search-o1 (Li et al., 2025a [#bib.bib50]): Search-o1 augments large reasoning models with adaptive search to address knowledge gaps encountered during reasoning. Its Reason-in-Documents module analyzes retrieved documents and extracts relevant evidence before incorporating it into ongoing reasoning.

WebThinker (Li et al., 2025b [#bib.bib51]): WebThinker equips LLMs with a Deep Web Explorer that supports iterative search, webpage navigation, and information extraction. Its Think-Search-and-Draft strategy interleaves reasoning, evidence collection, and report generation to support complex research tasks.

Overall, these baseline methods provide complementary comparisons across training stages, text-based and latent-space reasoning, and tool-integrated agentic settings.

H.3 Implementation Details

Models and Training Details. We instantiate HybridAgent with Qwen3-Base backbones (Yang et al., 2025a [#bib.bib29]) at three scales of 4B, 8B, and 14B.
For HybridLink, we use the same two-layer design across all three backbones. The HybridSFT training data is constructed by converting 54K text-only sources (Dong et al., 2025a [#bib.bib8]; Dong et al., 2025b [#bib.bib43]) into hybrid trajectories. We apply format and HybridLink activation for one epoch each, followed by one additional epoch of self-conditioned training. HybridRL training utilizes 12K deep reasoning examples (Yu et al., 2025 [#bib.bib10]) for math and 1K agentic reasoning and search examples (Dong et al., 2025b [#bib.bib43]) for deep search.
We train HybridAgent with the GRPO objective and set rollout batches of 512 trajectories (64×864\times 8), followed by one optimization pass over 8 minibatches.

Training Hyperparameters. Table 10 [#A8.T10], 11 [#A8.T11], and 12 [#A8.T12] provide detailed parameter setups on HybridSFT and HybridRL. Following Dong et al. (2025b) [#bib.bib43]; Yu et al. (2025) [#bib.bib10], we use HybridAgent checkpoints from deep reasoning training for mathematical evaluations and the checkpoint from agentic search training for knowledge-intensive search and agentic reasoning evaluations, as detailed in Appendix H.1 [#A8.SS1].

Inference Setups.
During inference, we set the temperature, top-p, and top-k to 0.6, 0.95, and 20, respectively. The generation budget is adjusted for each task based on its relative difficulty and counts both text tokens and latent steps, with limits of 4,096 for mathematical reasoning and knowledge-intensive search tasks, and 24,000 for challenging agentic reasoning tasks.
For each latent span, we enforce a minimum of 16 and a maximum of 256 steps to ensure sustained and semantically rich latent reasoning.
HybridAgent interacts with external environments primarily through two types of tools: a Python interpreter within sandbox environments and a live search engine (e.g., Tavily).
We cap tool usage at 10 calls per trajectory and retrieve the top 10 snippets for each search query.
We utilize GPT-5.6-Luna as both the search-page summarizer and LLM judge.
All training and inference are conducted on 32 H100 GPUs. We also provide detailed prompt templates in Appendix I [#A9].

Infrastructure and Configurations.
As existing training and inference frameworks such as verl (Sheng et al., 2025 [#bib.bib3]) and vLLM (Kwon et al., 2023 [#bib.bib39]) do not directly support multi-turn hybrid generation, we extend their infrastructure to enable hybrid training and generation in HybridAgent.
A shared generation loop coordinates text mode, latent mode, and tool interaction while maintaining each trajectory’s persistent KV cache as continuous working memory across turns.
The agent enters latent mode through the special entry anchor <latent>, feeds HybridLink-generated latent inputs back into the backbone, and returns to text mode when the nearest-token readout matches the exit anchor </latent>, as in Eq. 5 [#S3.E5]. Tool observations are tokenized and processed against the existing working memory, appending new KV entries without reconstructing or re-prefilling earlier context.

The engine also supports batched rollouts with per-sequence state tracking, allowing trajectories to occupy different generation states within the same batch. Pending tool calls execute concurrently, with waiting and completed sequences masked from further generation. Per-sequence attention masks and position tracking preserve each trajectory’s causal context as generation proceeds. For HybridRL, exact latent-context replay recomputes sampled text-token likelihoods in a single differentiable forward pass while keeping recorded latent inputs unchanged. We check rollout–replay agreement of log-probabilities at unchanged policy parameters. Multi-node data parallelism, sharded optimizer states, mixed precision, gradient checkpointing, and resumable checkpoints support full-parameter backbone optimization. Overall, the same engine supports RL rollouts, training-time validation, and downstream evaluation, ensuring consistent mode transitions and working-memory handling across configurable sampling settings, generation budgets, and tool-call setups.
