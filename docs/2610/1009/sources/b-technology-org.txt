Congress Members Warn Google’s $10 Million Spirit Airlines Data Buy Could Expose Employees

 [https://www.addtoany.com/share]

Posted Today

Lawmakers Question Spirit Data Sale

About 100 million emails and 500 million Microsoft Teams messages from a defunct airline could soon help train Google’s AI. On Thursday, more than 120 members of Congress pushed back on Google’s $10 million purchase of Spirit Airlines’ internal data [https://www.aol.com/articles/lawmakers-raise-alarm-google-plan-194914000.html], asking both companies to keep former employees’ information out of the transfer.

Spirit Airlines aircraft ready for takeoff. Image credit: Randolph Rojas via Unsplash, free license

Key Takeaways

A letter led by Senator Elizabeth Warren and Representative Steven Horsford asks Spirit and Google to exclude employee information “to the greatest extent possible” and set enforceable limits on its use.

Google won the bankruptcy auction for Spirit’s internal data in August with a $10 million bid; AI data firm Mercor offered $7.5 million.

A court-appointed privacy ombudsman recommended approval with data on 97 million passengers excluded, ahead of an October 14 hearing.

What the Letter Asks For

The lawmakers wrote to Spirit CEO Dave Davis and Google CEO Sundar Pichai. They said the sale could include about 100 million emails, 500 million Teams messages, employee records, timecard records, payroll and tax information and employment contracts.

“Innovation should not come at the expense of workers’ privacy or the confidentiality of information they were required to provide as a condition of employment,” the lawmakers wrote. “The unprecedented scale and sophistication of modern artificial intelligence make it particularly important that privacy protections keep pace with the technology.”

They also doubted that standard privacy steps go far enough. “Removing names, email addresses, or other direct identifiers does not necessarily make a dataset anonymous,” the letter says.

The group urged the companies to add safeguards against profiling [https://www.technology.org/2026/09/28/meta-new-mexico-cambridge-analytica-verdict-penalties/], misuse, disclosure and onward transfer. If the deal proceeds, they want a de-identification process shaped by former employees’ feedback and an independent employee confidentiality review.

Google’s Response

Google says it does not want personal data. “We are not looking to buy any personal information from Spirit. The information will either be completely excluded or will be de-identified by an independent third party before Google receives any data. We’re already working constructively with the appointed privacy ombudsman,” a Google spokesperson said.

What Google Would Receive

Spirit halted operations in May and has been selling assets in bankruptcy. Google won an August auction for data from the carrier’s spreadsheets and calendars, along with marketing, productivity and operations records. Court filings also list pricing data on 7.2 billion competitor flights, about 7.5 billion passenger transaction records dating to 2008, roughly 30 million lines of code and employee records going back to 1986.

The deal excludes personal data and privileged material, including Spirit’s 97.5 million passenger profiles and about 50.2 million loyalty program records. On Monday, the privacy ombudsman recommended that the bankruptcy judge approve the sale at the October 14 hearing with passenger personal information left out.

Unions remain uneasy. “The outcome of conditions on this proposed sale to Google carries tremendous implications for workers across the economy,” said Sara Nelson, president of the Association of Flight Attendants-CWA.

Other Spirit assets are moving too. The Federal Aviation Administration this week approved the $58.5 million sale of Spirit’s slots at New York’s LaGuardia airport to JetBlue Airways.

Workplace Records as AI Fuel

The flight attendants’ union first objected in August, arguing that the deal protects passengers far better than workers [https://fortune.com/2026/08/21/flight-attendant-union-google-confidential-data-spirit-airlines/] because its privacy rules were modeled on consumer law. An earlier hearing was pushed back after that objection.

The case tests a young market [https://www.technology.org/2026/10/06/what-happens-when-consumers-get-paid-for-their-data/]. AI developers increasingly look beyond the public web and pay for proprietary records that show how real organizations schedule, price, communicate and write software. Former Spirit employees have objected to the deal [https://gizmodo.com/former-spirit-employees-arent-happy-about-its-ai-deal-with-google-2000801201], and the October 14 ruling could set a template for what other failed companies may sell to AI buyers.
