By David Lyons [https://www.sun-sentinel.com/author/david-lyons/] | South Florida Sun Sentinel

PUBLISHED: October 7, 2026 at 3:53 PM EDT

Spirit Airlines and Google have installed sufficient measures to safeguard consumer privacy for a proposed $10 million bankruptcy sale of the defunct carrier’s business enterprise data, a court-appointed expert has concluded.

In a nine-page report to the U.S. Bankruptcy Court for the Southern District of New York, Lucy L. Thomson, a Washington-based cybersecurity specialist, said the buyer and seller are ensuring that the personal information of Spirit customers would be removed or “de-identified” from the trove of data Google intends to buy.

“As a result of ongoing discussions among the Debtors, the successful bidder, Google, and the Ombudsman, the parties have taken consequential steps to protect the privacy of the personal data of the Spirit consumers,” she wrote.

She said they “revised the scope of the consumer personal data to be included in the sale of the deidentified data,” excluding passenger databases from the sale.

A third-party expert or “deidentification agent” would certify to Spirit that “court-approved procedures were performed and that, in the agent’s professional opinion, the transferred data is deidentified.”

“These changes will significantly reduce the potential risk of harm to the 97 million consumers who provided their personal data to Spirit for the purpose of booking airline travel,” Thomson wrote in her report.

“In conclusion, the Ombudsman recommends to the Court that from a privacy perspective, approval of the sale of the deidentified data would be appropriate,” she said. “The potential privacy losses or costs to consumers have been eliminated or mitigated.”

The sale also “would not violate any applicable non-bankruptcy laws.”

A Google spokesman said Wednesday the company had no comment. Previously, the company has said it wants the data because it “can be helpful in improving our products and AI models.”

Thomson was appointed by the court as a “consumer privacy ombudsman” to review the data sale after various critics and employee unions, among others, raised concerns that it posed privacy risks for former customers whose data remained in the bankrupt airline’s systems. Spirit halted operations [https://www.sun-sentinel.com/2026/05/02/spirit-airlines-out-of-business-cancels-flights/] in early May.

The defunct airline, which filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy [https://www.sun-sentinel.com/2025/08/29/spirit-airlines-again-files-for-chapter-11-bankruptcy/] protection in August 2025, has been liquidating assets [https://www.sun-sentinel.com/2026/08/13/spirit-airlines-campus-in-dania-beach-sold-to-boston-hedge-fund-affiliate-for-93-25-million/] including jetliners, real estate and airport landing rights among other possessions.

In her report, Thomson suggested that both the court and the companies had entered into unexplored legal territory with the transaction when it comes to privacy issues.

“It illustrates that with respect to the protection of personal data and deidentification, technology has far outpaced the law, potentially putting consumers at risk of significant harm if their deidentified personal data is reidentified and misused or stolen, or if AI makes inferences about individuals,” she said.

On Aug, 13, Google won a court-supervised auction for ⁠data that included Spirit ​employee emails, Microsoft Teams messages, ​spreadsheets, and calendars, as well as marketing, productivity, and operations data. Spirit also received a $7.5 million bid from an AI data company called ​Mercor.

But the sale’s approval was delayed when unionized pilots and flight attendants raised privacy concerns. [https://www.sun-sentinel.com/2026/08/20/judge-delays-10m-sale-of-spirit-data-to-google-after-attendants-raise-privacy-concerns/]

Last week, the pilots union at American Airlines, whose members include hundreds of cockpit crew members who formerly flew for Spirit, objected on safety grounds.

Thomson in her report said her review did not assess potential privacy risks for former Spirit employees. “The Ombudsman did not investigate or reach any conclusion about whether the data offered for sale or the deidentification process to be followed presents any risks to affected employees or third parties,” she wrote.

The next milestone for the data sale is a hearing before U.S. Bankruptcy Judge Sean Lane in White Plains, N.Y. It is scheduled for Oct. 14.
