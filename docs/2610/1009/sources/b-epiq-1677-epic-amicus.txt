        Federal Rule of Bankruptcy Procedure 8017, incorporating the standard of
Federal Rule of Appellate Procedure 29, governs the standard for filing a brief as
amici curiae. The most relevant portions of Rule 8017 suggest that a brief from
parties such as EPIC and Professor Patel may be accepted either by leave of court or
if all parties have consented (FRBP 8017(a)(2)); that the motion should state the
movant’s interest and the reason why an amicus brief is desirable and relevant
(FRBP 8017(a)(3)); and must be filed no later than seven days after the principal
brief of the party being supported is filed (FRBP 8017(a)(6)). The decision whether
to permit a person to appear as amicus curiae is committed to the Court’s broad
discretion. Securities Investor Protection Corporation v. Bernard L. Madoff
Investment Securities, 550 B.R. 241, 256 (S.D.N.Y. 2016) (citing Picard v. Greiff,
797 F.Supp.2d 451, 452 (S.D.N.Y. 2011)). “The usual rationale for amicus curiae
submissions is that they are of aid to the court and offer insights not available from
the parties.” Id.

   I. The Sale Agreement does not adequately protect the privacy of AFA
           members.

       Under the proposed agreement, a third party would deidentify the data at issue
using standards drawn from the California Consumer Privacy Act (“CCPA”). Sale
Agreement § 3(c). The CCPA standards require that deidentified data “cannot
reasonably be used to infer information about, or otherwise be linked to, a particular
consumer.” Cal. Civ. Code §1798.140(m) (emphasis added). The CCPA further
provides that data owners cannot associate the information “with a consumer or
household.” Id. (emphasis added). Google warrants that all consumer data sold under
the agreement will be deidentified to Google’s “reasonable satisfaction,” Sale
Agreement § 3(c), and that it will not “intentionally” reidentify data. Id. § 1(c).

       As an initial matter, the guarantee to deidentify data does not clearly extend
to employee data. Even if it does, while an important method to mitigate some risks
to personal privacy, the guarantees in the Sale Agreement are far from sufficient to
protect the privacy interests of Spirit Airlines employees. The Agreement places too

employees. Under the terms of the sale, Google has the power to determine what

deidentified data could reveal information that employees reasonably believed

deidentification required in the Sale Agreement is simply not sufficient to protect
employee privacy.

       B. The Agreement does not adequately mitigate the risk of
               reidentification by third parties.

       In addition to using the data itself, Google reserves the right to transfer the
data to third parties on the condition that the recipients will “maintain and use the
Deidentified Data in deidentified form [and] will not intentionally associate the
Deidentified Data with any person or household.” Sale Agreement § 1(c). Increasing
the number of entities that have access to the data would naturally increase the risk
that someone will, intentionally or unintentionally, reidentify some portion of the
employee data at issue. The primary mitigation against this risk is the
deidentification of the data—to Google’s satisfaction—under “applicable laws . . .

and prevailing industry standards.” Id. § 3(c). This is insufficient to protect the AFA
members against reidentification.

       One issue with this approach is that Google’s designated deidentification
agent cannot foresee how the data in question will interact with other data that has
been, or will be, released. Reidentification often involves singling out an
individual—that is, querying data to find a set of characteristics that result in a group
of one—then using external information to identify that individual. See Aloni Cohen
and Kobbi Nissim, Towards Formalizing the GDPR’s Notion of Singling Out, 117
PROC. NATL. ACAD. SCI. 8344 (2020). The more characteristics of an individual that
are made public, the easier it is to single them out. See id. at 8346; see also Kate
Crawford and Jason Schultz, Big Data and Due Process: Toward A Framework to
Redress Predictive Privacy Harms, 55 B.C. L. REV. 93, 96 (2014) (explaining how
large amounts of deidentified data “can generate a predictive model of what has a
high probability of being [personally identifiable information]”).

       Furthermore, Google’s reference to “prevailing standards” does not account
for evolving reidentification techniques. Early data privacy techniques focused on
removal of specific fields from datasets to prevent information from being linked to
individuals. See Paul Ohm, Broken Promises of Privacy: Responding to the
Surprising Failure of Anonymization, 57 UCLA L. REV. 1701, 1706-16 (2010)
(describing the prevalent “release-and-forget” approach to deidentification). Then

in assuring privacy for AFA members whose data is scheduled to be sold. The history
of data privacy suggests that, over time, advances in computing will render current
protections less effective. See Ohm, supra, at 1740-41 (describing how advances in
reidentification undermine existing data protection laws); Crawford & Schultz,
supra, at 106-08 (describing inability of existing data protection laws to address “Big
Data”). The only way to truly ensure that AFA members’ personal data is not
exposed is to prohibit the sale of that data entirely.

       C. Deidentification of employee data does not adequately protect
               against exposure of sensitive information by inference.

       Finally, even if the employee data at issue could be adequately deidentified,
inferences drawn from the deidentified data could still intrude on AFA members’
privacy. Indeed, one of AFA’s primary objections is that neither the Agreement nor
any governing law adequately protects confidential employee information. AFA
Objection ¶¶ 10-12. That concern is well founded, especially in light of recent
advances in data analysis techniques. Modern artificial intelligence (“AI”) systems
are often able to infer sensitive information about individuals and discrete groups,
even from deidentified data. See Alicia Solow-Niederman, Information Privacy and
the Inference Economy, 117 NW. U. L. REV. 357, 378-87 (2022). The Sale
Agreement does little to guard against such possibilities.

       AI processes like machine learning and large language models are the latest
in a long line of computing techniques harnessing the power of large datasets. For
decades, “Big Data” has enabled data processors to both place anonymous users in
groups and predict how members of those groups will act. See Crawford & Schultz,
supra, at 96. This gives rise to “predictive privacy harms,” whereby data processors

       Like prior Big Data processing techniques, AI uses massive amounts of data
to detect patterns that would elude a human viewer. See Solow-Niederman, supra,
at 380. Various AI applications are vulnerable to different forms of privacy attacks,
from inference of sensitive information to leakage of the underlying data. See Nadav
Voloch and Ron S. Hirschprung, Both Ends of Artificial Intelligence Impacting

number of these privacy harms could manifest. Amici focuses on one particularly
likely harm: attribute inference.

       Attribute inference is the process of analyzing public, non-sensitive data about
an individual to infer non-public, sensitive characteristics of that individual. See
Marcin Waniek et al., Human Intuition as a Defense Against Attribute Inference, 13
SCI. REP. 16088 (2023). As early as 2012, retail chain Target developed an algorithm
that predicted whether a shopper was pregnant based on their buying patterns.
Crawford & Schultz, supra, at 94. A 2019 University of Pennsylvania study showed
that it was possible to predict, with reasonably high certainty, certain medical
diagnoses by analyzing individuals’ social media posts. See Mason Marks, Emergent

   II. Allowing the sale of employee information would create incentives
           for other businesses to over-collect employee data.

       For all the reasons described above, Google’s proposed purchase of
confidential employee data would impose significant privacy risks on AFA members.
That alone is reason enough to bar the sale of that data or, at least, modify the Sale
Agreement to provide more robust privacy protections. However, amici also have
other, more holistic concerns: the commodification of personal data, the incentives
it creates for employers, and the impact it has on privacy and security.

       As explained above, modern AI systems require massive amounts of data to
operate. AI developers acquire that data however they can, including by purchasing
from other data collectors. Personal data is among the most valuable: the average
American’s information output is worth an average of $6,563 per year, according to

       Monetization of employee data creates particularly perverse incentives,

data are drastically one-sided.

                        CONCLUSION

As stated above, the Sale Agreement as written puts AFA members at risk of

having personal information exposed, either through reidentification or inference,

while giving those same members no say in how their data is handled, used, or shared.

Moreover, the sale would incentivize risky data collection behaviors by other

employees, encouraging them to over-collect employee data on the hopes of future

monetization. For these reasons, amici respectfully urge the Court to exclude the

challenged categories of employee data from the sale or, in the alternative, to modify

the Sale Agreement to provide greater protections against misuse and more control

by AFA members.
