FBI Arrests OpenAI Engineer Accused of Bugging an Executive Conference Room

 [https://www.addtoany.com/share]

Posted Today

OpenAI Engineer Charged Over Hidden Recorder

A thumb-sized recorder wrapped in toilet paper and plugged into the back of a conference-room television has led to federal charges against an OpenAI engineer [https://www.justice.gov/opa/pr/saudi-national-arrested-installing-surreptitious-audio-recording-device-openai]. Prosecutors say [当事人], a 35-year-old Saudi national, planted the device in an executive meeting room at one of OpenAI’s San Francisco buildings, where leaders discussed research, product plans and government work.

OpenAI logo on a smartphone screen. Image credit: Levart_Photographer via Unsplash, free license

Key Takeaways

The FBI arrested [当事人] on October 9, and he faces up to five years in prison if convicted of trying to intercept oral communications.

OpenAI found the encrypted device on September 30 and says it used its own AI models to trace who planted it.

Prosecutors say a chatbot account tied to his spouse asked how to build, hide and encrypt a continuous audio recorder.

A Device Behind the Screen

Recording Device 1. Image credit: U.S. Department of Justice

OpenAI staff spotted the recorder on or about September 30. It was plugged into a USB port on the back of a TV in an executive conference room. According to the criminal complaint, it had been installed about two days earlier, on or about September 28. Its contents were encrypted.

The complaint describes a gadget about the size of a thumb, with a microphone and the ability to connect to a camera. Toilet paper hid it from view.

 Image of [当事人]. Image credit: U.S. Department of Justice

The room hosted meetings on highly confidential subjects. Prosecutors list proprietary research, product plans, business opportunities, government engagements and nonpublic details about OpenAI’s technical progress.

[当事人], an OpenAI engineer since July 2025, had no authority to enter that room or join its meetings. Security cameras still caught him near it on several dates before the discovery, the complaint says.

A Trail of Searches and Prompts

Investigators say the planning left a digital trail. Before September 28, [当事人] allegedly used a workplace productivity tool to look up OpenAI building addresses, meeting rooms, room capacities and calendars. He also searched for “leadership and exec” locations and meetings with outside partners.

A chatbot account linked to his spouse, which shared several identifiers with his own account, sent a run of prompts between September 24 and September 30. The questions covered building, customizing, installing, testing, hiding and remotely accessing a recording device. The Justice Department quotes prompts about “build[ing] a continuous audio recorder,” “improv[ing] audio and live listening,” and “encrypt[ing] the SD card.”

The same account asked about the planted device itself, including its custom header and unique MAC address, on September 27 and 28.

On October 5, cameras recorded [当事人] entering the room and appearing to look behind the TV. That day he also left a bag unattended in another room. OpenAI security opened it and found his wallet, his ID and a second recorder similar to the first.

OpenAI Turned Its Models on the Case

OpenAI said in a post on X that its own AI did much of the detective work. “We used our models to understand the device and where it had come from, identifying that a specific employee had planted it a few days earlier,” the company wrote.

OpenAI fired [当事人] and referred the matter to the FBI, which is still investigating. [当事人] previously worked at Meta and Microsoft.

He was arrested on October 9 and made his first appearance in federal court in San Francisco on October 13. He remains in custody ahead of a detention hearing set for October 19. The complaint does not name anyone who was meant to receive the recordings, and it states no motive. He is presumed innocent unless proven guilty.

Officials Call It a Security Case

FBI Director Kash Patel framed the arrest around OpenAI’s research. “According to the complaint unsealed today, [当事人] allegedly concealed a covert recording device inside his employer’s executive conference room to secretly capture sensitive communications about the company’s super intelligence technology,” he said.

U.S. Attorney Craig H. Missakian for the Northern District of California was blunter: “For us it is a matter of national security.”

The charge carries a maximum penalty of five years in prison, a $250,000 fine and three years of supervised release. The National Security Division’s Counterintelligence and Export Control Section is helping prosecute the case.

Silicon Valley has faced insider cases tied to Saudi Arabia before. In 2022, a jury convicted a former Twitter manager of working for the kingdom without registering as its agent [https://www.cbsnews.com/news/ahmad-abouammo-former-twitter-worker-convicted-of-spying-saudi-arabia], after prosecutors said he and a colleague pulled private data tied to more than 6,000 accounts. That case involved user records. The OpenAI case, as alleged, went after something no database audit can catch: what executives say behind closed doors.

AI labs now guard their plans the way defense contractors guard blueprints. Engineers move often between rivals, and a leaked roadmap carries enormous value while companies race to ship frontier systems such as Meta’s new Muse Wonder model [https://www.technology.org/2026/10/13/meta-muse-wonder-frontier-model-pricing-muse-code/]. Last Friday, OpenAI said it had fired three researchers for breaking its rules on handling sensitive information. In this case, the company said it saw no sign that model weights, source code or customer data left the building [https://www.cnbc.com/2026/10/13/openai-employee-arrested-recording-device.html]. Insider risk now spans physical gadgets as well as patient social engineering, like the hackers who spent weeks befriending a developer [https://www.technology.org/2026/04/07/north-korean-hackers-spent-weeks-befriending-a-developer-before-hijacking-his-code/] before hijacking his code.
