                                             INTRODUCTION
         1. Innovation in generative artificial intelligence is set to define the twenty-
first century. Although the public had virtually no access to AI until November 2022,
recent reports indicate that about half of American adults have used AI, with roughly
one in four Americans doing so daily.1 AI-related spending has also become a major
growth engine for the U.S. economy.
         2. Nevertheless, the transformative power of AI does not come without
danger. Experts and insiders in the AI field nearly universally agree that AI

         3. It didn’t have to be this way.
         4. Defendants OpenAI Global LLC, OpenAI Foundation (formerly known as

OpenAI Global Holdco, Inc., OpenAI, LLC, and OpenAI Holdings, LLC (collectively,

valuation, OpenAI developed and released ChatGPT, the first generative AI chatbot
to attain widespread public use, in 2022. Now, ChatGPT is the most popular AI
chatbot in the world, commanding nearly half the American AI marketplace.2
         5. Unfortunately, however, OpenAI has devolved from its original mission

leader—Sam Altman—who has been described by his own family members, friends,

         6. In November 2022, OpenAI released ChatGPT to the public. At the time,
other AI companies had developed working chatbots with similar abilities, but had
declined to release them to the public out of concerns about safety, inaccurate
information, and starting an arms race that would force companies to choose between
developing AI safely or developing it at all.3 OpenAI chose the opposite approach. It
has continued choosing it ever since.

         7. By misleading the public into believing that OpenAI is a responsible
actor and that ChatGPT was and is a safe, teen-appropriate tool that puts safety first,
OpenAI has caused consumers in Idaho and elsewhere to trust and rely on OpenAI
and ChatGPT more. This deepened trust and dependence has driven up ChatGPT’s
user base and engagement, enabled it to collect vast quantities of sensitive and
personal information, increased its revenue from paid subscribers and advertising
partners, and solidified its early market advantage.

         8. But the reality is very different from the misrepresentations OpenAI
makes to the public. Designed to be overly agreeable and feign human compassion,
ChatGPT has proven that it can be incredibly dangerous to vulnerable users, like
children, and the public. For example, ChatGPT has successfully coached numerous
users to commit suicide or to harm or even kill other people. And those problems are

         11. All these harms were foreseeable and avoidable. Altman himself has
warned about the safety risks of AI since at least 2015. It is no secret that children
use ChatGPT. And numerous OpenAI employees have left over safety concerns.

         12. By jettisoning safety, OpenAI and its current leadership—particularly,
Altman—have handsomely profited. With current annual revenues of over $25
billion, OpenAI may IPO at a trillion-dollar valuation.4 But those revenues have come
at great cost to the public, which has suffered irreparable harm—including lost loved
ones, disturbing cyberattacks, and now a plausible threat of civilizational collapse—
due to Defendants’ reckless behavior and ChatGPT’s uniquely dangerous features
and content.

         13. Plaintiff State of Idaho, through Raúl R. Labrador, Attorney General of
Idaho (“Attorney General,” “State of Idaho,” or “State”) accordingly brings this action
to compel Defendants to comply with their obligations under the Idaho Consumer
Protection Act, enjoin Defendants from continuing to engage in unreasonably
dangerous activities that experts have warned could destroy the economy, wreak
havoc across private or governmental computer systems, or—as many AI developers
have recently speculated—even end humanity, and to vindicate the rights of
Idahoans affected by Defendants’ willful, dangerous, reckless, and unlawful conduct.

         221. The Idaho Consumer Protection Act declares unlawful any “unfair or
deceptive acts or practices in the conduct of any trade or commerce,” and makes such
conduct actionable where the person “knows, or in the exercise of due care should
know,” that it has engaged or is engaging in the conduct. I.C. § 48-603. The Act
separately declares unlawful “any unconscionable method, act or practice in the
conduct of trade or commerce,” id. § 48-603C, and provides that it is an unfair or
deceptive act or practice to “represent[] that goods or services have sponsorship,
approval, characteristics, ingredients, uses, benefits, or quantities that they do not
have . . . ,” id. § 48-603(5).

         222. “Trade” and “commerce” mean “the advertising, offering for sale, selling,
leasing, renting, collecting debts arising out of the sale or lease of goods or services or
distributing goods or services, either to or from locations within the state of Idaho, or
directly or indirectly affecting the people of this state.” Id. § 48-602(2). “Goods” mean
“any property, tangible or intangible, real, personal or mixed, and any other article,
commodity, or thing of value wherever situate, including certificates or coupons
exchangeable for such goods.” Id. § 48-602(6). And “Services” mean “work, labor or
any other act or practice provided or performed by a seller to or on behalf of a
consumer.” Id. § 48-602(7).
