Anthropic's Scion derived data design traced back to Meta's Mononoke

Anthropic's Scion version control system uses derived data to speed up blame and similar operations — a design a former Meta engineer says comes straight from Mononoke, which went uncredited.

Scion and the case for derived data

A blog post on sunshowers.io that reached the Hacker News front page examines a detail of Scion, the new version control system Anthropic described in a write-up published the previous day. The feature under discussion is what Anthropic calls derived data: results that a source control server computes and stores ahead of time — blame annotations are the example given — so that expensive work is not repeated every time a developer requests it. The practical effect is that operations like blame, which normally require tracing every line of a file back through history, become far cheaper to serve.

A design with a decade-old lineage

The post's author is a former Meta engineer who helped build Mononoke, the source control server developed there in 2017 and 2018 alongside the Sapling client. They note that they had described the same derived data approach, for the same blame use case, in 2022 materials prepared as part of a candidacy at Oxide — and that in their view the overlap with Anthropic's write-up goes beyond two teams independently arriving at the same insight.

Their central criticism is about credit rather than copying. According to the post, Anthropic's write-up acknowledged Sapling only as a source of hard-won lessons and did not mention Mononoke at all, which the author considers close to plagiarism. The post reads to them as suggesting Claude designed the scheme, when much of it reflects work done by the Mononoke team nearly a decade earlier.

The author is explicit that they have no objection to the design itself being reused: Mononoke was released as open source precisely so that good ideas could spread. They also point out that the lineage runs deeper than Meta, since the Mononoke and Sapling teams drew on prior art of their own, from Google's SrcFS to write-ahead logs in databases.

Attribution in the age of AI-assisted design

The post ends on a broader argument about how software design credit should work when LLMs are involved in the process. Because such models are trained heavily on existing systems, they will naturally reproduce established designs, and Mononoke — being open source — is part of every model's training data. As a demonstration, the author notes that asking current frontier models, naming Opus 5.5 and GPT-6 Astra, about Scion's prior art immediately surfaces the comparison to Mononoke.

The conclusion is that crediting predecessors has become both easier and more important than ever: the same tools that make it simple to borrow a design also make it trivial to find out where that design came from, leaving little room for omission.

Why it matters

For engineering teams, Scion's adoption of derived data is a signal that the Mononoke-style server architecture — precomputing expensive results and serving them from storage — has effectively become the default template for version control at scale, validated at both Meta and now Anthropic.

The dispute also previews a question the industry will face repeatedly: when AI tools help produce a design that closely mirrors existing prior art, who deserves the attribution, and what does proper credit look like? This account is one person's perspective and Anthropic has not, per this source, publicly responded, so the claims about intent should be read as the author's characterization rather than established fact. Even so, the episode is a useful early test case for attribution norms in AI-assisted systems design.
