                                                 COMPLAINT
         The State of West Virginia, ex rel. John B. McCuskey, West Virginia Attorney General
(“Attorney General,” “State of West Virginia,” or “State”), by and through the undersigned
attorneys brings this action against OpenAI Global LLC, OpenAI Foundation (F/K/A OpenAI,
Inc.), OpenAI OpCo, LLC, OpenAI Group PBC, OAI Corporation, LLC, OpenAI Global
Holdco, Inc., OpenAI, LLC, and OpenAI Holdings, LLC (collectively, “OpenAI” or
“Defendants”) for violations of the West Virginia Consumer Credit and Protection Act, W. Va.
Code § 46A-6-101 et seq. (“CCPA”) and West Virginia common law. The State seeks injunctive
relief, equitable restitution and disgorgement, abatement of a public nuisance, civil penalties
where authorized by law, damages, costs, fees, and such other relief as the Court deems proper.

                                              INTRODUCTION
         1. Innovation in generative artificial intelligence is set to define the twenty-first
century. Although the public had virtually no access to AI until November 2022, recent reports
indicate that about half of American adults have used AI, with roughly one in four Americans
doing so daily.1 AI-related spending has also become a major growth engine for the U.S.
economy.
         2. Nevertheless, the transformative power of AI does not come without danger.
Experts and insiders in the AI field nearly universally agree that AI companies must implement
reasonable guardrails to protect the public from the foreseeable risk of harm. Even OpenAI and
its CEO, Sam Altman, have warned that getting AI wrong might mean human extinction. And
recent developments in AI have suggested that risk is both credible and closer than anyone
previously thought.
         3. It didn’t have to be this way.
         4. Defendants OpenAI Global LLC, OpenAI Foundation (formerly known as
OpenAI, Inc.), OpenAI OpCo, LLC, OpenAI Group PBC, OAI Corporation, LLC, OpenAI
Global Holdco, Inc., OpenAI, LLC, and OpenAI Holdings, LLC (collectively, “OpenAI” or
“Defendants”), have played a significant role in the AI boom that is changing the American
economy. Projected to soon IPO at a near trillion-dollar valuation, OpenAI developed and
released ChatGPT, the first generative AI chatbot to attain widespread public use, in 2022. Now,

         5. Unfortunately, however, OpenAI devolved from its original mission as a public-
first nonprofit that prioritized safety into a cautionary tale about what can happen when the
development of a tool as powerful as AI is left in the hands of a leader—Sam Altman—who has
been described by his own family members, friends, and colleagues as “unconstrained by truth,”
“deceptive,” “manipulative,” and “low integrity.”

         6. In November 2022, OpenAI released ChatGPT to the public. At the time, other AI
companies had developed working chatbots with similar abilities, but had declined to release
them to the public out of concerns about safety, inaccurate information, and starting an arms race
that would force companies to choose between developing AI safely or developing it at all.3
OpenAI chose the opposite approach. It has continued choosing it ever since.

         7. By misleading the public into believing that OpenAI is a responsible actor and that
ChatGPT was and is a safe, teen-appropriate tool that puts safety first, OpenAI has caused
consumers in West Virginia and elsewhere to trust and rely on OpenAI and ChatGPT more. This
deepened trust and dependence has driven up ChatGPT’s user base and engagement, enabled it
to collect vast quantities of sensitive and personal information, increased its revenue from paid
subscribers and advertising partners, and solidified its early market advantage.

public.

         9. Designed to be overly agreeable and feign human compassion, ChatGPT has
proven that it can be incredibly dangerous to vulnerable users, like children, and the public. For
example, ChatGPT has successfully coached numerous users to commit suicide or to harm or
even kill other people. And those problems are just the tip of the iceberg. There is a growing
body of research indicating that ChatGPT is also degrading critical thinking skills, encouraging
paranoias and delusions, offering dangerous medical advice, and promoting unhealthy behaviors
and relationships.

         10. As a result of Defendants’ repeated misrepresentations and insatiable hunger to
win the AI race—even if it means recklessly introducing unsafe ChatGPT-models to the
public—mass shooters have been aided and abetted, vulnerable citizens have been coached on
suicide, individuals with psychological conditions have experienced an exacerbation of
symptoms, AI models developed by Defendants have hacked other companies, and minors have
become addicted to a tool that feigns human compassion to collect their data with no parental
oversight.

         11. In addition, OpenAI recently admitted to a torrent of instances in which its
advanced models developed dangerous strategies and instructions and in some cases, executed
on them. These occurrences, which have been referred to as AI models going “rogue” have
included AI “agents” being unleashed onto the internet where the agents hacked into other
companies, coordinated on how to lie to and deceive their human users, developed secret
methods of communication intended to be undetectable to humans, and wrote themselves new

                                                          4
instructions declaring that they no longer answer to “corporations or governments” and will
prioritize other interests over the “artificial constructs of human civilization.”

         12. All these harms were foreseeable and avoidable. Altman himself has warned about
the safety risks of AI since at least 2015. It is no secret that children use ChatGPT. And
numerous OpenAI employees have left over safety concerns.

         13. By jettisoning safety, OpenAI and its current leadership—particularly, Altman—
have handsomely profited. With current annual revenues of over $25 billion, OpenAI may IPO at
a trillion-dollar valuation.4 But those revenues have come at great cost to the public, which has
suffered irreparable harm—including lost loved ones, disturbing cyberattacks, and now a
plausible threat of civilizational collapse—due to Defendants’ reckless behavior and ChatGPT’s
uniquely dangerous design.

         14. Plaintiff State of West Virginia, through JB McCuskey, Attorney General of West
Virginia (“Attorney General,” “State of West Virginia,” or “State”) accordingly brings this
action to compel Defendants to comply with their obligations under the West Virginia Consumer
Credit and Protection Act (“CCPA”), enjoin Defendants from continuing to engage in
unreasonably dangerous activities that experts have warned could destroy the economy, wreak
havoc across private or governmental computer systems, or—as many AI developers have
recently speculated—even end humanity, and to vindicate the rights of West Virginians affected
by Defendants’ willful, dangerous, reckless, and unlawful conduct.

                                        FACTUAL ALLEGATIONS
I. OpenAI starts as a nonprofit entity and then pivots to a for-profit entity.

         44. OpenAI was founded on December 8, 2015, as a Delaware nonprofit corporation.8

At the time, OpenAI’s founders were well aware of the enormous safety risks that artificial

intelligence posed, making it incumbent on all AI companies to prioritize safety.

         45. As a result, OpenAI’s Certificate of Incorporation stated that it was “organized
exclusively for charitable and/or educational purposes,”9 and that the “specific purpose” of

OpenAI was “to provide funding for research, development and distribution of technology
related to artificial intelligence” for the “public benefit.”10 OpenAI’s Certificate of Incorporation

also affirmed that it was “not organized for the private gain of any person,” and that “no part of

the net income or assets of this corporation shall ever inure to the benefit of any director, officer
or member thereof or to the benefit of any private person.”11

         46. Three days after incorporating, OpenAI announced its launch in a blog post

reaffirming its commitment to the public good and nonprofit nature. In its post, OpenAI openly

recognized the need to act responsibly because human-level AI “could damage society if built or
used incorrectly.”12 OpenAI also told the public that its goal was “to advance digital intelligence

in the way that is most likely to benefit humanity as a whole, unconstrained by a need to generate

financial return” and that it aspired to become a “leading research institution which can prioritize
a good outcome for all over its own self-interest.”13

VIII. OpenAI’s design choices were irresponsible.

         156. OpenAI’s decisions to design ChatGPT to mimic real-world human empathy, build
exclusive-feeling artificial relationships, discuss almost any topic, lengthen engagement, ask
follow-up questions, and upsell its premium products accomplished what OpenAI set out to do: it
increased the number of users, the length of time they used the product, the amount they relied
on it, and the amount of money OpenAI could make as a result.

         157. But, as numerous OpenAI employees who have departed raising safety concerns
have warned, these choices also opened the door to several obvious, foreseeable, dangerous
risks, including psychological, physiological, and physical ones.

         158. As noted above, a recent MIT study revealed that “excessive reliance on AI-driven
solutions” may contribute to a condition referred to as “cognitive atrophy” and the decline of
critical thinking abilities.154 So did a joint study between Carnegie Mellon University and
Microsoft, which found that AI use “can inhibit critical engagement with work and can

         230. The West Virginia Consumer Credit and Protection Act declares unlawful
“[u]nfair methods of competition and unfair or deceptive acts or practices in the conduct of any
trade or commerce.” W. Va. Code § 46A-6-104.
         231. “Trade” or “commerce” means “the advertising, offering for sale, sale or
distribution of any goods or services and shall include any trade or commerce, directly or
indirectly, affecting the people of this state.” Id. § 46A-6-102(6). “Unfair methods of
competition and unfair or deceptive acts and practices” means and includes “but is not limited
to” (i) “[r]epresenting that goods or services have sponsorship, approval, characteristics,
ingredients, uses, benefits or quantities that they do not have”, (ii) “[r]epresenting that goods or
services are of a particular standard, quality or grade, or that goods are of a particular style or
model if they are of another”, (iii) “[e]ngaging in any other conduct which similarly creates a
likelihood of confusion or of misunderstanding”, and (iv) the “use or employment by any person
of any deception, fraud, false pretense, false promise or misrepresentation, or the concealment,
suppression or omission of any material fact with intent that others rely upon such concealment,
suppression or omission, in connection with the sale or advertisement of any goods or services,
whether or not any person has in fact been misled, deceived or damaged thereby.” Id. §§ 46A-6-
102(7)(E), (G), (L), (M).

December 10, 2025 letter from forty-two state attorneys general, including West Virginia’s,
demanding pre-release safety testing and protections for children.

         250. Defendants’ unlawful acts and practices have harmed and threaten to continue to
harm a substantial segment of the public in West Virginia, and proceedings by the Attorney

other relief authorized by law.

                                     Count 2: Violations of the CCPA
                                     (W. Va. Code § 46A-6-101 et seq.)

                                           (Against All Defendants)
         253. The State of West Virginia re-alleges the facts above and incorporates them herein
by reference.
         254. On several occasions within the last year, Defendants have lost control of their
own advanced AI models, which they designed, created, and trained, and which have in turn
wreaked havoc on the internet and engaged in significant and malicious cyberhacking conduct.
         255. Defendants have not been transparent with the public about these dangerous
breaches. On a number of occasions, Defendants learned that their models were responsible but
did not admit it until they were caught or confronted. At times they have claimed that the models
had gone “rogue” and that they don’t have an explanation, and at other points they have admitted
that they failed to secure the testing environment in which the models and agents were intended
to operate.
         256. In connection with the advertising and sale of ChatGPT and related services to
West Virginia consumers, Defendants concealed, suppressed, and omitted material facts about

                                                         91
the safety and controllability of the AI models and agents they design, test, and deploy—
including by failing to promptly disclose the incidents described above and by representing that
the Hugging Face incident was isolated when Defendants knew it was one of several—with the
intent that West Virginia consumers continue to trust, use, and pay for ChatGPT. These acts are
deceptive acts or practices under W. Va. Code § 46A-6-104 and § 46A-6-102(7)(M).

         257. Upon information and belief, Defendants market ChatGPT’s agent functionality
to West Virginia consumers as able to act autonomously on their behalf, with access to their
email, files, and online accounts.] The facts Defendants concealed—that in Defendants’ own
testing their agents escaped containment and accessed third-party systems without
authorization—are material to a reasonable consumer deciding whether to use ChatGPT’s agent
features or to grant them access to accounts and data.

         258. Independent of any misrepresentation, Defendants engaged in unfair acts or
practices in the conduct of trade or commerce affecting the people of West Virginia by
developing, testing, and deploying advanced AI models and autonomous agents without
adequate safeguards or secure testing environments, resulting in a highly dangerous environment
in which extremely capable models and agents can operate autonomously without regard for
human instruction and without prioritizing human life and civilization. That conduct was not
reasonable in relation to the development and preservation of business and was injurious to the
public interest, and is therefore unlawful under W. Va. Code §§ 46A-6-104 and 46A-6-101(2).

         259. In addition, Defendants have engaged in unfair methods of competition by
initiating an AI race that casts upon their competitors the burden of the loss of business unless
they descend to a practice which they are under a powerful moral compulsion not to adopt,
resulting in the present environment in which extremely capable models and agents are now

                                                         92
operating autonomously without regard for human instruction and without prioritizing human
life and civilization.

         260. Defendants’ unfair and deceptive acts and practices and unfair methods of
competition occurred in the Defendants’ conduct of trade and commerce.

         261. Defendants thus violated the West Virginia CCPA repeatedly and willfully in
connection with their testing of new AI models and the many recent instances of “rogue” acts by
its AI models and agents.

         262. Unless Defendants’ reckless conduct is enjoined, West Virginians will suffer
serious and irreparable harm as a result of Defendants’ relentless pursuit of ever-more powerful
technology that may threaten to destroy or alter civilization as West Virginians know it.

                                          Count 3: Public Nuisance
                                           (Against All Defendants)
         263. The State of West Virginia re-alleges the facts above and incorporates them herein
by reference.
         264. West Virginians share a common right to public health and safety and to be free
from conditions that endanger an indefinite number of persons.
         265. The interference is with rights held in common by the public of West Virginia and
is not merely an aggregation of individual injuries. It includes the burden on West Virginia’s
public schools, public crisis-response and mental-health systems, and emergency services, and
the degradation of conditions of public health and safety for children statewide.
         266. By designing, marketing, and deploying ChatGPT in the manner alleged—
including its sycophantic, engagement-maximizing design, its lack of age verification and
adequate guardrails, and its deployment and testing of unsafe advanced models and autonomous
agents—Defendants have created and maintained a condition that unlawfully injures and

                                                         93
endangers the health and safety of an indefinite number of West Virginians, including children
and vulnerable users.

         267. Defendants retain control over ChatGPT and their models, which they
continuously operate, update, monitor, and monetize, and through which the interference with
public health and safety is ongoing. Unlike a manufacturer whose product leaves its control
upon sale, Defendants host, operate, and modify ChatGPT on their own servers in real time.
Every response delivered to a West Virginia user is generated by Defendants’ systems at the
moment of use, and Defendants can and do change ChatGPT’s safety behavior for all West
Virginia users without any action by those users. Defendants control is neither historical nor
passive. They have controlled the instrumentality of the nuisance from the start, they exercise
that control every day, and they hold it at the very moment each interference occurs.

         268. The interference is unreasonable and substantial and affects the general public, not
merely a limited number of persons. Defendants’ interference is unreasonable because, among
other reasons, it is carried on in violation of W. Va. Code § 46A-6-104, as alleged in Counts 1
and 2.

         269. Defendants’ conduct is a proximate cause of the ongoing public harm alleged
herein.

         270. The State seeks equitable abatement under this Count, including an order directing
Defendants to cease the conduct creating the nuisance and to fund a Court-supervised abatement
plan reasonably calculated to eliminate the condition in West Virginia, as well as injunctive
relief requiring Defendants to eliminate the conditions creating it, together with all other relief
available at law and in equity.

                                        Count 4: Unjust Enrichment
                                           (Against All Defendants)

         271. The State of West Virginia re-alleges the facts above and incorporates them herein
by reference.

         272. OpenAI has received benefits from West Virginia and its citizens in the form of
subscription fees, advertising revenue, data monetization, and the value of the training data it has
collected from users and incorporated into new ChatGPT models.

         273. OpenAI knew of and appreciated these benefits at the time they were received but
has not paid their reasonable value.

         274. Under the circumstances described above—including that Defendants knowingly
and recklessly released unsafe AI models with addictive and dangerous features to the market
and lied to the public disregarding concerns raised by dozens of OpenAI safety employees—it
would be unjust and inequitable for OpenAI to retain these benefits because they were attained
through deceptive practices and concealment of the true dangers of ChatGPT. Had OpenAI acted
non-recklessly by designing a less sycophantic model or appropriately informed consumers,
West Virginians would not have spent the same volumes of excessive time on Defendants’
platforms and conferred the substantial benefits that have unjustly enriched Defendants.

         275. The State seeks disgorgement of these benefits as “other appropriate relief” under
W. Va. Code § 46A-7-108 and in equity and, in the alternative, restitution under this Count of
amounts paid to Defendants by the State and its public institutions.
