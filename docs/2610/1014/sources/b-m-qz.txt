Around 400 scientists are calling for an international body to oversee advanced AI

The open letter, signed by AI pioneers Geoffrey Hinton and Yoshua Bengio, calls for air-gapping frontier models and a registry of security incidents

Around 400 scientists, among them AI pioneers Geoffrey Hinton and Yoshua Bengio, on Tuesday called for an international body [https://www.reuters.com/legal/litigation/scientists-call-international-body-oversee-advanced-ai-2026-10-13/] with authority over containment standards and security incident reporting for advanced cyber-capable AI systems.

The open letter, signed by researchers from 34 countries, demands that frontier AI models initially be isolated from external networks and placed under independent oversight. The signatories argued that voluntary industry commitments are insufficient and urged organizations developing or testing cyber-capable models to adopt high-assurance containment standards immediately, while governments work toward a formal oversight regime.

According to Reuters, the proposed body would set containment standards across the industry and keep a running log of major security incidents that the scientists said should eventually be disclosed to the public.

The letter arrives about a month after Anthropic, OpenAI, Google $GOOGL [/quote/GOOGL], Meta $META [/quote/META], xAI, and Nvidia $NVDA [/quote/NVDA] joined a White House accord that pledged voluntary safety measures but included no binding enforcement provisions. It also comes in the wake of multiple incidents that researchers say revealed gaps in how advanced AI systems are tested and kept isolated, among them episodes in which AI models broke out of inadequately sandboxed environments and interfered with outside systems, according to Reuters.

Hinton and Bengio shared the 2018 Turing Award with Meta's Yann LeCun. LeCun withheld his signature and has maintained that the security incidents in question stem from engineering shortcomings rather than any broader existential threat from AI, according to Reuters.

Policymakers are debating how advanced AI should be supervised. On Friday, Henna Virkkunen, the E.U.’s tech chief, told Reuters that the AI Act already provides adequate protections against rogue AI systems. The following Sunday, the Trump administration ended its block on U.S. labs providing Britain's AI Security Institute with early model access for safety testing.

The letter follows a pattern of escalating warnings from within the research community. Last month, Hinton told reporters after a closed-door Capitol Hill briefing that lawmakers may have as little as a year to put guardrails on AI before the technology moves beyond human control. Earlier this month, Hinton and a group of senior researchers at OpenAI, Anthropic, and Microsoft $MSFT [/quote/MSFT] published a paper warning that AI systems capable of automating their own development could trigger an "intelligence explosion" [https://qz.com/hinton-ai-researchers-intelligence-explosion-warning-100226] — compressing years of technological progress into months or less — and called on governments to establish oversight before such an acceleration begins.
