                          INTRODUCTION AND SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT

        The best way to support Wikimedia’s free-knowledge mission is to foster an ecosystem

of human-driven knowledge production. Such an ecosystem requires a legal structure that meets

the desires of the people contributing to free knowledge by ensuring their work receives credit

and remains freely usable for additional knowledge production.15 To maintain such a legal

structure, fair-use analysis of AI tools must not be monolithic. Instead, the Court should treat the

multiple allegedly infringing uses identified in this case with their own separate fair-use

analyses, as mandated by the Supreme Court’s decision in Andy Warhol Found. for the Visual

Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith, 598 U.S. 508 (2023) (“Warhol”).

        As a preliminary matter, the act of scraping should not be treated as a “use” of a

copyrighted work. Scraping is simply a means of acquiring copies of works, but acquisition in

and of itself is not a use of those works. Instead, what one actually does with the acquired copies

is the “use” to which a fair-use analysis should be applied. In the context of large language

models (“LLMs”), copies acquired via scraping are subsequently used by developers at several

stages of building and deploying LLM-based services. As most relevant here, there are key

distinctions between (1) a copy’s use in training a general purpose LLM and (2) using copies to

supplement an LLM-based service through retrieval augmented generation (“RAG”).16 RAG is

useful to discuss because it is emblematic of a number of similar grounding techniques that make

use of copies of sources at time of inference (i.e., in response to individual user queries). Each

distinctive use should be assessed with independent fair-use analyses.

        First, the use of copyrighted works to train a general purpose LLM is likely to be fair

under the four-step analysis. While fair use is always context-specific to each case, the

experience that Wikimedia and its volunteer communities have with building and using AI/ML

tools suggests that using copied works to build a general purpose LLM is likely transformative

and does not inherently create market harm by substituting for the underlying works.

        Second, after developers incorporate a general purpose LLM into a consumer-facing

service (like a chatbot), the way copies are used by that service differs from the way copies are

used in the process of training the underlying LLM. Per the use-by-use approach, while uses of

works in some aspects of a deployed LLM-based service may be fair, others may not be.

        It is likely not fair use when an LLM-based service uses copies of original works for their

intended purposes—to provide relevant information, perspectives, expressions, and context—

while supplanting readers’ engagement with those original works. This is particularly the case

with a consumer-facing chatbot, like ChatGPT, that relies on RAG techniques to respond to

queries. Such a service copies text from third-party websites (like Wikipedia or The New York

Times), selects and pastes the most relevant passages or “chunks” of that text as supplemental

inputs into the LLM’s context window alongside the user’s query, and often substantially

reproduces the original content in its generated output. This is problematic for Wikimedia and

the free-knowledge movement when such copies are made and used without compliance with the

required attribution and ShareAlike terms of the Creative Commons license.17

        In summary, Wikimedia respectfully urges the Court to take a more distinct and nuanced

approach toward the fair-use question than the parties advocate. This includes recognizing that

the act of scraping itself is not a copyright “use,” and instead subjecting only what is actually

done with those scraped copies to fair-use analyses. The use of works in training an LLM that

can be used for many different purposes is typically transformative without immediately causing

market harm, making it likely to be a fair use. At the same time, a chatbot that implements RAG

to copy and use works in ways that supplant the original works is not likely to be a fair use of

those works. Both analyses apply here to ChatGPT: Wikimedia respectfully urges the Court to

rule that while the pre-training of ChatGPT’s general LLM was fair use, OpenAI’s use of copies

in ChatGPT’s RAG feature is not fair and must abide by the terms of the relevant licenses, such

as the Creative Commons license for Wikimedia Project content, to avoid infringement.

I.     Warhol Requires that the Fair-Use Analysis Be Applied on a Distinct, Use-by-Use
       Basis Throughout Each Stage of LLM Development.

       At a high level, Wikimedia agrees with Plaintiff The New York Times that the deployment

of an LLM can be described in four general stages: data acquisition (i.e., via scraping), pre-

training, fine-tuning or post-training, and a final product’s output generation. When conducting a

fair-use analysis across these stages, courts must identify every allegedly infringing use an LLM

developer and/or deployer makes of copyrighted works and apply the four-step analysis

separately to each such use. Warhol, 598 U.S. at 533. This is because “[t]he same copying may

be fair when used for one purpose but not another.” Id.

       Within the four-step fair-use analysis, “the first factor in particular” requires a clear view

of how an infringing copy is used at each stage. Id. Bartz v. Anthropic PBC is instructive in the

proper application of Warhol. 787 F. Supp. 3d 1007 (N.D. Cal. 2025). First, that court held that

the purpose of keeping unauthorized copies of books in an internal library “should they prove

useful” is akin to the original works’ purpose in “any university or corporate library” as being

available for the “various contingent uses” of their expressive content. Id. at 1026. Second, the

further, subsequent use of a subset of those same copies to train an LLM model had a

transformative purpose. Id. at 1021-22. That court did not impute the fairness of one use, or the

assessment of any of the fair-use factors, to another use of the same copies at a later stage in the

process of creating an LLM. Instead, the court made separate determinations that “retain[ing

pirated] copies [of books] should they prove useful for one thing or another[] was…not a

transformative [use]” and that “using copyrighted works to train LLMs to generate new text was

quintessentially transformative.” Id. at 1022, 1026. Ultimately, under Warhol, courts must not

“swallow” any “distinguishable infringing uses” by blending them together under a singular fair-

                                                      6
Case 1:25-md-03143-SHS-OTW                     Document 2361-1             Filed 10/29/26         Page 14 of 27




use analysis. Id. at 1020-28. Nor should courts import or export the ‘purpose’ of a copy’s use

between upstream or downstream stages of generative-AI product development.

         Wikimedia respectfully asks this Court to align its fair-use approach with the distinctive

uses of copies across the multiple separate stages of this process, as in Bartz, rather than treating

different uses as part of a continuum.

II.      Scraping—Making a Technical Request to a Website for a Copy of a Work through
         an Automated Process—Is Not a “Use” and Should Have No Bearing on the Fair
         Use Analyses Applied to Other Uses of Works in the Creation of a Product.

         Scraping does not involve any copyright-law “use” to which the four-step fair-use

analysis should apply. Scraping means making a copy of a work from a website, a necessary

precondition to using a work on a computer no matter what the purpose, and thus comes before

any type of creation or innovation.18 What matters for fair-use analysis is what happens with

those copies once made. Courts must “attend to different uses of different copies, and even to

different uses of the same copies.” Id. at 1028 (emphasis in original). Unauthorized acquisition

of copies does not mean later uses of those infringing copies are not fair—indeed, such a rule

would undermine the purpose of fair use. Likewise, fair uses of copies at one point do not

transform other, unfair uses of those copies at another point into fair ones. Failing to recognize

the “direct use of the copyrighted material” at each stage risks over- or under-applying fair use in

a way that stymies both innovation and the distribution of information between human authors

and audiences. Sega Enters. Ltd. v. Accolade, Inc., 977 F.2d 1510, 1522 (9th Cir. 1992).

III.       Using Copied Works at the Pre-Training Stage Is Likely Fair Under the Four-Factor
           Test.
           While this issue has been well-briefed by the parties, Wikimedia emphasizes that

developing new tools and models can be beneficial to a wide range of applications very different

from the original source material, including in manners beneficial to the free-knowledge

movement.

           Although the knowledge in the Wikimedia Projects is free, its creation can take a great

deal of work, care, and creativity. Volunteers do careful research and debate the quality of

sources, distilling their various viewpoints into the language and style19 of Wikipedia. While

Wikimedia has seen LLMs and other AI tools cause increased difficulty for volunteer

contributors20 when used poorly, Wikimedia also has witnessed the wide range of benefits that

can flow from the use of these tools.21 Tools from the field of machine learning that produced

LLMs help volunteer editors fight vandalism, detect copyright violations and inappropriate

personal information, determine what articles most need their help for improvement, and find

new topic areas to work on. These uses made possible by LLM training help people work more

effectively, and there likely are many more use cases that can help volunteers build better

encyclopedia articles, find and check sources, and improve their writing. In this context, the

ability to train and experiment with AI tools is likely to be a fair use based on the applicable

factors.

works in pre-training are used to analyze various works’ syntax and structure to adjust the

model’s understanding of relationships between words. Such a use does not reproduce expressive

IV.      Using Copied Works in a Chatbot’s RAG System is Likely Not Fair Under the
         Four-Factor Test.
         LLM-based chatbots like ChatGPT depend on RAG to provide timely and accurate

responses to user queries that cannot be answered by the base knowledge in the underlying LLM.

The RAG system can search the web (or another database that may be built from copies of

protected works) to identify, copy, rank, and then paste relevant content into the LLM’s context

window alongside the user’s query. This process augments a user’s prompt with information and

context that the LLM lacks.23 When relevant content is copied from the internet, a chatbot with

RAG relies on and reuses the most up-to-date and salient expressions from copies of websites

like Wikipedia to inform its responses to a user’s queries. Using copies of original web content

as RAG inputs to augment system knowledge on a query topic therefore substitutes those

original works' purpose in augmenting a human users’ knowledge on a query topic. Further,

RAG features sometimes respond to user queries by reproducing protected expressions without

citing to the sources referenced.24 This unattributed reproduction likewise substitutes the original

works’ intended purpose in providing expressive information sought by humans. Under the four-

factor test, such uses are likely not transformative and also tend to create substantial harm to the

market for the originals.

purpose—to inform readers—is not transformative. First, copies of works used in RAG “do

                                                CONCLUSION

           Wikimedia’s experience has been that freedom to work on and with new technologies

benefits the free-knowledge community. Trained LLMs, and indeed the broader sweep of trained

machine-learning models, have demonstrated substantial benefits to the Wikimedia projects

outside of text generation. These tools help address attempts at vandalism (even including

detecting potential copyright infringement), help volunteers review new edits to determine the

most valuable use of their time, and might be good at detecting undesirable posting of personal

information and doxxing attempts, based on recent experimentation within the Wikimedia

movement. It would be contrary to the purposes of copyright law to block these capabilities by

labeling LLM development as categorically not fair use.

            At the same time, fair use principles should not be used to greenlight the displacement of

human-produced knowledge in the name of innovation alone. Volunteers create a range of new

freely licensed media to illustrate articles, and contests like Wiki Loves Monuments25 have

spurred the creation of some of the most amazing pictures of the world’s natural and
