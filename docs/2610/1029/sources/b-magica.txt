CLEAR’s study of roughly 200 languages finds large differences in the tokens needed for equivalent text. The results expose a potential billing penalty, but the biggest gap is not a comparison of providers’ prices or successful task costs.

Equivalent benchmark text required up to 17 times as many tokens in one language as in another within a tested model; the baseline was not always English.

Rankings depend on the question: GPT-5 had a large worst-case gap but kept 78% of tested language-script pairs below twice its minimum token count.

CLEAR wants language-cost disclosures and pricing changes; the study does not establish the cost of completing real tasks successfully.

A September 29 study [https://clear-languages.org/blog/the-tokenizer-tax] by the Coalition for Language Equity in AI Research, or CLEAR, found that equivalent text required up to 17 times as many tokens in one language as in another within the same model. The coalition, which works to improve AI’s representation of underrepresented languages, measured disparities ranging from 2.7-fold to 17-fold across roughly 200 languages.

For services charging a uniform rate per token, these differences become a billing penalty. But the study compares text encoding, not actual customer invoices: its results depend on which languages are counted, which tokenizer is used and whether the question is about the worst-served language or coverage across the whole set.

CLEAR’s GPT-5 tokenizer example uses 13 tokens in English, 26 in Malayalam and 78 in Amharic. Replacement symbols indicate fragments that cannot render individually. This illustrates input encoding, not the total cost of a generated answer. Source: CLEAR. Source: CLEAR [https://clear-languages.org/blog/the-tokenizer-tax].

The billing unit carries a language penalty

A tokenizer converts text into the units a language model processes: words, word fragments, characters or smaller pieces. When one language gets broken into more pieces than another, a uniform price per token produces an unequal bill for equivalent content.

In CLEAR’s study, one sentence and its translations required 13 tokens in English, 26 in Malayalam and 78 in Amharic using the tokenizer for OpenAI’s GPT-5 general-purpose language model. At the same input-token rate, the Amharic version therefore costs six times as much to submit. That example measures the supplied text, not the total bill for an answer generated afterward.

A February 10 account by founding member LILT, an AI translation provider, [https://lilt.com/blog/clear-the-coalition-for-language-equity-in-ai-research] described CLEAR’s inaugural gathering in San Francisco as bringing together nearly 20 leaders from technology, academia and nonprofits. Its roadmap included benchmarks, open language resources and guidance on working with language communities. The tokenizer comparison is CLEAR’s first equity study, part of that research and advocacy effort.

The underlying problem predates this coalition. A 2023 research paper [https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.13707] examined the cost and utility of OpenAI’s language-model API on benchmarks covering 22 typologically diverse languages. It found that users of many supported languages faced higher charges while receiving poorer results. CLEAR adds a broad comparison of model families and identifies which languages their tokenizers serve least efficiently.

CLEAR’s Figure 2 tracks the maximum-to-minimum token-count ratio as progressively lower-resource languages enter the comparison. Colored bands show CLEAR’s grading thresholds. Each tokenizer has its own minimum-count baseline, which is not necessarily English; these are not comparisons of provider prices. Source: CLEAR. Source: CLEAR [https://clear-languages.org/blog/the-tokenizer-tax].

What the 17-fold figure does—and does not—compare

The researchers used FLORES-200, a benchmark containing professionally translated sentences. Their analysis covered 203 language-script pairs after excluding a deprecated language code. Some languages appear in more than one script, so the denominator is not 203 distinct languages. The language-resource ranking used Common Crawl web-page counts, followed by speaker numbers, as a proxy for availability in training data—not a measurement of each model’s actual training mix.

For each tokenizer, CLEAR divided the largest token count by the smallest for equivalent benchmark content. The baseline is that model’s most efficiently tokenized language—usually English or Simplified Chinese—not invariably English. Nor does the ratio compare the dollar prices of competing providers.

Among the 20 languages with the greatest web presence, most tested tokenizers had maximum-to-minimum ratios between 1.5 and 3.1. Falcon 3 was the exception at 4.5. Expanding the comparison exposed much larger disparities, with Falcon 3 reaching 17.0 across the full set.

Falcon 3 is a family of small language models released in December 2024 by the Technology Innovation Institute (TII), an applied research center under Abu Dhabi’s Advanced Technology Research Council. Its launch announcement [https://www.tii.ae/news/falcon-3-uaes-technology-innovation-institute-launches-worlds-most-powerful-small-ai-models] listed English, French, Spanish and Portuguese as its available languages. CLEAR’s test therefore extends well beyond that advertised language coverage.

But the worst case is only one way to measure equity. CLEAR also counted how many language-script pairs required less than twice the tokenizer’s minimum count. These selected results from its tables show how the two measures change the picture:

GPT-5’s tokenizer consequently looks weak when judged by its worst-served language but leads the tested general-purpose models on the share below the twofold threshold. Google’s group has a much smaller worst-case gap, yet fewer pairs below twice its own minimum. Neither measure is an absolute token-count or dollar-price comparison between providers. A buyer serving a particular language needs its individual result.

NLLB-200 supplies another important qualification. Meta built the translation model’s tokenizer for 200 languages. CLEAR treats its result as evidence that broader language coverage is technically possible, while acknowledging that it is not a like-for-like substitute for a general-purpose model handling code, mathematics and other tasks.

Scripts and design choices shape the gap

The study finds that less common writing systems are particularly disadvantaged. Latin-script languages can benefit even when their own digital presence is small. Asturian, for example, benefits from a shared script and related Romance languages, according to CLEAR. Languages written in scripts such as Tifinagh, Khmer and Ol Chiki fare much worse across many tested models.

The affected languages are not confined to small populations. CLEAR also identifies penalties for widely spoken languages including Punjabi, Bengali and Urdu. The relevant scarcity is digital representation, not simply the number of speakers.

The disparities cannot simply be read as differences in how much people have to write. CLEAR reports that the longest language by character count in its benchmark, Shan, was 42% longer than English—far short of the largest token-count multiples. The same Amharic benchmark sentences also ranged from roughly twice to eight times the favored language’s count depending on the tokenizer.

Progress is uneven. CLEAR’s maximum-to-minimum ratio improved from 14.8 for Meta’s Llama 3.3 to 9.5 for its Llama 4 Scout/Muse Glimmer group, while Alibaba’s Qwen 3.6 scored 13.1 against Qwen 3’s 10.5. Successive model generations can improve or worsen the gap.

There is also a capacity penalty. More tokens for equivalent text use up a model’s finite context allowance faster, leaving less space for documents or conversation history. CLEAR identifies latency as another consequence, although its token-count comparison is not itself a measured speed benchmark.

There are efforts to expand the underlying data. In interviews about the findings [https://restofworld.org/2026/ai-tokenizer-tax-non-english-languages-cost-inequity/], Raoul Nanavati, co-founder and CEO of Mumbai-based voice AI infrastructure startup Navana.ai, cited RESPIN, a Gates Foundation-funded collaboration with the Indian Institute of Science. He said the effort collected native-speaker text from 38 dialects alongside read speech.

But more data and lower bills are distinct from a better tokenizer. Dheemanth Reddy, CEO of conversational AI developer Maya Research, said in the same report that rapidly falling token prices should ease the direct financial burden over time. He cautioned that better multilingual and synthetic data alone would not fix the encoding gap: future tokenizers would still need deliberate changes to handle the languages and scripts.

The next test is cost per successful task

CLEAR proposes two different interventions. Future tokenizers should allocate vocabulary more deliberately across languages and scripts. The coalition says replacing tokenizers must wait for the next base model to be trained, while API providers could change billing sooner. It proposes character-normalized or language-normalized prices rather than passing the token-count disparity directly to customers. That would address the financial penalty without by itself expanding usable context capacity.

The coalition also wants model cards to disclose both the worst-case ratio and the share of languages below twice the minimum. It urges public-sector buyers serving multilingual populations to make disclosure and minimum equity grades procurement requirements, and research funders to support open language corpora with the communities’ agreement.

Its public repository [https://github.com/clear-coalition-ai/token-cost-equity] provides code and instructions for reproducing the analysis and evaluating additional models. The repository says it contains code rather than the underlying datasets; reproducing the results requires obtaining those inputs separately.

The remaining commercial question is how much a system costs to complete comparable tasks accurately in each language. Pratyush Choudhury, co-founder of AI venture fund Activate AI, which backs multilingual voice AI businesses including ElevenLabs, Sarvam and Wispr Flow, called for language-by-language scorecards of successful task costs in the same interviews.

Token counts isolate one part of that bill, but do not measure successful outcomes. CLEAR says a companion study on quality equity is underway. Combining that evidence with language-specific token usage and actual prices would let buyers judge whether more efficient encoding also produces a cheaper, effective service.
