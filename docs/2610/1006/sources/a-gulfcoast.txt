A Bonita Springs woman was arrested after she was accused of using an artificial intelligence platform to make violent threats against the Lee County Sheriff's Office.

Deputies responded to [当事人]'s residence on [地址] in Bonita Springs, where she was detained without incident. She's facing a charge for written threats of violence.

In the same week, deputies also arrested a man who they said posted a threat targeted at Sheriff Carmine Marceno in an AI-generated image showing him running away and injured as two people shoot at him.

Legal analyst and attorney Michael Raheb explained the challenges of proving such threats in court.

"They're going to argue that is a direct threat because you have the individual person and you show a violent act towards that individual. And that may be their theory. I would tell you, it's not going to be easy," Raheb said.

Raheb also highlighted the complexities of hate speech on AI platforms.

"This is a very slippery slope. We gotta remember the First Amendment, a right to free speech," he said. "As long as it doesn't cross that threshold, like I said, and harm someone."

Dr. Chrissann Ruehle, assistant professor of AI ethics at Florida Gulf Coast University, emphasized the responsibility of technology developers in preventing harmful applications.

"The technology developers have a responsibility to develop some guardrails around how the AI is being developed, and if some harmful applications are developed, they have a responsibility for flagging those," Ruehle said.

Marceno issued a statement warning users about the risks of anonymity in AI platforms, saying, "Artificial intelligence is a powerful tool, and like any technology, it can be misused. When someone uses AI to make or facilitate a threat, we have to take that threat seriously. Users need to understand that you are never truly anonymous."

These cases highlight concerns about how artificial intelligence is changing the way threats are identified and addressed.
