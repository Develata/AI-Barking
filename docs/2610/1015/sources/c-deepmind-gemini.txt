Migrating legacy code

Argon helped the Chrome team separate core webpage handling from a ~6 million line legacy folder of browser features held within Chromium’s 40 million-line codebase. Piping multiple clean code changes at a time, Argon helped the team finish an architectural refactor across hundreds of files, completing months of work in weeks. For Chrome's billions of global users, this will unlock the ability for live webpages to move fluidly between tabs, side panels, and floating windows without reloading, eliminating an entire category of browser crashes.

xTB (Semiempirical Extended Tight-Binding) is one of the most-cited open-source quantum chemistry packages for molecules. Scientists use it to predict molecules' 3D shapes and to screen large numbers of drug and materials candidates. Using Argon in Antigravity, our researchers made xTB up to 2.3x faster at predicting molecular structures and 1.8x faster at conformer search. The changes have been contributed to the official xTB repository on GitHub.

Argon in Antigravity is helping the team behind Google's open-source Agent Development Kit (ADK), a framework powering enterprise agents, push their performance engineering further. The team gave Argon their .agents/skills rulebook and a one-paragraph goal to eliminate framework overhead without breaking existing apps. Working autonomously overnight, Argon surfaced optimizations, now live in google/adk-python, that cut in-memory session prep time by 40% in long sessions and sped up cold imports by 21%.

Using Argon, we achieved a 2.85x end-to-end inference speedup on an A100 via vLLM (accelerating throughput from ~37,900 to over 108,000 tokens per second on Gemma 4 E2B) with 100% token parity and zero quality drop in just a few days, a process that would usually take months. Check out the upstream pull requests (optimizing everything from attention tuning to speculative decoding) to see the exact changes.

Argon in Antigravity migrated a backlog of 700+ Google Skills training labs off of hardcoded system images and runtimes spread across five code repositories, catching 16+ hidden timeout and race-condition bugs on the way. The work saved 955 engineering hours and reduced recurring cleanup sweeps from roughly 540 hours a year to a single 3-hour update.

Accelerating real-world impact with researchers and domain experts

Argon is built to be an active research partner, moving beyond literature search to help scientists, mathematicians and researchers formulate, stress-test, and prove non-obvious hypotheses.

Enzyme design

Argon is helping our researchers accelerate progress across both quantum error correction and algorithm design. To help error decoders keep pace with quantum hardware in real-time, Argon translated an internal version of AlphaQubit, our ML quantum error correction decoder, into a single, highly optimized hardware kernel – nearly doubling its inference throughput so we can deploy larger, more capable models and scale to larger quantum devices, while continuing to meet strict real-time thresholds. Argon is also reducing spacetime costs for quantum subroutines. For arcsin, a building block in recent quantum simulation algorithms, an implementation written by Argon and formally verified in Lean cuts spacetime cost by more than 100x compared to a published quantum CORDIC baseline, a step toward a standard library of optimized quantum circuits.

Learn about AlphaQubit
 [/blog/alphaqubit-tackles-one-of-quantum-computings-biggest-challenges/]

Stellarators are incredibly complex machines that represent a leading candidate for limitless clean fusion energy, but optimizing their 3D magnetic fields is famously difficult. Since 1967, a long-standing hypothesis known as “Grad’s conjecture” assumed that finding exact mathematical solutions for these fields was impossible, until recent counterexamples were found, including by Professor Matt Landreman (University of Maryland). Now, a team at Google alongside Professor Landreman has discovered a broader class of counterexamples to Grad's conjecture. These new solutions give the fusion community reliable benchmarks to test their stellarator simulations codes, which are valuable tools for future designs.

Researchers at Gamow Labs used Argon to analyze de-identified genomes from newborns with rare developmental lung disorders. In one case, Argon pinpointed a chromosomal rearrangement that routine software missed for years, and explained why it went undetected. On private Gamow Labs evals derived from real clinical and lab data, Argon topped on RareBench (57% on assistance with rare-disease diagnosis) and LabBench (52.2% on wet-lab reasoning). These advances point to faster diagnosis for families facing rare conditions.

Engineering enzymes with precise reactivity is notoriously slow, often demanding months of bespoke model tuning and candidate screening. Serine esterases are a class of enzymes that cut ester bonds on targeted molecules, widely used in recycling, and the manufacture of pharmaceuticals, biofuels and detergents. Our scientists worked with Argon to design serine esterases, targeting the benchmark molecule 4MU-acetate that glows under UV light once its ester bonds are broken. In a matter of hours, Argon assembled relevant data, performed model tuning and identified multiple biochemistry-inspired filters to get to a final set of five novel designs. Subsequent laboratory testing confirmed robust catalytic activity across these designs. The speed and ease of successful enzyme generation demonstrates the potential for rapid acceleration in enzyme engineering in fields such as biomanufacturing, green chemistry, and drug development.
