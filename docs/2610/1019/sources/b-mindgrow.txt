A bug exposed the hidden rules for WhatsApp's support chatbot on 80 help pages

Mindgrow Daily · 8 min read· Current as of Oct 19, 2026

Bug fixed, questions remain

Santeri Viinamäki · CC BY-SA 4.0

The news

On Oct. 19, 2026, WIRED reported that about 80 help and FAQ pages on WhatsApp's website had been carrying hidden instructions for one of Meta's AI chatbots. The privacy company Redact found roughly 100 blocks of the text in the pages' code, some under the heading 'WHAT NOT TO SAY.'

One rule told the bot not to recommend contacting any data protection authority or regulator. Another told it not to discuss privacy laws such as Europe's GDPR, even when a user raised them. Meta removed the text after WIRED asked, and a spokesperson said a bug had caused it.

The question

How can a company control what its chatbot tells you?

Through a system prompt, hidden instructions placed in front of every conversation: The company writes text telling the AI who it is, what to cover and what to avoid. The AI reads your question together with those rules and answers with both in mind. WhatsApp's leaked rules included not recommending privacy regulators and not discussing privacy laws, even when a user named one.

The AI model underneath, a large language model, writes replies by predicting the next words and has no built-in idea of who it works for. The system prompt supplies that. Users see their question and the answer, never the page of rules above them, so a bot's silence on a topic can be a rule rather than a gap. The only ways to see those rules are a leak, like this one, or a company choosing to publish them.

The number

80 pages

About 80 WhatsApp help pages carried roughly 100 blocks of hidden chatbot instructions until Meta removed them after WIRED asked about them.

What each side says

BOTH SIDES ACCEPT
Both sides agree the text was genuine instructions to Meta's support chatbot and was exposed by mistake.

privacy researchers at Redact

The key issue is the gap between what people are told and what the bot is told. A rule never to mention regulators, even when a user names a privacy law, tilts the answer toward the company. Users have no way to know such a rule exists unless it leaks.

Meta

The rules exist to give accurate, relevant answers to people who choose to message WhatsApp Support with AI. WhatsApp gives users transparent information about their data rights. A bot interpreting privacy law for the company could get it wrong, so it is told not to give legal advice.

How sure are we?

What we know

WIRED viewed the exposed text, and Meta confirmed it was instructions to its support chatbot, displayed by a bug that has been fixed. The rules quoted here, including not recommending regulators and not discussing privacy laws, come from that text as reported by WIRED.

What's still unclear

It is unknown how long the text was public, whether other AI systems collected it, and how often the bot followed these rules in real chats. Mindgrow relied on WIRED's reporting and did not view the original pages. Meta says WhatsApp gives users transparent information about their data rights elsewhere.

The short version

“WhatsApp's help chatbot came with a hidden rulebook, and a bug put it on public pages. One rule told it never to suggest you complain to a privacy regulator, which is a reminder that what a chatbot leaves out can be on purpose.”

THE FULL PICTURE

How did we get here?

2023

A day after Microsoft unveiled its new Bing chatbot, a student tricked it into revealing its hidden rules and its code name, "Sydney."

2025

The OWASP Foundation listed system prompt leakage among the top security risks for AI applications.

2026

WIRED reported on Oct. 19 that about 80 WhatsApp help pages had exposed rules for Meta's support chatbot.

Every chatbot runs on a written rulebook from its company. Leaks like Bing's in 2023 first showed the public what those rulebooks look like, and the WhatsApp case shows they can also shape what users hear about their rights.

What happens next

If nothing changes: Meta keeps the fix in place and the instructions stay private. Researchers keep scanning company sites, and similar leaks surface now and then.

If regulators take an interest: Privacy regulators, especially in Europe, could ask whether a bot told never to mention them gives users fair information about their rights. None has announced action.

If companies publish their prompts: More firms could post their chatbot instructions openly, as Anthropic does for its Claude apps, so users can see what a bot is told to avoid.

Tap a question for one way to think about it.
Should a company's support chatbot be allowed to stay silent about your legal rights?+
ONE WAY TO THINK ABOUT IT
A company has good reasons not to let a bot interpret the law for it, since a wrong legal answer can mislead people. But staying silent is also a choice that favors the company. Requiring bots to at least mention that regulators exist would be one middle path, though no law currently demands it.

Is a chatbot's hidden prompt different from a human agent's private script?+
ONE WAY TO THINK ABOUT IT
In one sense no: companies have always told staff what to say and what to avoid. The difference is reach and consistency. A single page of text now shapes every conversation the bot has, and the bot follows it every time, without the judgment a person might use.

If prompts can leak anyway, is there any point keeping them secret?+
ONE WAY TO THINK ABOUT IT
Security guidance from the OWASP Foundation argues both ways: leaked prompts can help attackers, yet no prompt should be treated as truly secret. Publishing prompts, as Anthropic does for its Claude apps, gives users more to go on, while companies that keep them private say it protects their products and their users.
