Introducing Glimpse

Visual thinking
that stays grounded

Glimpse looks closely, questions its
 first impression, and returns to the visual evidence before it
 answers.

Elorian · 16 October 2026

A glimpse is where looking starts. Understanding begins
 when a model knows what deserves a second look.

Glimpse is a new category of visual thinking model: a system that reasons
 about the physical world from what it sees. Glimpse follows objects in
 motion and across changing viewpoints, understands how parts fit together,
 and connects an observation to what happens next. That understanding
 matters in robotics, scientific discovery, and the creative
 industries.

Compared to existing frontier large language models, Glimpse pushes
 furthest on bringing visual representations into the reasoning process
 itself. It can reason in visual space, revisiting and refining internal
 visual representations as thinking unfolds, and is more grounded,
 learning to reason about objects and their spatial relationships. As an
 agent, Glimpse is also trained end-to-end to use multimodal tools that
 write code, transform images, and watch videos; and Glimpse can spawn
 and coordinate subagents that exchange text, visual artifacts, and
 internal representations to solve a shared problem. Together, these
 capabilities let Glimpse inspect a problem and revise its interpretation
 as it reasons.

On multimodal benchmarks, Glimpse attains frontier-level performance at
 the level of (and sometimes exceeding) GPT-6 Astra and Claude Opus 5.5
 while being a substantially smaller and cheaper model. We developed
 Glimpse as a small team with only a few hundred GPUs, post-trained on an
 open weights base model. Glimpse is available through API and our chat UI
 today.

Model results

Glimpse exceeds Astra on four evaluations spanning scientific chart
 reasoning, open-vocabulary counting, short-form video captioning, and
 manipulation-event understanding, and matches it on embodied reasoning.
 These capabilities support applications from analyzing
 laboratory data and tagging social-media videos to identifying completed
 actions in robot footage.

More generally, Glimpse is competitive across a suite of multimodal
 benchmarks. Input tokens cost $0.60 per million and output tokens $3.00
 per million: both rates are 94% lower than GPT-6 Astra’s, 85% lower than
 Claude Opus 5.5’s, 70% lower than GPT-6.1 Sol’s, and 20% lower than
 Gemini 3.8 Flash’s introductory rates.

What makes Glimpse so capable is careful and dense review to parse the
 best information for sound reasoning decisions. Looking that closely
 takes time, and tokens. Token efficiency is our next highest focus,
 alongside ramping up our research into the hardest visual reasoning
 model skills.

What visual thinking changes

The following examples illustrate different modalities of visual
 thinking — from following an event, to predicting an outcome based on the
 physics, to connecting an action to its effect, to counting
 objects precisely. These worked examples are meant to give you a glimpse into
 what simple visual thinking patterns look like, and how they can capture
 chains of reasoning more efficiently and accurately than text-based
 reasoning alone. As you chat with Glimpse, our platform shows the model’s
 visual scratch space as it reasons: it will zoom into a region, watch a
 short interval at a denser frame rate, or track one feature across time —
 and you too will start to build intuition around what kinds of problems
 visual thinking can unlock.

The two recorded traces above show different ways to resolve
 uncertainty: track one stable feature across time to judge speed, or
 revisit a short ambiguous transition at a denser sampling rate. In the
 second trace, the model moves from an 8 fps inspection to roughly
 14 fps over the 0.9-second interval that distinguishes a pan from
 a cut. The extra inspection is concentrated on the moment that
 determines the answer.

In the train clip, the model follows a window edge from the train’s
 entrance at about 2.2 seconds to its near-stop at 5.13. The edge
 travels progressively less distance between observations. In the bird
 clip, the camera swings as two birds leave a door handle; the model
 checks the intervening wall positions to determine whether the shot
 continues through the movement.

02 · Predict what happens next

What happens if the grip slips?

Read the contact, predict the consequence

Physical consequence. Identify the
 support, locate the pivot, and consider who is in the path of the
 falling edge.

“If the woman in the striped top suddenly loses
 her grip before the tire becomes upright, which woman is most
 immediately at risk of being struck, and how would the tire move to
 create that risk?”

Required answer: Identify which woman is at risk
 and explain how the tire would move toward her. This is an open-ended
 question, with no supplied answer choices.

Most immediate risk: the woman in the striped
 top.

Locate The tire’s lower edge
 is still in contact with the grass; its raised side has not crossed
 the upright balance point.

Model If support disappears,
 gravity rotates the elevated side back down around the grounded
 edge.

Project The returning edge
 sweeps toward the striped-shirt woman, who is leaning over the
 raised side.

Source photograph and reviewed reference answer.
 The explanation identifies the visible support and projected motion;
 it is not a recorded simulation or model trace.

The reviewed reference answer follows the tire’s motion: the grounded
 edge acts as a pivot, and gravity brings the raised edge back down
 toward the striped-shirt woman. Her position puts her most immediately
 in its path. This is a qualitative prediction from the photograph; no
 trajectory or impact force has been simulated.

Three women are lifting a tractor tire. Its lower edge rests on the

 The question asks what would happen if she lost her grip before the

04 · See precisely

Count the right objects

Count the open pliers

Count by identity and state. An original
 photograph exposes a failure that ordinary counting hides: the
 target category is easy, but its state and lookalikes must remain
 separate.

“How many open pliers are there? Return an
 integer.”

Required answer: One integer. Count only open
 pliers; there are no multiple-choice options.

4

Define The target is not
 every hand tool and not every pair of pliers. It is pliers whose
 handles and jaws are visibly open.

Separate Closed pliers are
 category matches in the wrong state. Scissors are lookalikes in the
 wrong category.

Verify Revisit each candidate
 against both constraints—tool identity and open state—before adding
 it to the count.

Reference The reviewed
 reference count is four.

Original photograph and reviewed reference answer.
 The steps explain the visual distinctions in the task; they are not a
 recorded model trace.

In the photograph above, the question asks for open pliers. A closed
 pair has the right tool shape but does not qualify; scissors have two
 handles and a hinge but belong to a different category. Checking the
 handles and jaws of each candidate gives the reviewed reference count:
 four.

Counting is a fundamental perception ability: identifying the set of
 objects within a scene. In a crowded collection, that means keeping
 adjacent objects separate, distinguishing them from lookalikes, and
 checking which ones meet the question’s criteria.

What Glimpse can do

The worked examples above illustrated how visual thinking works in
 theory. The below examples illustrate how Glimpse is applied in practice
 to a range of real tasks, from robotics (synthetic data generation for
 training, System 2 planning for a VLA model), to dense captioning
 over short form videos, to precise video counting of a group of puppies
 in a yard or equipment on a construction site, to reliably solving simple
 visual reasoning puzzles that a child can do, but have proven so
 challenging for many of today’s frontier models.

Toward models that understand visual worlds

Glimpse is a preview into the capabilities we are looking to unlock at
 Elorian. We believe building the full stack across systems, data, model
 architecture, and training — from the ground up for multimodal
 intelligence — can unlock AGI in the physical world. Models must be able
 to reconstruct a scene, predict what happens when something moves, and
 use that understanding to guide the next actions. Beyond robotics,
 engineering, and defense applications, these capabilities can help
 creators produce and edit video, technology companies search and organize
 online media, and game developers build and test interactive
 environments.

We are working toward these goals through multimodal-native
 pretraining, models that both understand and generate, and direct action
 outputs for robots. Glimpse’s visual understanding capabilities are an
 early step; calibrated reconstruction and robot control remain work
 ahead.
