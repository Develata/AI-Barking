                                                 Abstract
         We prove Sogge’s local smoothing conjecture for the Euclidean wave equation in every spatial
     dimension at least two. We deduce the Bochner–Riesz conjecture, maximal estimates for these
     means, and sharp bounds for Stein’s square function. We obtain Fourier restriction estimates
     for the sphere, the compact paraboloid, and the truncated cone throughout the interior of the
     conjectured range, as well as local smoothing for the Schrödinger equation. We also prove the
     Kakeya and Nikodym maximal conjectures; in particular, every Euclidean Kakeya set has full
     Hausdorff dimension.

of the initial data are needed to control the norm averaged over a time interval [72, Section 4,

1.2   Development of the local smoothing problem
The question grew out of Bourgain’s theorem on maximal averages over circles [12]. At high
frequency the Fourier transform of a circle of radius T has two leading terms, with phases T |ξ| and
−T |ξ|. This explains why a spacetime estimate for waves can help bound the supremum of circular
averages over radii. Sogge used this connection to prove a positive smoothing gain for every p > 2
in two space dimensions [72, Theorem 4.1]. Mockenhaupt, Seeger and Sogge gave a proof of the
circular maximal theorem using angular square functions [55], and developed local smoothing for
Fourier integral operators under a curvature condition on the associated family of cones [56].
    A persistent difficulty is to put waves moving in different directions back together. Wolff
decomposed a neighborhood of the cone into thin angular sectors and controlled their sum by
induction on scales. This gave every gain below 1/p for p > 74 in the plane [80]; Łaba and Wolff
extended the argument to higher dimensions [49]. Bourgain and Demeter’s cone decoupling theorem
gives this gain for p ≥ 2(n + 1)/(n − 1) in every dimension [14, Theorem 1.2]. A different argument
using radial Fourier multipliers attained the exact gain 1/p, with no additional Sobolev loss, for
n ≥ 4 and p > 2(n − 1)/(n − 3) [37, Theorem 1.2]. In the plane, Guth, Wang and Zhang reached the
critical exponent p = 4 by controlling the L4 norm of a wave by the spacetime square function of its
angular pieces [35, Theorems 1.1 and 1.2]. For the canonical sectors of a truncated cone, Cushman,

                                                   3
Demeter and Wu give the sharp power 1/4 of the logarithm in this square function inequality [20,
Theorem 1.4]. To obtain a bound in terms of ∥f ∥4 , one must also estimate the square function from
the initial data.
    In higher dimensions the distinction between a partial gain at pc and a full gain above pc
is substantial. At n = p = 3, Gao, Liu, Miao and Xi obtained every gain below 2/9 by broad
Fourier restriction estimates, compared with the conjectured 1/3 [29, Corollary 1.3]. Gan, He, Li
and Wu proved the full gain up to an arbitrarily small loss for p ≥ 10/3 in R3 and on compact
Riemannian three-manifolds [28, Theorem 1.3]; see also Wang’s study of wave packet density [78].
Interpolating the p = 10/3 estimate with the L2 identity gives every gain below 1/4 at p = 3.
Some three-dimensional metrics require p ≥ 10/3 for gains arbitrarily close to 1/p [53], whereas the
Euclidean exponent is 3.

1.4   A conserved current and two circles
Consider a finite sum of plane waves with |ξ| ≍ N whose directions lie in one small cap. We need to
take a square after summing these waves: summing the squares would discard the interference that
produces a focus. An imaginary translation along a light ray filters the sum by direction. Translation
by −iℓ(1, ω), with |ω| = 1 and ℓ > 0, multiplies a mode of frequency ξ by exp[−ℓ|ξ| |ω − ξ/|ξ||2 /2].
Its Gaussian width in direction is therefore of order (N ℓ)−1/2 for N ℓ ≫ 1. The symmetric first-order
wave system provides these light rays as its quadratic fluxes. The sum of two such fluxes gives the
imaginary displacement. Two flows built from the continued wave then give a positive semidefinite
tensor. Each of its real columns has zero divergence, and its time component still detects the original
wave on the chosen cap (Section 2).

Related constructions. The bilinear flux of a symmetric hyperbolic system is classical [25].
Analytic continuation in an imaginary spacetime direction has also been used to study waves [42]. For
positive-energy Klein–Gordon waves, Kaiser found a vector conserved in physical spacetime at each
fixed imaginary displacement. Integration over displacements gives a current with nonnegative time
density [41, Section 4.5, Equations (36)–(40)]. Here the continued wave gives a positive symmetric
tensor whose columns have zero divergence in the combined physical and auxiliary variables.
    Serre’s compensated integrability estimates pair a positive divergence-free tensor with the Hessian
of a real Monge–Ampère potential to bound a power of its determinant [69, Section 5.1]. The
usual stress tensor of a wave need not be positive in n ≥ 2 spatial dimensions [70, Section 4.1].

2     A conserved current for a superposition of waves
We use the flux of a symmetric first-order system twice. It selects an imaginary displacement that
gives each mode a Gaussian weight; applied to the continued wave, it gives a positive conserved
tensor. The modes are summed before taking the square, so their interference remains in the time
component of the tensor. We shall apply the construction on a fixed spatial torus: an annulus then
contains only finitely many modes, and integration by parts has no boundary in the real spatial
variables. Section 7 transfers the estimate to Rn .

A     The Dirichlet problem on a periodic fiber
The balance law in Section 4 uses a Dirichlet potential smooth up to a boundary that moves with
the time variables. Section 5 bounds this potential by comparing determinants on the whole fiber
and on small balls inside it. We prove the required existence, comparison, and smooth dependence
here; the quantitative bounds in the wavelength h come from the comparison functions in Section 5.

bounded domains in quaternionic space in the presence of a smooth subsolution [81, Theorem 1.1].
