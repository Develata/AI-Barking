OpenAI announced on October 26, 2026 three results produced by its internal models. The first is an algorithm that, according to the paper, disproves the Exponential Time Hypothesis (ETH, a conjecture about the top speed at which the hardest logic problems can be solved). The second is a proof of the local smoothing conjecture for the wave equation, and the third is an existence proof for Yang-Mills theory in four dimensions. None of the three has been confirmed by independent verification yet.

The news sits on the official page [https://openai.com/index/building-scientific-understanding-with-academic-communities/] of the San Francisco company, which for the first time pairs the results with a group of outside scholars tasked with checking them. Dan Roberts, who leads OpenAI's Frontiers team, also walked through the ETH result in a thread on X, as Runtime Wire [https://runtimewire.com/article/openai-ai-eth-disproof-external-review] reports. We had already reconstructed the bigger picture of the manuscript catalog OpenAI has been publishing on GitHub since October 6 in the report on Riemann and Hodge [/en/news/21359/openai-millennium-problems-riemann-hodge].

The three results OpenAI announced on October 26, 2026, with the dates declared on the official page

Exponential Time Hypothesis disproved, what the paper says

The Exponential Time Hypothesis states that solving the 3-SAT problem takes, in the worst case, a time that grows exponentially with the number of variables. 3-SAT asks whether a logical formula made of many conditions on three variables each admits an assignment of true and false that satisfies all of them. Russell Impagliazzo, Ramamohan Paturi and Francis Zane proposed it in the early 2000s, and it has become the foundation of hundreds of conditional hardness proofs in graph theory, computational biology, databases and computational geometry, according to OpenAI.

The paper, titled The Exponential Time Hypothesis is False [https://github.com/openai/math/blob/main/preprints/The-Exponential-Time-Hypothesis-is-False-October-25-2026/paper.pdf] and signed simply "OpenAI", describes a deterministic algorithm that counts the solutions of a k-CNF formula (the standard form of these problems) in a time of roughly 2^(n/(log n)^(1/2+o(1))) with n variables, using polynomial space. The gain over pure exponential time is modest, since the exponent drops only by a factor proportional to the square root of the logarithm of n, but it is enough to violate the form of ETH that rules out any sub-exponential algorithm.

The text goes further. It claims the same theorem also disproves the strong version (SETH, which predicts a time close to 2^n for k-SAT with large k), the Gap-ETH variant and the nondeterministic one, NSETH. Among the consequences listed in the appendix is a circuit lower bound for the class ENP, derived from a 2013 result by Ryan Williams, but the paper states it proves no superpolynomial lower bound for NP. The P versus NP problem, in other words, stays where it was.

The dates are the ones OpenAI declares. The system produced the natural-language proof on October 16, the formalization in Lean (the proof assistant that has the computer check every logical step) arrived on the 17th, and the published version dates to the 25th. Both the text and the Lean proof sit in the openai/math GitHub repository [https://github.com/openai/math/tree/main/preprints/The-Exponential-Time-Hypothesis-is-False-October-25-2026], under an Apache-2.0 license.

Who is checking the disproof

OpenAI handed the review to a group led by Russell Impagliazzo and Ramamohan Paturi (University of California San Diego), the two authors of the hypothesis, together with Ryan Williams of MIT, who has in the past refuted strong versions of ETH and has used its weak form to obtain hardness results. The company accepted two requests from the group, publishing all the raw data for independent verification and funding a conference on the implications. A disproof validated by its own creators would be a strong signal, but for now there is no validation, only a commitment to look for one.

On the practical side OpenAI urges calm. The disproof "reopens questions" about how much faster these tasks can be solved and may steer the search for better algorithms, but turning it into concrete improvements, the company writes, will take a lot more research.

Local smoothing and Kakeya in every dimension

The local smoothing conjecture, proposed by Christopher Sogge in 1991, concerns how waves behave when they concentrate at a point. It says the most violent concentration lasts a very short time, so once you average over time the effect stays far more contained than it is at the worst instant. If it is true, it implies two other famous conjectures in harmonic analysis, Kakeya (a set containing a line segment in every direction must fill space in the sense of Hausdorff dimension) and Stein's restriction conjecture.

The path, as OpenAI reconstructs it, proceeds in stages. In 2019 Larry Guth, Hong Wang and Ruixiang Zhang had proved the case of two spatial dimensions. In 2025 Wang and Joshua Zahl had closed Kakeya in three dimensions. The company's models now claim to have proved the conjecture in every dimension and, as a consequence, Kakeya and Stein as well, with a technique they describe as based on a new auxiliary partial differential equation.

The paper [https://github.com/openai/math/blob/main/preprints/Local-smoothing-for-the-Euclidean-wave-equation-October-25-2026/paper.pdf] does in fact claim a lot, from the Bochner-Riesz conjecture to restriction estimates for the sphere, paraboloid and cone, up to the closing sentence of the abstract, which says every Euclidean Kakeya set has full Hausdorff dimension. The models' output dates to October 18, the Lean formalization to the following day.

Here OpenAI took the more sober route. After consulting an expert it does not name, it published the model's text and the Lean proof and left mathematicians free to organize themselves to understand and explain them, exactly as with the October 6 release. For the mathematics there is no announced verification group.

Has OpenAI solved the Yang-Mills millennium problem?

No, at least not according to the company's own words. OpenAI declares an existence proof for quantum field theories with gauge fields and fermions in four dimensions, including quantum chromodynamics (QCD) and a unified completion of the Standard Model, and presents pure Yang-Mills theory as a subproblem that is "part" of the Clay Mathematics Institute millennium problem.

The choice of words matters. Pure Yang-Mills theory describes only how gluons (the particles that carry the strong force) interact with each other, without quarks. The millennium problem, in its official formulation, also asks for a second requirement, the so-called mass gap, which OpenAI's page does not claim. The text stops at existence and says the work leans on Balaban's approach to constructive field theory.

The timeline, again according to OpenAI, goes like this. A natural-language proof for Yang-Mills was produced on September 9, one for QCD on September 18, and the Lean formalization of Yang-Mills was completed on October 16. Given the length and complexity of the work, the company delayed publication until the formalization was done, and today the material is not public. The group reviewing it has decided to withhold the AI artifacts and to write an explanation of its own.

The group is led by David Gross (2004 Nobel laureate in physics, for the 1973 work on asymptotic freedom) and includes Michael Douglas, Arthur Jaffe (who with Edward Witten formulated the millennium problem), Igor Klebanov and Nikita Nekrasov. It asks that all communication go through a dedicated email address. For now, then, all that circulates on that work is OpenAI's statement, and there is no paper to read.

How OpenAI organized the verification

The real novelty of the announcement is the method. OpenAI writes that its manuscripts should be understood as "raw research data" and that, to turn them into scientific understanding, it asked outside experts to act as "shepherds", organizing groups that study them. The experts recommended three different strategies, one per result. For mathematics, release the output and give researchers freedom. For computer science, form a group that explains the result while keeping the output public. For physics, form a group and defer to it on the release.

Four principles, stated in the announcement, govern the collaborations. Transparency (no nondisclosure agreements, participants may speak and publish), no academic credit claimed by OpenAI, recognition for the group that builds the understanding, and independence (voluntary, unpaid participation, with conclusions of their own). The company also admits the downside, since choosing the first "shepherds" decides who gets in first and creates an information asymmetry. On top of the three groups comes a $5 million contribution to the Simons Foundation for workshops and conferences on AI-produced results.

The move comes after criticism. The Association for Human Mathematics had called [/en/news/21555/ahm-openai-demonstration-of-power] the release of more than 700 files "a demonstration of power" and had urged mathematicians to stop working with the company, while back in September 25 Fields medalists had sounded the alarm [/en/news/15971/fields-medalists-alarm-ai-mathematics] about AI in mathematics. OpenAI writes that it changed the process "in response to feedback from academic communities" and cites neither of the two positions.

Limitations to keep in mind

The three proofs are OpenAI statements, produced by its unreleased internal model, and no conclusion from outside experts has been published yet. For the ETH the group of Impagliazzo, Paturi and Williams has only just started, so the word "false" in the paper's title is for now a claim by the lab.

Lean checks that the steps are correct relative to a written formalization, but on its own it does not guarantee that the formalized statement matches the original conjecture, and the Lean verification was run by OpenAI. That is the point the outside group has to confirm.

On Yang-Mills and QCD there is only the official statement. The material is not public and OpenAI itself speaks of existence as "part" of the millennium problem, so nobody can yet say whether that part has really been solved or what remains outside it.

A sub-exponential disproof of the ETH does not by itself produce faster programs. The paper's algorithm gains only a square-root-of-log-n factor in the exponent, and OpenAI says more study is needed before any concrete improvement.

Frequently asked questions

1. Is the Exponential Time Hypothesis really false?

According to OpenAI it is, but no third party has confirmed it yet. The paper describes a sub-exponential algorithm for counting k-CNF solutions, and the company says the proof was verified in Lean. The group of Impagliazzo, Paturi and Williams has the job of checking and explaining the result, and OpenAI will fund a dedicated conference.

2. What changes for people working with SAT algorithms?

Nothing in the immediate term. The algorithm's running time remains enormous for real-world formula sizes, and the paper does not offer a ready-to-use program. What changes are the hardness proofs that rested on the ETH and on SETH, which will have to be revisited one by one if the disproof holds.

3. Are Kakeya and local smoothing really solved?

OpenAI declares it has proved them in every dimension. The two-dimensional case was known since 2019 and the three-dimensional Kakeya case since 2025, both by human mathematicians. For higher dimensions the only text available is the company's manuscript, with its Lean proof.

4. Where can the papers be read?

In the openai/math GitHub repository. The ETH and local smoothing folders carry the date of October 25, 2026. The one on Yang-Mills and QCD has not been published.

5. Why is OpenAI's model not named?

The repository README speaks of an unreleased internal model. The company specifies that the catalog's results were obtained with a common procedure, on average three hours of ChatGPT Pro per result, but gives neither a name nor a release date.

What to watch in the coming days

The decisive days will be the ones when somebody outside OpenAI writes the first line with their own name under it. A complexity theorist saying "the algorithm holds" or "there is a hole here" weighs more than a hundred threads. On the ETH the San Diego and MIT group has the advantage of knowing the field better than anyone, and the choice to open all the data should shorten the timeline.

On Kakeya and local smoothing, by contrast, the weight shifts to a community of analysts with no coordinator, who will have to read a long text by hand along with a Lean proof. On Yang-Mills everyone is waiting for a document. Meanwhile the Navier-Stokes experience [/en/news/15191/openai-navier-stokes-millennium-prize] in September and the one with the Erdős unit distance conjecture [/en/news/3065/openai-erdos-unit-distance-conjecture-ai-breakthrough] in May teach that the company's announcements should be treated as working hypotheses until the first serious check. The new method remains what OpenAI itself calls an experiment, to be judged on the results of the three groups.
