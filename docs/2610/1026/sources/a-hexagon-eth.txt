                               Abstract. We refute the Exponential Time Hypothesis (ETH) by presenting a deterministic
                               algorithm for 3-SAT on n variables that runs in 2o(n) time. Our algorithm in fact solves the
                               harder problem #3-SAT, providing a count of the number of satisfying assignments.
                                  The result was discovered by an internal research Claude model and has been formalized in
                               Lean 4.

                          A central question in the theory of computing is the P vs NP problem [Coo71, Lev73, Kar72,
                       Coo06]. Roughly, it asks if, for all problems with easily-verifiable solutions, is it easy to find
                       such solutions? NP is the class of problems whose solutions can be verified in polynomial time
                       and P is the class of problems that can be decided in polynomial time.
                          The canonical example of an NP problem is 3-SAT. An instance of the 3-SAT problem has
                       n Boolean variables x1 , . . . , xn and a formula consisting of the AND of m clauses, with each
                       clause consisting of the OR of at most 3 literals – a literal is either a variable xi or its negation
                       ¬xi . The question is whether or not there exists an assignment of the variables that satisfies
                       the formula – i.e., each clause has at least one literal that is true. Given an assignment of the
                       variables it is easy to validate whether or not the formula is satisfied. Thus the 3-SAT problem
                       can be solved by enumerating all 2n possible settings of the n variables and checking whether
                       the formula is satisfied.
                          Since the 1980s, a series of works have improved on brute force enumeration and reduced the
                       exponent by constant factors [MS85, Sch99, PPSZ05, MS11, Her14, Sch21]; the prior record
                       is O(1.328n ) time for deterministic 3-SAT algorithms [Liu18] and O(1.308n ) for randomized
                       algorithms [PPSZ05, Her14, SS17, HKZZ19, Sch21].
                          Since 3-SAT is NP-complete [Coo71, Kar72], the P vs NP problem is equivalent to asking
                       whether or not we can solve 3-SAT in time polynomial in the size parameters n and m. It is
                       widely conjectured that P ̸= NP, i.e., that it is not possible to solve 3-SAT in polynomial time.
                          An even stronger conjecture is that 3-SAT requires exponential time – i.e. there is a constant
                       δ > 0 such that 3-SAT cannot be solved in O(2δn ) time – this is the exponential time hypothesis
                       (ETH) of Impagliazzo and Paturi [IP01]. We show that this stronger conjecture is not true.
                       However, our result does not bear on the P vs NP question directly, nor does it yield any
                       practical algorithms for NP-complete problems.
                          Our result also extends to k-SAT (3-SAT but with clauses containing at most k literals;
                       such a formula is called a k-CNF) and to the #k-SAT problem, which asks for the number of
                       satisfying assignments of a k-CNF; in contrast k-SAT only asks whether that number is zero or
                       nonzero. We now state the general form of our main theorem.
                       Theorem 1 (Main Theorem). There is a deterministic algorithm that, given a k-CNF with m
                       clauses in which n variables occur, computes the number of satisfying assignments of these n

Consequences. Theorem 1 implies faster algorithms for many other NP-complete problems:
Independent Set, Clique, Vertex Cover and Dominating Set on graphs with n vertices can be
solved in time O(2δn ) for every δ > 0 (see Corollary 7 for Dominating Set; the other improvements
follow from prior work [IPZ01, FG04]), for example. Moreover, ENP does not have Boolean
circuits of linear size (this follows from Theorem 1 by prior work [Wil13, Wil14b, JMV15]). See
§7 for these and more consequences.
   The exponential time hypothesis, and its even-stronger twin the strong exponential time
hypothesis (SETH) [IP01, CIP09], are standard hardness assumptions in the literature [LMS11,
CFKLMPPS15]. SETH is a conjecture about k-SAT, which states that for every δ < 1 there is
a large enough k = k(δ) such that k-SAT cannot be solved in O(2δn ) time. SETH is known to
imply ETH [IP01, IPZ01], so Theorem 1’s refutation of ETH also refutes SETH (Corollary 5).
However, Theorem 1 does not refute the Orthogonal Vectors hypothesis, which SETH implies
and on which many lower bounds in fine-grained complexity rest [Wil05, BI18, Vas18]. In a
literature search carried out by Claude, we found more than 800 papers that prove at least one
result of their own only under ETH or under a hypothesis that implies it, such as SETH or
Gap-ETH; we did not count results that are also proved under a hypothesis that we do not
refute, such as Orthogonal Vectors.
   Our result is purely asymptotic: for arbitrary 3-CNF formulas with m = 5n clauses, the
bound we prove does not improve on the 2n of exhaustive search unless n > 1010 000 , and
the bound in n alone, which holds for any number of clauses, does not unless n > 1010 (see

the ends of Appendices A and D). Our general approach seems unlikely to yield any general
improvement unless n > 107 , however it is instantiated. Brute force enumeration is already
impractical for n = 100, so the improvement of our result does not appear in practical settings.
Thus our result says nothing about solving formulas in practice.

1.2. Related work. Algorithms for k-SAT. After the branching algorithms [MS85, Kul99],
the two main randomized methods for k-SAT are the local search of Schöning [Sch99] and the
algorithm of Paturi, Pudlák and Zane [PPZ99], sharpened in later work of the authors joint
with Saks [PPSZ05]. The latter solves k-SAT in time 2(1−µk /(k−1))n+o(n) , with constants µk
that increase to π 2 /6 [PPSZ05]; for k = 3, 4 this needs Hertli’s analysis [Her14], and later work
has lowered the exponents only minutely [SS17, HKZZ19, Sch21]. Schöning’s algorithm was
derandomized in steps [DGH+02, MS11], and the resulting bound has since been improved
[Liu18]. For counting, the known bounds were O(1.6423n ) for #3-SAT [Kut07], improving an
earlier bound [DJW05], and 2(1−Θ(1/k))n for #k-SAT [IMP12, CW16]. In terms of the number
of clauses m, satisfiability of CNF formulas with clauses of any length was known in time
1.2226m times a polynomial in the length of the formula [Hir00, Yam05, CXZ21]. In terms of
the total number of literals L, the bound was O(1.0638L ) [PX23].
The hypotheses and what was built on them. Before ETH, Stearns and Hunt had
proposed a hypothesis of this kind [SH90]. The bounds in the Sparsification Lemma [IPZ01]
were improved by Calabro, Impagliazzo and Paturi [CIP06] and in Calabro’s dissertation
[Cal09], and Dell et al. [DHMTW14] gave a form of it that preserves the number of satisfying
assignments. Unless ETH fails, k-Clique has no algorithm running in time f (k) no(k) [CHKX06,
LMS11], treewidth-based algorithms for constraint satisfaction are optimal up to a logarithmic
factor in the exponent [Mar10], and many parameterized algorithms are optimal [LMS18b,
CFKLMPPS15]. Under SETH there are matching bounds for dynamic programming on
graphs of bounded treewidth [LMS18a], for problems tied to CNF-SAT by reductions [PW10,
CDLMNOPSW16], and for polynomial-time problems such as Edit Distance [BI18, Vas18].
Earlier doubts. Williams refuted a Merlin–Arthur version of SETH [Wil16], and Super-Strong
ETH, a strengthening of SETH, fails for random k-SAT [VW21]. The set cover conjecture,
a relative of SETH [CDLMNOPSW16, CFKLMPPS15], is incompatible with the asymptotic
rank conjecture [BK24, Pra24]. In 2019 Williams estimated the likelihood that ETH is true at
70% and that SETH is true at 25% [Wil19].
From algorithms to circuit lower bounds. By a theorem of Williams [Wil13, Theorem 6.1],
Theorem 1 implies that ENP does not have Boolean circuits of linear size; this is part of his
program of deriving circuit lower bounds from satisfiability algorithms slightly faster than
exhaustive search [Wil13, Wil14, MW20]. The best lower bound known before for a language
in ENP , over the full binary basis, was 3.1n − o(n) [LY22]; the class S2 E, which contains ENP ,
requires circuits of near-maximum size [CHR24, Li24].
Sums of products and tensor networks. The sums of products that we evaluate (§2.1) are
weighted counting constraint satisfaction problems (#CSPs) with constraints on two variables
[DGJ09, CC17], pairwise sum-product expressions [Koi06], (for nonnegative weights) partition
functions of pairwise Markov random fields [WJ08], and contractions of closed tensor networks
[MS08, Oru14, RS19]. Passing from a formula to such a sum on a small dense graph follows
Williams’s algorithm for 2-CSP [Wil05], which goes back to Nešetřil and Poljak [NP85]; there
the saving comes from fast matrix multiplication, which we do not use. The closest work we
know is that of Austrin, Kaski and Kubjas [AKK22], who study the cost of evaluating such
sums by executing one tensor network along one contraction tree and prove, in that model, an
unconditional lower bound in terms of branchwidth. By our calculation (Appendix G), in that
model no algorithm for the problem on which we recurse (§2.2) has an exponent below about 49

                                  2. Overview of the proof
Notation. Throughout, log = log2 , and time is measured in the word RAM model. A
conjunctive normal form (CNF) formula is a set of clauses (i.e. no clause is repeated, and thus
a k-CNF on n variables has fewer than (2n + 1)k clauses); our input is a k-CNF. The variables
of a formula on n variables are x1 , . . . , xn ; a formula in which n variables occur, as in the Main
Theorem, is one on n variables each of which occurs in some clause. By #k-SAT, we mean the
problem of counting the number of satisfying assignments to a given k-CNF.
   The main body of our paper proves the following simplified statement. The improved
exponent of Theorem 1 is proved in the appendix (Appendices A and B for the bound in m,
and Appendix D for the bound in n).
Theorem 2 (Simplified version of Theorem 1). For every δ > 0 there is a deterministic
algorithm that computes the number of satisfying assignments of a 3-CNF on n variables, with
any number of clauses, in time O(2δn ).
Theorem 2 refutes the Exponential Time Hypothesis (ETH) of Impagliazzo and Paturi [IP01].
The algorithm depends on δ. Adjusting the parameters appropriately and using stronger
ingredients gives the Main Theorem.
   We describe the proof of Theorem 2 in six steps (§2.1 to §2.6); §2.7 explains how to strengthen
the proof to yield the Main Theorem. In this overview, we drop lower-order terms, rounding
and polynomial factors; the exact statements are in Sections 3–8. A bracket such as [x = u] is 1
if the statement inside it is true and 0 otherwise.
2.1. From #3-SAT to a sum of products on a graph. By the count-preserving form of
the Sparsification Lemma [IPZ01, DHMTW14] (Lemma 4), without loss of generality it suffices
to reach time O(2δm ) for every δ > 0 under the sparsity assumption m ≤ O(n).
   We can view a CNF formula as a graph. Each clause corresponds to one node, and edges
represent consistency constraints for shared variables. If two nodes have some number t of
shared variables, we say the edge between them carries t bits, corresponding to how those
shared variables are set.
   The first observation is that if this graph is disconnected, then the problem can be factored
into smaller problems with no shared variables. More generally, if we can partition the graph
with few edge bits crossing the cut, then for each fixing of the shared variables we can solve the
counting problem on the two halves recursively then solve the overall counting problem via

enumeration. In our actual algorithm, each level of the recursion rewrites the sum as a sum of
polynomially many sums, where the degree of the polynomial depends on δ. Each of these inner
sums is again a sum of products over a graph as before. We partition this graph not into two
but into some larger number of pieces, where that number also depends on δ, merge each piece
into a single node, and recurse on the resulting smaller graph, which has fewer edge bits in total.
The key quantity is still the number of edge bits that cross between different partitions. The
crux of our algorithm is to iteratively reduce this quantity while also keeping the parts small.
   The other key ingredient in our algorithm is a way to rewrite edges. At a very high level, we
take a pair of edges between two parts of the graph and replace them with a single hyperedge.
This reduces the number of edge bits between parts. To explain how this works we need to
introduce an algebraic view of this graph problem that allows us to exploit cancellations.
   Each node a has “local” variables za and has a value Wa (za ) – this is 1 if these bits satisfy
the clause and 0 otherwise. (In general, we will let W take arbitrary values.) Two nodes a, b
are joined by an edge if their clauses share variables. The edge carries one bit for each shared
variable, and Ψa,b (za , zb ) is 1 if the local variables za and zb give a consistent view of a global
set of variables and 0 otherwise. The number of satisfying assignments of the variables that

deciding whether a given k-CNF is satisfiable. Let sk be the infimum of all δ ≥ 0 such that some
deterministic algorithm decides k-SAT on n variables in time O(2δn ), and let s∞ = supk≥3 sk .
The Exponential Time Hypothesis (ETH) is the statement that sk > 0 for all k ≥ 3. The Strong
Exponential Time Hypothesis (SETH) is the statement s∞ = 1.
  Trying all assignments gives sk ≤ 1, so s∞ ≤ 1. A k-CNF is also a (k + 1)-CNF, so sk ≤ sk+1 .
Hence s∞ = limk→∞ sk , and ETH is the same as s3 > 0. Impagliazzo and Paturi [IP01, p. 374]
pose s∞ = 1 as an open problem, and Calabro, Impagliazzo and Paturi [CIP09] give it its name.
Impagliazzo and Paturi [IP01] do not say whether the algorithms in the definition of sk are
deterministic or randomized, and Calabro, Impagliazzo and Paturi [CIP09] allow randomized
ones. A deterministic algorithm is a randomized algorithm that uses no random bits, so allowing
randomness can only make sk smaller, and Corollary 5 holds under each of these conventions.
Corollary 5. sk = 0 for every k ≥ 3. Hence s∞ = 0, and both ETH and SETH are false.
Proof. A 3-CNF is satisfiable if and only if its number of satisfying assignments is positive,
so Theorem 2 gives s3 = 0, which is the negation of ETH; for this, the proof of Theorem 2
needs Lemma 4 only in the form of Impagliazzo, Paturi and Zane [IPZ01, Corollary 1, p. 523],
without the condition that no two of the formulas share a satisfying assignment: without it
the sum may count an assignment more than once, but it is still positive exactly if the given
formula is satisfiable. By a theorem of Impagliazzo, Paturi and Zane [IPZ01, Theorem 3, p. 525],
which rests on their Sparsification Lemma, s3 = 0 implies sk = 0 for every k ≥ 3; the reduction
(sparsify, then split every long clause into clauses of 3 literals with new variables) uses no
                                A 2o(n) TIME ALGORITHM FOR 3-SAT                                  21

randomness (Calabro, Impagliazzo and Paturi [CIP06, Lemma 1] state the Sparsification Lemma
with a deterministic algorithm). In terms of the numbers sk this is stated by Impagliazzo and
Paturi [IP01, Theorem 1, p. 368]: the statements “For all k ≥ 3, sk > 0”, “For some k, sk > 0”
and “s3 > 0” are equivalent. Hence s∞ = limk sk = 0 ̸= 1. Put differently: s∞ = 1 gives sk > 0
for some k, hence s3 > 0; so SETH implies ETH, and refuting ETH refutes SETH.               ■
The nondeterministic variant NSETH [CGIMPS16], which says that for every ε > 0 there is a
k such that the tautologies among the k-DNF formulas on n variables are not recognized in
nondeterministic time 2(1−ε)n , is false as well: a k-DNF is a tautology exactly if its negation, a
k-CNF, is unsatisfiable, and a deterministic algorithm is in particular a nondeterministic one.
Circuit lower bounds. By a theorem of Williams [Wil13, Theorem 6.1], if ETH is false
then ENP , the class of languages decided in time 2O(n) with an oracle for NP, does not have
linear-size circuits (see also Williams [Wil14b, Theorem 5.8] and Jahanjou, Miles and Viola
[JMV15, Corollary 2, item (1), with Corollary 3, item (1′ )]). So, by Corollary 5, there is a
language in ENP that is not decided by (non-uniform) Boolean circuits of size O(n).
Corollary 6. For every k ≥ 3 and δ > 0 there is a deterministic algorithm that computes the
number of satisfying assignments of a k-CNF on n variables in time O(2δn ). For k = 3 this
says that the counting version #ETH of ETH [DHMTW14, p. 21:5] is false.
Proof. For k = 3 this is Theorem 2. By the work of Dell et al. [DHMTW14, Lemma A.1 with
condition (1′ ), and the proof of Theorem 1.1, pp. 21:24–21:25] this case suffices. First, their form
of the Sparsification Lemma, which Lemma 4 states for k = 3, turns a k-CNF on n variables,
for any ε > 0, into at most 2εn k-CNFs on the same variables, each with O(n) clauses, no two
of which share a satisfying assignment, so that their numbers of satisfying assignments add up.
Second, each of these is turned into a 3-CNF with O(n) clauses and O(n) variables and the
same number of satisfying assignments, by the standard width reduction, which introduces a
constant number of new variables for each clause. The number is preserved if each new variable
is forced, by three clauses of at most 3 literals, to equal the disjunction of the two literals that
it replaces in a long clause; Dell et al. [DHMTW14] do not spell this out. The constants in
O(n) depend on k and ε, so choosing ε = δ/3 first and then applying Theorem 2 with a small
enough value in place of δ gives time O(2δn ).                                                      ■
Corollary 7. For all δ > 0 and k ≥ 3 there are deterministic algorithms that, given a graph
on n vertices, with any number of edges, and an integer s, compute in time O(2δn ) the number
of its independent sets of size s, and likewise of its cliques, vertex covers and dominating sets
of size s and of its proper colorings with k colors. In particular, they decide Independent Set,
Clique, Vertex Cover, Dominating Set and k-Coloring, since subsets of independent sets and of
cliques, and supersets of vertex covers and of dominating sets, are again such sets.
Proof. For deciding Independent Set, Clique, Vertex Cover and k-Coloring, the corollary follows
from prior work: these problems, with the number n of vertices as the parameter, reduce to
3-SAT by subexponential reduction families [IPZ01, Theorems 3 to 5, pp. 525–526], which carry
over Theorem 2’s time O(2δn ) for every δ > 0 (see also Flum and Grohe [FG04, Corollary 7.1]).
These results cover neither Dominating Set nor the counts, for which we argue as follows.
   Let the variable xv say whether the vertex v is in the set S. As in the work of Impagliazzo,
Paturi and Zane [IPZ01, proof of Theorem 5], a circuit with O(n) gates of two inputs tests
whether |S| = s; a new variable for each gate, clauses of at most 3 literals that force it to
equal the value of the gate, and a clause that makes the output true give a 3-CNF Γ with O(n)
variables and O(n) clauses. Each S of size s extends to exactly one satisfying assignment of Γ,
and no other S to any. Add the clause {¬xu , ¬xv } for each edge uv; Corollary 6 then counts
the independent sets of size s. The cliques of size s are the independent sets of size s of the
22                                                CLAUDE

complement graph, and the vertex covers of size s are the complements of the independent sets
of size n − s. The proper colorings correspond one to one to the satisfying assignments of a
k-CNF on kn variables, which says that each vertex has exactly one color and that the ends of
each edge have different colors. For dominating sets add to Γ, for each vertex v, the clause
that consists of xv and of xu for all neighbors u of v, and, for an integer w ≥ 3, shorten these
clauses as Calabro, Impagliazzo and Paturi do [CIP06, §3]: while a clause has more than w
literals, pick w of them and branch, either deleting its other literals or setting the w literals to
false. Each satisfying assignment survives in exactly one branch. A path to a leaf takes the
first branch at most n times and the second, which sets w more of the variables xv , at most
n/w times, so there are 2O(n log w/w) leaves, each a w-CNF on the variables not yet set. Choose
w so large that the number of leaves is at most 2δn/2 , apply Corollary 6 to each leaf, and add
up. All these formulas have O(n) variables, so Corollary 6, applied with a small enough value
in place of δ, gives time O(2δn ).                                                                ■

Appendix C. Counting satisfying assignments as a function of n (proof sketch)
  Theorem A(d) only decides, because the formulas produced by the sparsification in its
proof may share satisfying assignments. Dell et al. [DHMTW14, Appendix A] change the
sparsification algorithm so that they share none (Lemma 4). Their Lemma A.1 gives no explicit
bound on the number of clauses, so we make the analogous change in the algorithm of Calabro
[Cal09, Figure 5.1 and Theorem 5.15], which the proof of Theorem A(d) uses.
Theorem C. One deterministic algorithm computes the number of satisfying assignments of a
                                              α/10 )
3-CNF on n ≥ 2 variables in time 2O(n/(log n)        .
Proof sketch. First delete every clause that contains a variable together with its negation; this
does not change the satisfying assignments. Given a 3-CNF ψ and ε ∈ (0, 1], the algorithm of
Calabro [Cal09, Figure 5.1] deletes every clause that properly contains another clause, which
does not change the satisfying assignments either, and then either outputs ψ, or selects a
nonempty set H of literals and a set F ⊆ ψ of clauses that properly contain H and calls
itself on ψ ∪ {H} and on ψ ∪ Q, where Q = {E \ H : E ∈ F }. We carry along a set N of
clauses with one literal each, empty at the start, which the algorithm never reads: the first
call receives N , the second N ∪ {{¬ω} : ω ∈ H}, where ¬ω is the negation of the literal ω;
and where the algorithm outputs ψ we output ψ ∪ N . An assignment of the n variables that
satisfies ψ ∪ N satisfies ψ ∪ {H} ∪ N if it makes some literal of H true; otherwise it satisfies
ψ ∪ Q ∪ N ∪ {{¬ω} : ω ∈ H}, because each E ∈ F has a true literal, which lies in E \ H. No
assignment satisfies both, since the second contains {¬ω} for every ω ∈ H and the first contains
the clause H. Conversely, each of the two contains ψ ∪ N , so an assignment that satisfies either
of them satisfies ψ ∪ N . By induction over the recursion, each satisfying assignment of the input
satisfies exactly one output, and no other assignment satisfies any. Since no choice depends on
N , the tree of recursive calls and the formula ψ at each leaf are those of Calabro’s algorithm
[Cal09]: there are at most 2εn outputs, produced in time 2εn times a polynomial in n that does
not depend on ε, and each has at most (M (ε) + 2)n clauses, as there are only 2n clauses with
one literal. If an output Φ has m ≥ 2 clauses, Theorem A(c), applied after the v variables that
occur in Φ have been renamed to x1 , . . . , xv , computes the number Z of §6, and Φ has Z · 2n−v
satisfying assignments; with no clause it has 2n , and with one clause of j literals (2j − 1)2n−j .
Sum over the outputs. The time is bounded as in the proof of Theorem A(d), with M (ε) + 2 in
place of M (ε), and ε = (log n)−α/10 gives the theorem.                                         ■

# The two main results of the accompanying paper, as Lean statements

This file has definitions only: no theorem, no proof. It imports Mathlib and nothing else, so it
can be read, and compiled, on its own. Two of its definitions are the claims:
                                   A 2o(n) TIME ALGORITHM FOR 3-SAT                                          41

* `ETHIsFalse`: the Exponential Time Hypothesis, `ETH`, is false.
* `MainTheorem`: one machine counts the satisfying assignments of every k-CNF (no clause written
  twice) with k ≥ 1 and m ≥ 2 clauses on n ≥ 4 variables x₀ … x_{n−1} (the paper's x₁ … xₙ ), each of
  which occurs, within 2^{C · min{km/√(log m), k² · n · log log n/√(log n)}} steps (log is log₂ ), for one
  constant C that does not depend on k.

A proof is a separate file that imports this one and states theorems whose types are exactly these
two propositions.

**The machine.** An algorithm is a machine of Mathlib's kind `Turing.FinTM2`: a deterministic
machine with finitely many stacks in place of tapes. Its program is a finite set of labelled
blocks; it has finitely many internal states; on a stack it can push a symbol, pop the top symbol,
or read it. `Turing.TM2ComputableAux Bool Bool` adds a bijection from the alphabet of the input
stack to Bool = {false, true}, and one from the alphabet of the output stack; bits are written
through them. Time is the number of steps. One step runs the current block until it jumps to a
block (possibly itself) or halts; for a fixed machine this is a bounded number of stack operations.
`Turing.TM2OutputsInTime M.tm x (some y) t` says that the machine, started with x on its input
stack and all other stacks empty, halts after at most t steps with y on its output stack, all other
stacks empty, and its internal state back at its initial value. Of the stack alphabets, Mathlib
requires only that of the input stack to be finite; the condition `∀ i, Finite (M.tm.Γ i)`, used
below, requires every stack alphabet to be finite, as for a textbook machine.

**Why this machine suffices.** A random-access machine simulates such a machine with a bounded
number of operations per step, and it can rewrite a formula on the paper's variables x₁ … xₙ into
the input words used here (with x₀ … x_{n−1}) in time polynomial in n and the length of the
formula. In the paper a formula is a set of clauses, so no clause is repeated in it. For ETH: a
3-CNF on n variables then has length polynomial in n. So, for δ > 0, a machine here that runs in
time O(2^{δn}) gives an algorithm in the usual sense that runs in time O(2^{δn}). Hence s₃ (defined
below) for algorithms in the usual sense is at most s₃ as defined here, and s₃ = 0 here gives
s₃ = 0 there.
For the Main Theorem: every variable occurs and no clause is repeated, so n ≤ km and m ≤ (2n+1)^k;
then the formula has O(km · log n) bits, and every polynomial in that length is at most
2^{O(min{km/√(log m), k² · n · log log n/√(log n)})}. So a machine as in `MainTheorem` gives an
algorithm in the usual sense within the same bound, with another constant. The simulation and the
rewriting are standard facts; they are not part of the Lean statements or proofs.

/-! ### Formulas -/

/-- A literal on x₀ … x_{n−1} is a pair (i, b): it is x ᵢ if b is true and ¬x ᵢ if b is false. -/
abbrev Lit (n : ℕ) : Type := Fin n × Bool

/-- A clause is the OR of a list of literals; a formula is the AND of a list of clauses. -/
abbrev CNF (n : ℕ) : Type := List (List (Lit n))

/-- An instance of k-SAT: every clause has at most k literals, and no clause is written twice. A
clause is a list: it may be empty (then it is false), may repeat a literal or hold both x and ¬x,
and the same literals in another order make a different clause. So a k-CNF on n variables has at
most (2n+1)^k clauses. (Without this, no time bound in n alone could hold: before halting, a machine
must pop nearly all of its input, a bounded number of symbols per step.) -/
def IsKCNF (k : ℕ) {n : ℕ} (φ : CNF n) : Prop := (∀ C ∈ φ, C.length ≤ k) ∧ φ.Nodup

/-- The number written for one position of a clause: 0 for an empty position, 2i+1 for ¬x ᵢ ,
2i+2 for x ᵢ . -/
def slot {n : ℕ} : Option (Lit n) → ℕ
  | none => 0
  | some l => 2 * l.1.val + 1 + l.2.toNat

/-! ### The Exponential Time Hypothesis -/
42                                               CLAUDE

/-- Some assignment makes a literal of every clause true. -/
def Satisfiable {n : ℕ} (φ : CNF n) : Prop := ∃ a : Fin n → Bool, ∀ C ∈ φ, ∃ l ∈ C, a l.1 = l.2

/-- The input word for k-SAT with k fixed, n in unary: 1ⁿ 0, then for each clause k positions of
`Nat.log2 (2 * n) + 1` bits each (⌊log₂ (2n)⌋ + 1 bits; 1 bit when n = 0), each holding a `slot`
number, lowest bit first. A clause with fewer than k literals is padded with empty positions (0). -/
def encClauses (k : ℕ) {n : ℕ} (φ : CNF n) : List Bool :=
  List.replicate n true ++ false :: φ.flatMap fun C => (List.range k).flatMap fun j =>
    (List.range (Nat.log2 (2 * n) + 1)).map (slot C[j]?).testBit

/-- `M` decides k-SAT within t(n) steps: started on `encClauses k φ`, for any n and any k-CNF φ on
x₀ … x_{n−1} with any number of clauses, it halts after at most t(n) steps with one bit as output,
which is 1 if and only if φ is satisfiable. Here k is fixed and is not a part of the input. -/
def DecidesKSat (k : ℕ) (M : TM2ComputableAux Bool Bool) (t : ℕ → ℕ) : Prop :=
  ∀ (n : ℕ) (φ : CNF n), IsKCNF k φ → ∃ b : Bool, (b = true ↔ Satisfiable φ) ∧
    Nonempty (TM2OutputsInTime M.tm ((encClauses k φ).map M.inputAlphabet.invFun)
      (some ([b].map M.outputAlphabet.invFun)) (t n))

/-- “Some deterministic algorithm decides k-SAT on n variables in time O(2^{δn})”: one machine,
finite alphabets, one constant c; the machine decides k-SAT within c · 2^{δn} steps, rounded down,
for every n. The machine and c may depend on k and on δ. -/
def InTime (k : ℕ) (δ : ℝ≥0) : Prop :=
  ∃ (M : TM2ComputableAux Bool Bool) (c : ℕ), (∀ i, Finite (M.tm.Γ i)) ∧
    DecidesKSat k M fun n => ⌊(c : ℝ) * (2 : ℝ) ^ ((δ : ℝ) * n)⌋₊

/-- s_k: the infimum of all δ ≥ 0 such that some deterministic algorithm decides k-SAT on n
variables in time O(2^{δn}). It is a number in [0, ∞], and ∞ if there is no such δ. Only s₃ is
used below. -/
noncomputable def s (k : ℕ) : ℝ≥0∞ := ⨅ (δ : ℝ≥0) (_ : InTime k δ), (δ : ℝ≥0∞)

/-- **The Exponential Time Hypothesis** (Impagliazzo and Paturi, “On the complexity of k-SAT”,
J. Comput. Syst. Sci. 62 (2001)), in the form s₃ > 0: for some δ > 0 no deterministic algorithm
decides 3-SAT on n variables in time O(2^{δn}). The paper that this file accompanies defines ETH as
“s_k > 0 for all k ≥ 3” and notes that this is the same as s₃ > 0. The form here follows from that
one: take k = 3, then use that s₃ for algorithms in the usual sense is at most s₃ as defined here
(see the head comment); so its negation refutes both. (A remark outside Lean: a deterministic
algorithm is a randomized algorithm that uses no random bits, so allowing randomness could only make
s₃ smaller, and refuting this deterministic form also refutes the randomized form.) -/
def ETH : Prop := 0 < s 3

/-- **The Exponential Time Hypothesis is false**: not s₃ > 0. As s₃ ≥ 0, this says s₃ = 0. It is
equivalent to: for every δ > 0 some deterministic algorithm decides 3-SAT on n variables in time
O(2^{δn}) (`InTime 3 δ`, which gets weaker as δ grows). -/
def ETHIsFalse : Prop := ¬ ETH

/-! ### The Main Theorem -/

/-- Every one of the variables x₀ … x_{n−1} occurs in φ, positively or negatively. -/
def AllOccur {n : ℕ} (φ : CNF n) : Prop := ∀ i : Fin n, ∃ C ∈ φ, ∃ l ∈ C, l.1 = i

/-- The number of satisfying assignments of φ, among all 2ⁿ assignments to x₀ … x_{n−1}. -/
def numSat {n : ℕ} (φ : CNF n) : ℕ :=
  (Finset.univ.filter fun a : Fin n → Bool => ∀ C ∈ φ, ∃ l ∈ C, a l.1 = l.2).card

/-- The lowest n + 1 bits of the number c, lowest bit first. (A count of assignments of n variables
is at most 2ⁿ , so nothing of it is lost.) -/
def binary (n c : ℕ) : List Bool := (List.range (n + 1)).map c.testBit

/-- The input word of the Main Theorem, with k as a part of it: 1ᵏ 0, then `encClauses k φ`. -/
def encInput (k : ℕ) {n : ℕ} (φ : CNF n) : List Bool :=
  List.replicate k true ++ false :: encClauses k φ

/-- The paper's log: the logarithm to base 2, not rounded, and of x itself, not of x + 1. In the
comments of this file, log always means this. Under the conditions of `MainTheorem` (m ≥ 2, n ≥ 4)
it is applied only to numbers that are at least 2. -/
                                   A 2o(n) TIME ALGORITHM FOR 3-SAT                                         43

noncomputable def lg (x : ℝ) : ℝ := Real.logb 2 x

/-- The exponent in the number m of clauses: k · m/√(log m). -/
noncomputable def expM (k m : ℕ) : ℝ := (k : ℝ) * m / Real.sqrt (lg m)

/-- The exponent in the number n of variables: k² · n · log log n/√(log n). -/
noncomputable def expN (k n : ℕ) : ℝ := (k : ℝ) ^ 2 * n * lg (lg n) / Real.sqrt (lg n)

/-- The time of the Main Theorem, rounded down: 2^{C · min{km/√(log m), k² · n · log log n/√(log n)}}. -/
noncomputable def timeMin (C k m n : ℕ) : ℕ :=
  ⌊(2 : ℝ) ^ ((C : ℝ) * min (expM k m) (expN k n))⌋₊

/-- `M`, started on `encInput k φ`, halts after at most t steps with output the number of
satisfying assignments of φ, written in n + 1 bits, lowest bit first (`binary`). -/
def CountsWithin (M : TM2ComputableAux Bool Bool) (k : ℕ) {n : ℕ} (φ : CNF n) (t : ℕ) : Prop :=
  Nonempty (TM2OutputsInTime M.tm ((encInput k φ).map M.inputAlphabet.invFun)
    (some ((binary n (numSat φ)).map M.outputAlphabet.invFun)) t)

/-- **The Main Theorem.** There is one machine with finite alphabets, and one constant C, both
chosen before k and the formula, such that: for every k ≥ 1, every n ≥ 4, and every formula φ on the
variables x₀ … x_{n−1}, each of which occurs in it, with `IsKCNF k φ` (clauses of at most k
literals, none written twice) and m ≥ 2 clauses, the machine, started on `encInput k φ`, outputs
the number of satisfying assignments of φ within
2^{C · min{km/√(log m), k² · n · log log n/√(log n)}} steps (rounded down). Where no thresholds are
stated, an O( · ) as in the bound 2^{O(…)} speaks only of all large enough m and n; here one C
serves every m ≥ 2 and n ≥ 4, which implies that reading. These are the least values with
log m ≥ 1 and log log n ≥ 1. At n = 2 the factor log log n is 0, so the bound would be one step.
At m = 1 the divisor √(log m) is 0, so km/√(log m) is undefined (Lean's convention x/0 = 0 would
make the bound one step there too). And k ≥ 1 because the paper defines k-CNFs only for k ≥ 1; with
k = 0 every clause is empty, so there is at most one clause and no variable occurs.
Nothing is said about any other input word, including words for k = 0, for formulas with a
repeated clause or with a variable that does not occur, and for formulas with fewer than two
clauses or fewer than four variables. -/
def MainTheorem : Prop :=
  ∃ (M : TM2ComputableAux Bool Bool) (C : ℕ), (∀ i, Finite (M.tm.Γ i)) ∧
    ∀ (k n : ℕ) (φ : CNF n), 1 ≤ k → IsKCNF k φ → 2 ≤ φ.length → 4 ≤ n → AllOccur φ →
        CountsWithin M k φ (timeMin C k φ.length n)

                         Appendix G. Related work in more detail
   Before this work, the fastest randomized algorithms known ran in time O(1.308n ) (O(20.3874n ))
for 3-SAT and 2(1−µk /(k−1))n+o(n) for k-SAT, with constants µk that increase to π 2 /6 [PPSZ05,
Her14]; later work has lowered the exponents only minutely [SS17, HKZZ19, Sch21]. The
fastest deterministic ones ran in time O(1.32793n ) (O(20.4092n )) for 3-SAT and slightly below
(2 − 2/k)n+o(n) for k-SAT [MS11, Liu18].
   The closest work we know is Austrin, Kaski and Kubjas [AKK22]. Their object contains
ours: an index on every vertex of a hypergraph, an arbitrary table on every hyperedge, and the
sum, over all values of the indices, of the product of the table entries. For the (K, λ)-clique
the vertices are the K2 pairs of nodes (indices in {0, 1}λ ) and the hyperedges are the K stars
                         

(all pairs containing a node w; table Ww ). For algorithms that execute one tensor network
along one contraction tree, they prove an upper bound via fast matrix multiplication and
an unconditional lower bound of Ω (2λ )bw operations. Here bw is the branchwidth of the
hypergraph: over all trees with the hyperedges as leaves and inner nodes of degree 3, the
minimum of the largest number of vertices shared by the two sides of a tree edge. Every such
tree has an edge with about K/3 or more stars on each side, and these share about 2K 2 /9
or more pairs (our calculation, not in their paper [AKK22]); so in their model (K, e) is not
achievable for any e below about 29 , which is 49 of the e = 12 of brute force. The algorithm of

Why eight. For strings of λ bits, Definition 5 uses the identity once for each block of three
bits; do the same with blocks of log N bits. The proof of Lemma 2 counts the bits of a piece Ai
in a simple way: it factors R or C in each block, and it names the set of the blocks that take C,
with one more bit for each block in each direction. By this count, in the two directions together,
a block takes the logarithms of the two ranks of R and 2 more bits, against 4 log N bits for the
crossing. Row-wise and column-wise, R has the ranks 2 and 2 for N = 2, the ranks 4 and 16 for
N = 4, and the ranks 8 and 64 for N = 8. This makes 1 + 1 + 2 = 4 of 4 bits, 2 + 4 + 2 = 8 of 8
bits, and 3 + 6 + 2 = 11 of 12 bits. So by this count, of the three identities only the eight-square
identity leaves a gain, the ρ = 11/12 of Lemma 2. Hrubeš tensors the same identity block by
block and counts squares rather than bits [Hru24, Section 5.2]; see Appendix G.
