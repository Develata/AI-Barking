AI Layoffs Are Exploding in 2026: Over 93,000 Tech Jobs Gone, Microsoft Targets 60,000 More

AI-linked layoffs are mounting across major companies, including Microsoft, as businesses turn to automation and AI to cut costs, improve efficiency and reshape their workforces.

AI layoffs are rising as Microsoft, Oracle, Meta and Barclays cut jobs, but the bigger question is whether AI productivity gains will drive more job cuts.
magnific.com

AI layoffs are accelerating in 2026, with 93,217 tech employees laid off in 116 AI-attributed layoff events, while Microsoft is reportedly preparing buyouts for 60,000 workers and Barclays is cutting around 5,000 jobs as it expands its use of artificial intelligence (AI).

But the emerging AI jobs [https://www.ibtimes.sg/big-tech-layoff-microsoft-axes-4800-jobs-ai-push-xbox-shake-redefine-tech-giant-89221] story is more complicated than a simple replacement narrative. Researchers say the technology has not yet triggered widespread layoffs across the economy, even as they warn that the biggest employment shock could still be years away.

The numbers are becoming harder for companies and workers to ignore. Layoffs.fyi data shows 93,217 tech employees have been laid off due to AI in 2026, across 116 tech layoff events attributed to AI.

Layoffs.fyi

At the same time, companies are investing heavily in AI infrastructure [https://www.ibtimes.sg/big-ai-faces-national-backlash-over-140-gcc-protests-across-42-states-challenge-data-center-expansion-90080] and automation, creating a strange economic transition in which some jobs are disappearing while others are being created to build and support AI systems.

Microsoft is among the biggest examples. According to recent reports, Microsoft is apparently offering buyouts to 60,000 employees in an effort to reduce costs amid the rising use of artificial intelligence. The reported offer is the latest in a series of workforce reductions at the company this year and includes some of Microsoft's most recognizable divisions.

Microsoft, Oracle and Barclays Put AI at the Center of Workforce Restructuring

Microsoft announced in April that it was offering voluntary retirement to around 7% of its US workforce, which stood at approximately 125,000 employees at the time. Its global workforce was about 223,000.

The latest reported buyout offer would involve divisions including Xbox, Windows and Copilot, Microsoft's AI tool, according to Bloomberg. Employees in areas including cybersecurity, data centers [https://www.ibtimes.sg/meta-doubles-down-50-billion-louisiana-ai-data-center-despite-water-environmental-concerns-89701] and marketing would reportedly not be eligible.

"This offer gives eligible employees who may be considering a future outside the company a new choice to make that transition now with support. It also gives participating organizations another way to adapt their workforce to changing needs," Amy Coleman, Microsoft's executive vice-president and chief people officer, said in a statement.

The Microsoft development is part of a wider technology-sector restructuring. Meta announced plans in April to cut 10% of its personnel, or about 8,000 employees, to boost efficiency.

Amazon [https://www.ibtimes.sg/amazon-deploys-1-million-robots-what-does-that-mean-its-1-5-million-workers-95264]has announced several workforce cuts, most recently in its retail division.

Oracle [https://www.ibtimes.sg/oracle-ai-layoffs-meta-workforce-cuts-amazon-restructuring-microsoft-workforce-changes-signal-88452] shed nearly 13% of its workforce, cutting 21,000 jobs, in June, with the cuts reported as a result of AI.

But the scale of the reductions does not automatically mean every job being eliminated is being directly replaced by an AI system. It remains unclear whether workforce reductions have translated into greater efficiency, while some companies have scaled back layoff plans.

That uncertainty is particularly important because the broader evidence suggests companies may be moving faster on AI restructuring than they are on extracting measurable productivity gains from the technology.

A recent National Bureau of Economic Research working paper found that, among 9,000 senior executives surveyed around the world, 90% said AI had yet to result in greater productivity at their companies.

Barclays offers another example of the complicated relationship between AI and layoffs.

Barclays is cutting around 5,000 jobs, or roughly 5% of its workforce, this year as the British bank uses technology including artificial intelligence to reduce fixed costs and improve returns.

The cuts came as Barclays reported third-quarter profit of £2.3 billion ($3.08 billion), up 12% from a year earlier but below analysts' expectations.

The bank said it took a further £201 million in "structural cost actions" during the third quarter as part of a longer-term plan to reduce expenses, including staff costs, through greater use of technology.

Around 60% of the planned job cuts will affect support functions such as developers, according to CFO Anna Cross, making Barclays one of the clearest examples of how AI and automation could reduce headcount in technology-heavy roles.

Cross said around 1,000 roles had already been eliminated, with the remaining cuts expected by the end of the year. The scale and nature of the reductions underline the growing pressure on banks to use automation to reduce their reliance on human workers in support operations.

For Barclays, the latest figures show that the AI-driven workforce shift is not limited to routine administrative roles.

With developers and other support-function employees accounting for a large share of the cuts, the bank's restructuring offers a glimpse of how AI adoption could increasingly affect white-collar and technology jobs that were previously considered more insulated from automation.

The warning extends beyond technology and banking. Fortune, citing Pantheon Macroeconomics' Samuel Tombs and Oliver Allen, reported that AI is causing US job losses [https://www.ibtimes.sg/billions-spent-thousands-laid-off-now-mark-zuckerberg-says-metas-ai-agents-are-lagging-89007] at a rate of more than 10,000 roles per month.

At the same time, construction employment has increased because technology companies are building data centers to support AI, while jobs in accounting, advertising, office administration and legal services are slowly disappearing.

It was also reported that 9% of workers could lose their jobs during the "great AI transition".

But the broader labor market does not yet resemble an economy-wide employment collapse.

The Harvard Gazette pointed to several reassuring indicators. The US unemployment rate is around 4.1%, while Accenture says 52% of top executives expect AI to increase entry-level hiring rather than decrease it.

At the same time, entry-level job postings have reportedly fallen by as much as 73% in four years, by some counts.

The conflicting signals explain why the AI jobs debate remains unsettled.

Researchers Warn Today's Layoffs May Be Only the Beginning

Harvard Kennedy School economist Doug Elmendorf argues that the current labor market may tell us very little about the eventual consequences of AI.

"What we've seen so far in the labor market from artificial intelligence has very little predictive power for what we're going to see in the labor market because of AI in five years, or 10 years, or 15 years," Elmendorf said.

Elmendorf, Karen Dynan and Louise Sheiner of the Brookings Institution examined four possible scenarios for AI's economic impact in an NBER working paper. Those scenarios range from a moderate GDP boost with little reduction in employment to much faster GDP growth accompanied by persistently high unemployment.

The researchers did not say which scenario is most likely. But Elmendorf said significant job losses are plausible over the coming decades.

"I think it's very likely that there will be significant job losses from artificial intelligence in the coming couple of decades, but that most — not all, but most — of those people will be able to find new work," he said.

Their scenario could involve three million people, or roughly 2% of the labor force, being out of work at any given time, potentially affecting many millions of people over several decades.

Joseph Fuller of Harvard Business School offers another explanation for why the predicted disruption has not arrived at full scale: companies are still figuring out how to use AI effectively.

His work with Accenture Research found that 41% of all work tasks can currently be automated or augmented by AI.

Yet only around one-third of companies' AI experiments are successful, potentially because of poor data inputs or employees who do not understand how to use the technology.

"Companies are not training people formally, so employees are basically using it like glorified Google, and they're not getting much productivity out of it," Fuller said, adding, "Maybe the boss says, 'Well, this isn't as good as I was hoping, I guess we shouldn't worry too much about this.'"

Fuller expects that to change as cheaper and more specialized AI agents become available.

Raffaella Sadun of Harvard Business School warns companies against treating AI-driven workforce reductions as an easy path to savings.

"For the first time we have a technology that learns, sometimes autonomously, that allows us to have access to expertise that has previously been dispersed," Sadun said.

Executives wonder whether AI can automate away the need to invest in human capital, she added. "I think that's a tremendous trap, a terrible trap."

Her argument is that employees possess tacit, company-specific knowledge that cannot always be captured by a job description. Her Digital Reskilling Lab works with companies to retrain workers for AI-augmented roles.

"At the lab, we see investments in human capital and training as strategic investments, meaning these are things you should do not because you want to be nice or corporate-social-responsibility-compliant, but because knowledge plays an important role in your company, and investment in human capital is part of how you create knowledge," Sadun said.

Fuller also argues that companies should rethink entry-level roles rather than eliminate them completely. At one large technology company, he helped executives redesign junior software engineering jobs so new hires would receive structured exposure to sales and product management.

"So rather than stopping hiring junior software engineers, we changed the job description so those hires are still doing some software engineering, but they're also getting regular structured introductions to sales and product management," he said and noted, "We can pre-position what we want 10 years from now by getting them the experience their current bosses didn't get."

That could become one of the defining features of the AI transition: entry-level jobs may not disappear entirely, but they could look very different.

For workers, however, the biggest warning comes from the gap between corporate incentives and the broader labor market.

"Individual companies are going to be focused on what's good for their company, and yelling at them to do something different can matter a little bit but it won't fundamentally change their desire to produce goods and services at the lowest possible cost. And for a number of companies, that will mean the use of AI instead of workers for some things," Elmendorf highlighted.

The researchers therefore argue that policymakers should prepare for a larger transition through measures including worker retraining, unemployment insurance changes, shorter work weeks, subsidized employment and wage insurance.

As of now, AI is already being linked to tens of thousands of layoffs, with Microsoft, Oracle, Meta and Barclays among the companies restructuring their workforces.

But the question is whether traditional companies will make more and more job cuts to achieve their desired productivity gains or find a way to balance AI-driven efficiency with their existing workforces.
