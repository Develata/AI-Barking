Anthropic Warns Small AI Models Could Self-Replicate Across Devices by End of 2026

Anthropic's Frontier Red Team says AI models are increasingly capable of completing replication tasks, raising concerns about autonomous worms exploiting vulnerable IoT devices.

By Bhaswati Guha Majumder [/reporters/bhaswati-guha-majumder]

Anthropic warns small AI models could soon self-replicate across vulnerable devices, potentially creating autonomous AI worms that spread through insecure IoT networks.

Small AI models could be only months away from becoming capable of self-replicating across vulnerable internet-connected devices, potentially turning smart ovens, Android TV boxes and other consumer IoT hardware into hosts for autonomous AI worms, according to Anthropic's Frontier Red Team.

"A replicator like this would be challenging to shut down, since there's no centralized point to shut off," the company said in its October 12 research post.

The Anthropic [https://www.ibtimes.sg/could-ai-finally-give-cyber-defenders-edge-anthropic-sees-shift-within-two-years-94861] team said current experiments show that models are increasingly capable of completing the individual steps required for autonomous replication, raising the possibility of "a slow, distributed 'digital slime mold'" spreading across insecure computing infrastructure.

"Our experiments suggest that small AI models may be months away from being capable enough to self-replicate on commercial internet-of-things (IoT) devices," said the Anthropic research post.

AI Worms Could Spread Without a Central Shutdown Point

Anthropic's researchers examined autonomous replication and adaptation (ARA), in which an AI model deploys and propagates itself without human control. Unlike conventional malware, an AI worm could potentially adapt to unfamiliar devices and hardware rather than relying entirely on pre-programmed attack paths.

The researchers found that a nine-billion-parameter model successfully got inference running on eight of nine hardware types tested. Models with 30 billion parameters or fewer can already perform many of the individual steps required for replication, although they still make mistakes when combining those steps. In simplified evaluations, GLM-5.2 and stronger models were capable of end-to-end replication.

Anthropic estimated that if smaller models continue improving at their historical rate, they could reach the capability level needed for replication by the end of 2026, with small weight-based replicators potentially possible soon afterward.

The researchers demonstrated that inexpensive consumer hardware can provide meaningful computing capacity. They ran Gemma4-26B-A4B across 15 off-brand Android TV boxes costing about $55 each, with the model distributed across the devices. Typical Android TV boxes in this category have between 1GB and 4GB of RAM.

From Vulnerable Smart TVs to an Autonomous AI Botnet

Anthropic's researchers built a firewalled test environment containing eight Google Cloud nodes running Android 15 emulators. A seeded worm moved from node to node through an unauthenticated ADB port, transferred its payload and set up an inference server.

GLM-5.2 was able to propagate Llama3.1-8B effectively in the simplified evaluation. However, Anthropic stressed that the cyber-exploit element was deliberately made trivial, meaning the result represents an upper bound rather than a demonstration of real-world success.

The researchers estimated that replication could initially be slow. Using their demonstrated hardware speeds, a nine-billion replication could take more than one to two days. But they warned that future replicators could exploit different types of low-security computing and deploy models of varying sizes.

A key difference from traditional botnets is adaptability. AI botnets would not necessarily be tied to one hardware type and could potentially adjust to unfamiliar environments. Anthropic said models could also pass prompts, code and notes to successor instances, allowing adaptations to be inherited as the system spreads.

Anthropic said there is "not yet" a full rogue replicator, but researchers are seeing "signs of life for AI worms." Daniel Freeman similarly warned that small, capable models could eventually "gobble up vulnerable compute if they want to."

The researchers said defenders should harden vulnerable systems while preparing for a future in which autonomous AI replicators may become persistent.
