Small AI models could self-replicate on IoT devices within months, Anthropic warns

Oct 12, 2026, 9:58 PM UTC•

Lukas Brandt [/author/lukas-brandt]
•5 min read

Share this article

Save

Anthropic's Frontier Red Team found that 9B-30B parameter models can already complete individual replication steps on consumer hardware, and projects that models capable of full autonomous replication will emerge by late 2026.

Anthropic researchers have demonstrated that small AI models can run distributed inference on networks of cheap consumer devices — and they're months away from stitching together the full replication chain without human help.

The Frontier Red Team ran a 26-billion-parameter model across 15 off-brand Android TV boxes purchased for roughly $55 each. The cluster served tokens at 0.39 per second, slow but functional. Each box held a fraction of the model's layers, communicating over a local network to complete forward passes.

"We're seeing signs of life for AI worms," the team wrote. "Like coding agents in 2024, we're seeing agents become increasingly capable of completing the individual steps in the replication chain."

The replication chain breaks into four stages: finding targets, gaining execution, establishing inference, and propagating from the new host. Earlier work (CyberGym, ExploitBench, adaptive worms) already showed small models can handle the first two. Anthropic focused on inference setup — the least studied and most technically demanding step.

In controlled evaluations, a 9B-parameter model (Qwen3.5-9B) successfully stood up inference servers on eight of nine hardware types tested, from Jetson Nanos to Macs and RTX 4090s. GLM-5.2, a stronger model, completed the full end-to-end cycle: scanning a network, accessing nodes via unauthenticated ADB, delivering payloads, and starting inference on each new host. It replicated across all eight test nodes in four of five trials.

No other open-weight model tested achieved reliable end-to-end replication. But the capability trend line is steep. Plotting model capability (Epoch Capabilities Index) against release date shows a gain of 13.7 ECI points per year (R² = 0.95). At that rate, a small model matching GLM-5.2's replication competence arrives by end of 2026.

"If small models improve at their historical rate, they'll match this capability level by the end of this year," the researchers wrote. "Small weight-based replicators may be possible soon after."

A weight-based replicator carries its model weights as payload. Unlike traditional malware, it doesn't need a command-and-control server. It runs inference directly on compromised hardware — smart ovens, robot vacuums, lawnmowers, security cameras, video-conferencing units. Any device with 1-4GB of RAM can host a shard of a sub-30B model. Ten to fifteen such devices can serve a 26B model.

The Kimwolf botnet, which infected over two million primarily Android TV devices, proves the target pool exists. These boxes typically run unhardened forks of Android Open Source Project. Manufacturers don't add security features.

Spread would be slow. On the TV-box cluster, a full 32k-context inference setup trajectory takes 71-150 hours for Gemma4-26B-A4B, 28-51 hours for Qwen3.5-9B. Doubling time: days. But the worm persists. No central off switch. Re-infection after reboot is trivial on devices without secure boot.

Unlike hardcoded botnets (Mirai, Kimwolf), an AI worm isn't tied to one device class. The same model set up inference on Jetson Nanos, Macs, and Nvidia GPUs. It adapts. In generational experiments, Qwen3.5-9B instances that inherited notes from predecessors improved inference-setup success from 45% to 61-84% over six generations — Lamarckian evolution in real time.

Access to a frontier "advisor" model (Opus 4.8) didn't help. Small models couldn't ask productive questions. The researchers expect coordination to flow the other way: a large model handling strategy, spinning up small models as subagents.

Defenders have some advantages. A replicator moving multi-gigabyte payloads and sustaining device-to-device inference traffic creates network anomalies visible in ISP flow monitoring. Faster replication means more noise. Honeypots can capture payloads for analysis. The Mirai takedown — ISPs quarantining infected subscribers, restricting traffic to a whitelist, redirecting browsers to cleanup pages — cleared 92% of infections in 14 days. Similar tactics would work here.

Manufacturers can shrink the attack surface. UK regulations now ban default IoT passwords. Secure boot, signed firmware, and minimal attack surfaces raise the bar for both scripted and AI-driven botnets.

Edge accelerators change the math. Nvidia Jetson AGX Orin 64GB serves Qwen3-30B-A3B at 76.7 tokens/second; AGX Thor hits 226. Roughly 9.5 million Jetson-equipped units exist across ~90 product lines — smart cameras, conferencing hardware, robots, drones. Most sit behind outbound management tunnels, not inbound ports. Historical mass compromises haven't happened. But the theoretical compute ceiling is 9,000-35,000 H100-equivalents.

"We should aim to harden our systems to make it difficult for such replicators to spread," the team concluded, "but also prepare to deal with a world where they are here to stay."

The Memento harness, built atop the Pi coding agent, mitigated a common failure mode: agents fixating on wrong hypotheses for hours. Periodic fresh instances inheriting workspace and brief notes improved long-horizon reliability without granting new capabilities.

Pipeline parallelism, not tensor or expert parallelism, enables fault tolerance. A single node failure in tensor parallel kills the whole replica. Pipeline parallel lets a downed node be replaced, its KV cache recomputed from the prior node's forward pass.

The researchers emphasize this is a near-term threat model, not speculative. The floor (capability needed for reliable replication) and ceiling (model size that fits on target devices) don't intersect today. The trend line says they will within months.
