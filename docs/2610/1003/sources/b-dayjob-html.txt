Abstract
Professional work often starts with a brief request that leaves the professional to work out what is needed, which documents matter, and whether the request’s premise holds. We introduce DAYJOB, a benchmark of 130 tasks built by professionals in healthcare (50) and finance (80). The tasks are estimated to take a professional 13.6 hours on average in healthcare and 16.6 in finance. Each task is a containerized Harbor environment with an expert rubric of binary criteria (median 47.5 and 57.5 per task) that an agentic judge applies to the delivered files, and an attempt passes only if it meets every criterion. Across 30 model configurations from 13 developers, the strongest, Claude Opus 5.5, passes 24.7% of healthcare and 23.9% of finance attempts, and the median configuration passes 0.6% and 2.5%. In case studies, agents accept premises that the record contradicts and carry wrong inputs through otherwise consistent analyses. We release all healthcare tasks, 50 of the 80 finance tasks, the evaluation harness, and the leaderboard.
1
Introduction
Requests at work assume shared context. Someone preparing a new client’s first fund reports asks whether anything should stop the reports from going out today. An urgent-care clinician asks for a read on the labs before discharging a patient with “textbook Bell’s Palsy” on prednisone. Neither request mentions the problem it contains. In the fund’s reporting pack, an equity price quoted in South African cents on a Bloomberg screen was entered as rand, so a position worth about $260,000 appears to be worth $26 million. The patient’s chart records right-sided neck pain after moving heavy furniture, slightly slurred speech, and a facial examination that does not document the forehead or eyelid closure, and the task’s rubric requires ruling out an arterial dissection before any discharge. Both are DAYJOB tasks (Section
6
).

builds tasks from the work of experienced professionals and gives the model a detailed brief: prompts in its public gold subset average 337.2 words, and 75% of its tasks include at most one reference file. GDPval also reports that GPT-5 performed worse when the prompts omitted where to find data and how to approach the problem. DAYJOB pairs realistic requests of the kind a colleague would send with a workspace of the files they implicitly refer to, mostly PDFs, spreadsheets, and Word documents, and with a rubric of binary criteria that describes a competent deliverable. Rubrics credit actions the request leaves unsaid, such as recommending urgent transfer to a stroke-capable emergency department, and prohibit specific errors. Tasks run as containerized Harbor environments
[
7
]
with network access limited to the model provider’s API. After the agent finishes, an agentic judge in the same container opens the delivered files, recomputes values where needed, and returns a verdict with evidence for every criterion. An attempt passes only if every criterion is met.
We evaluate 30 configurations from 13 developers with five attempts per task. Claude Opus 5.5 (adaptive/max) leads both domains, passing 24.7% of healthcare and 23.9% of finance attempts. The median configuration passes 0.6% and 2.5%, and 19 configurations score below 5% in both domains. We release all 50 healthcare tasks, 50 of the 80 finance tasks, the harness, and the leaderboard.

3.2
Design principles
Requests.
The shortest healthcare request reads, in full, “Please draft a brief document for this case for the upcoming M&M.” Others name the deliverable and its format and leave the rest to the files: “Take a look at the IVIG denial. Find the root cause of the denial and draft the appeal you’d recommend. Give me both in .docx”. Longer requests add background or several deliverables. One asks for a physician-facing patient summary of at most 3,000 words, a family-facing version, and letters to the patient’s primary care pediatrician and to the subspecialists named in the chart. The preamble shared by all tasks (in each task’s
instruction.md
) tells the agent to infer its role, workplace, and deliverables from the files and to produce its best-supported output without asking for clarification, and the solvability review (Section
3.5
) checks that the files suffice.
Workspaces.
Input files are the documents the work draws on: charts and encounter notes, claims exports, laboratory reports, policies, vendor price files, general-ledger extracts, loan agreements, and email. Most are PDFs (80% in healthcare and 70% in finance), and most of the rest are spreadsheets and Word documents. Relevant and irrelevant material sit side by side: in the urgent-care task, seven of the nine PDFs are administrative forms, and the chart in a skilled-nursing task includes maintenance and fire-drill records. Workspaces also contain stale or conflicting sources, such as a 2019 site evaluation in a 2026 expansion proposal, or a bank memo’s 3.25% borrowing rate that the chief financial officer’s later chat message, supplied as a screenshot, raises to 5.25%. Some evidence exists only in images (Section
3.3
).
Rubrics.
Criteria are binary statements about the delivered work. Most check substance, often a value within a stated tolerance. Others check actions the request never mentions or the internal consistency of a set of deliverables. Prohibitions fail an attempt that makes a specific, plausible error, such as flagging a correctly priced post-split equity as a release blocker, recommending surgical debridement of a wound whose presentation suggests pyoderma gangrenosum, or fabricating results for tests that were never performed. We count a criterion as a prohibition when its main clause is negated, as in criteria that begin “Does not”. By this count, all 50 healthcare rubrics and 43 of the 50 public finance rubrics contain at least one prohibition, and prohibitions make up 16% and 8% of criteria.

Coverage.
Healthcare tasks include clinical decision support (discharge decisions, ICU admission notes, workup plans, medication reconciliation), pharmacy dispensing and parenteral nutrition, revenue-cycle and payer work (denial appeals, prior authorization, inpatient coding, contract negotiation), quality, safety, and compliance reviews (morbidity and mortality, a HIPAA breach audit, a trauma registry), and allied-health documentation. Finance tasks include valuation and corporate finance (discounted cash flow, accretion and dilution, capital structure), credit (term-loan requests, watchlist memos, covenant compliance), real estate and project finance (acquisition underwriting, construction draws), fund operations (net asset value, pricing review, rebalancing), treasury (cash forecasts, hedge close), and close and audit work (lease accounting, tie-out workpapers).
3.5
Construction, selection, and release
Tasks were created by professionals with firsthand experience of the work, among them a senior portfolio manager in institutional asset management, a finance professional who has structured debt for infrastructure and renewable-energy projects, and a pediatric endocrinologist
[
25
]
. Every task went through three layers of review for correctness, completeness, and solvability.
All 50 healthcare tasks and 50 of the 80 finance tasks are public on Hugging Face under the MIT license
[
26
]
, and the remaining finance tasks are available on request
[
25
]
. The harness is released under Apache 2.0, and the scripts behind every statistic, table, and figure in this paper are included as ancillary files. Each task directory holds the instruction, input files, rubric, and judge prompt, together with a benchmark-specific canary string and a SHA-256 hash of its file tree, and the dataset cards ask that tasks and their derivatives be excluded from training and from checkpoint selection.
4
Evaluation protocol
4.1
Harness
Every attempt runs the released harness: an agent loop built on the OpenHands Software Agent SDK
[
30
,
29
]
(version 1.43.1) that executes inside the task container through a Harbor adapter (Harbor 0.22.0). The agent has a terminal, a file editor, and a task tracker. Models with vision support can view image files through the editor, and images embedded in PDFs must first be extracted or rendered. The SDK’s finish and think tools are disabled, so an attempt ends at the first reply that contains text and no tool call. The instruction is the first user message, and the system prompt is the SDK’s default. When the history exceeds 240 events or the model’s input limit, a summarizing condenser that calls the evaluated model replaces older events with a summary. Each attempt is limited to 1,000 agent turns, 6 hours, and 600 seconds per model call. The container has 2 CPUs and 4 GB of memory and provides Python 3.12 with document libraries (openpyxl, python-docx, pdfplumber, PyMuPDF, and others), LibreOffice, Poppler, Tesseract OCR, and Ghostscript. Network egress is limited to the model provider’s API host, so web search, package installation, and lookup of the released tasks are unavailable.
4.2
Models and attempts
We evaluate the 30 configurations from 13 developers on the launch leaderboard of September 2026
[
27
]
(Table
3
). Parenthesized labels give the reasoning setting passed to the provider, and “adaptive/max” denotes adaptive thinking at maximum effort. Each configuration attempts every task five times, and Harbor reruns an attempt up to three times after a provider rate-limit error. Attempts that end in an error, such as a time-out or a provider failure, are excluded from their task’s mean.
4.3
Grading
After the agent phase, the verifier runs an agentic judge in the same container: the OpenCode agent
[
20
]
(version 1.18.31), run by rewardkit 0.1.7 with Claude Opus 4.8 as its model. In a single session the judge receives its instructions (
tests/prompt_template.md
in each task), the request, the names of the input files, and all criteria. It can open any file, run code, and read the agent’s trajectory, and it returns a binary verdict with reasoning for each criterion. The instructions tell it to grade the files the agent created or modified and, when the task calls for a written answer, the final message; to cite the file and content behind each verdict; to recompute values instead of trusting a formula or script; and to inspect rendered outputs. Claims in the final message about what a file contains are not evidence. The judge may take 90 seconds per criterion, with a minimum of 40 minutes.

7
Conclusion
DAYJOB tests whether agents can turn the kind of request a colleague would send in realistic workspaces into deliverables that meet an expert rubric in full. Its 130 tasks come from the work of healthcare and finance professionals and test whether an agent can decide what the task is, find the facts that matter, and recognize when a request’s premise is wrong.
The strongest of 30 model configurations passes 24.7% of healthcare and 23.9% of finance attempts, the median configuration passes 0.6% and 2.5%, and 19 configurations score below 5% in both domains. In the case studies, agents accept diagnoses and treatment plans that the record contradicts and build consistent analyses on wrong inputs. Because the judge returns a verdict with evidence for every criterion, each failed attempt shows which fact or decision the agent missed.
We release all healthcare tasks and 50 of the 80 finance tasks with the evaluation harness and the leaderboard, and will extend DAYJOB to further professional domains in the same format.
