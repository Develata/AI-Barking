                 Abstract. We disprove a conjecture of M. Kida by exhibiting a finite semiabelian group
                  of order 384 that is not monomial. The example is given by GAP’s SmallGroups library as

                    SmallGroup(384, 20127) which is isomorphic to the group ((C2 × ((C2 × Q8) ⋊ C2)) ⋊ C2) ⋊ C3.
                    We remark on the use of AI in this research, see Statement of AI Use.

                                                     1. Introduction

           Semiabelian groups were first established within inverse Galois theory by Saltman [Sal82],
       Thompson [Tho84], and Matzat [Mat87] as a subclass of finite solvable groups that play a role
       in the solvability of split embedding problems with abelian kernel. This was further explored by
       Stoll [Sto95] and Dentzer [Den95] who showed that semiabelian groups occur as the Galois groups
       over Hilbertian fields. In [Leg22], Legrand extended this result to division rings by showing that for
       every division ring H and every automorphism σ of finite order, all semiabelian groups occur as the
       Galois group over the skew field of fractions H(T, σ). It was over the course of these developments
       that the definition of semiabelian groups evolved into its present form:

       Definition 1.1. A finite group G is called semiabelian if it has a sequence

                                                    G0 = 1 ≤ G1 ≤ · · · ≤ Gn = G

       such that Gi+1 is a quotient group of Gi ⋉ Ai with a finite abelian group Ai for all i = 0, ..., n − 1.
           Elementary properties of semiabelian groups were derived naturally through the explicit deriva-

       tion of semiabelian groups as Galois groups, but still much remained unknown about their group
       structure. Kida [Kid25] conducted a more thorough investigation into their group properties, but
       had found the recursive nature of their definition to be challenging in characterizing such groups.
       In fact, any inductive argument to characterize would fail in this regard since subgroups of semia-
       belian groups do not inherit the semiabelian property. Thus as a means to assist in classification,
       Kida uses the machinery of P. Hall [Hal40] to prove that semiabelian groups are invariant under
       isoclinism class. Here Kida suggests a strong connection with monomial groups, also invariant un-
       der isoclinism class, and derived further results in [Kid25] to support this. This led to the following
       conjecture:

       Conjecture 1.2 (Kida). If a group G is semiabelian, then G is monomial.

                                                     2. Preliminaries

       2.1. Semiabelian Groups. For the purpose of identifying semiabelian groups within the family
       of finite solvable groups, we present a few useful properties.

           First, it is easily seen that abelian groups are semiabelian. For a nilpotent group to be semi-
       abelian, the group must have nilpotency class of at least 2 [Tho84]. If a solvable group G is
       semiabelian, then it admits an abelian normal subgroup which is not contained in the Frattini
       subgroup Φ(G). These three conditions will be useful for our search strategy outlined in section
       3.1.

           To provide background on the derivation of Conjecture 1.2, we also explore a convenient link be-
       tween semiabelian groups, modular groups, and quaternion-free groups, and recall their definitions
       here:
       Definition 2.1 (Kida, Definition 3.1). A finite p-group G is modular if the subgroup lattice of G
       is a modular lattice, namely, ⟨A, B⟩ ∩ C = ⟨A, B ∩ C⟩ for subgroups A, B, C of G such that A ≤ C.
       Definition 2.2 (Kida, Definition 3.7). A 2-group G is called quaternion-free if all homomorphic

           As it turns out, modular p-groups and quaternion-free 2-groups are semiabelian [Kid25]. It
       is from these subclasses that Kida forms the main connection between semiabelian groups and
       monomial groups, and we establish this in the next section.

       2.2. Monomial Groups. We first recall the definition of a monomial group here:

       Definition 2.3. A group is monomial if every irreducible representation of G can be obtained via
       induction from a one dimensional representation of some subgroup of G.

           As a means to identify if a finite solvable group is monomial, Dornhoff [Dor67] proposed the
       following criterion in connection with modular p-groups and quaternion-free 2-groups:

       Theorem 2.4 (Dornhoff, Theorem 2.6). If a solvable group G is such that
         1. the Sylow p-subgroups are modular for p ≥ 3, and
         2. the Sylow 2-subgroup is quaternion-free,

       then G is monomial.

           Kida suggests in Remark 5.2 of [Kid25] that an analogous statement to Theorem 2.4 may be said
       about solvable groups that are semiabelian, raising the following question: under what conditions
       should we expect the semiabelian and monomial properties to agree? We investigate this further
       in Section 2.3.

       2.3. Isoclinism Classes and Conjecture 1.2. In an attempt to formally bridge the two proper-
       ties, Kida turned to isoclinism classes, an equivalence relation first introduced by P. Hall to classify
       subclasses of solvable groups [Hal40].

           Given a pair of groups G1 and G2, we say that they are isoclinic if there exists an isomorphism
       φ : G1/Z(G1) → G2/Z(G2) that induces an isomorphism ψ : [G1, G1] → [G2, G2] such that the
       following diagram commutes:

       G1/Z(G1) × G1/Z(G1)  φ×φ               G2/Z(G2) × G2/Z(G2)

               [·,·]G1                                [·,·]G2
                                           ψ
                                              [G2, G2]
       [G1, G1]

           Furthermore, we say groups G1 and G2 lie in the same isoclinism class if G1 is isoclinic to G2.
       Isoclinism is therefore a weaker version of isomorphism. Informally, we think of two groups as
       isoclinic if their commutator structure is the same. For instance, all abelian groups are isoclinic to
       one another since their commutator is trivial, and D8 and Q8 are isoclinic but not isomorphic.

           Many subclasses of finite solvable groups, including monomial groups, were first shown to be
       invariant under isoclinism in [BvdW78], and later extended to semiabelian groups in [Kid25]. As a
       result, we have the following hierarchy:

       converse of Conjecture 1.2. In fact, it is well known that the converse does not hold true within the
       class of supersolvable groups. An explicit example of which was referenced by Kida as the group
       G = SmallGroup(256, 330) ([Kid25], Example 4.8).

                                                  3. Search Strategy

           In this section, we propose our two-fold search strategy: first verifying whether a group is
       semiabelian, then checking for non-monomiality.

       3.1. Semiabelian Group Check. The first goal of our search strategy is to manually check
       if a group is semiabelian. Given the recursive nature of Definition 1.1, we aim to minimize the
       computational complexity of the search. Hence we perform our check over the following minimal
       characterization of semiabelian groups given by Dentzer [Den95]:

       Lemma 3.1 (Dentzer, Theorem 2.3). A non-trivial finite group G is semiabelian if and only if
       there is an abelian normal subgroup A and a proper semiabelian subgroup M such that G = AM.

           Computationally, finding a candidate pair (A, M ) that satisfies Lemma 3.1 would require enu-
       merating all subgroups of G and computing their product with every normal abelian subgroup.
       However, we will show that it suffices to check only one class representative of each conjugacy class
       of maximal subgroups.

       Lemma 3.2. Let G be a finite semiabelian group that admits an abelian normal subgroup A ◁ G
       and a proper semiabelian subgroup U < G such that G = AU . Then for every maximal subgroup
       M with U ≤ M < G, G = AM and M is semiabelian.

       Proof. If U is maximal, we are done. So, assume that U < M and let L = M ∩ A. We will show
       that L is a normal abelian subgroup of M and that M = (M ∩ A)U . Hence M is the product of a
       normal abelian subgroup and a semiabelian subgroup, which implies that M is semiabelian.

           Let m = au for some m ∈ M , a ∈ A, and u ∈ U . Then a = mu−1 ∈ M , so that a ∈ M ∩ A.
       Given that G = AU and U ≤ M , the Dedekind modular law gives

                                              M ∩ G = M ∩ AU = (M ∩ A)U = LU.

                                                       5. Conclusion

           In our search over groups up to order 900, we did find a pair of groups of order 768 that are
       semiabelian and non-monomial, but they lie in the same isoclinism class as G. Beyond our search,
       the AI agent Nilradical [Nil26] had found another group of order 2592 that lies in this isoclinism
       class. Still, it remains unknown whether there exists examples unique to G. We hope that this
       motivates further study into the structure of such groups.

           As a closing remark, one insight possibly worth studying further would be the conditions of
       Dornhoff’s theorem given in Theorem 2.4. The main question of interest being the following:
       can Dornhoff’s criteria be refined into necessary and sufficient conditions for a group to be both
       semiabelian and monomial? For the particular counterexample that we found, we have G = P ⋊ C3
       where P is a normal 2-subgroup of order 128 isomorphic to (C2 × ((C2 × Q8) ⋊ C2)) ⋊ C2. Hence
       P is the unique Sylow 2-subgroup and contains a Q8 subgroup. Consequently, G fails to satisfy
       condition (2) of Dornhoff’s theorem.

           Therefore, we conclude with the following question:
       Question 5.1. Must all finite semiabelian, non-monomial groups admit a Sylow 2-subgroup that
       contains a group isomorphic to Q8?
