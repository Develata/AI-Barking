       AI Article Reviews Dominate Human Peer
       Reviews at Their Best: Evidence and Policy
                        Choices*

                          Gary King†                Steven Worthington‡

                                        October 25, 2026




                                                Abstract
       We show that AI-generated article reviews are now better, by every measure we could
       construct, than reviews written by well-compensated human experts and judged to
       be of unusually high quality by journal editors and authors. We then discuss how
       this result may inform journal policy choices, with concerning downsides as well
       as potential paths to increase publication speed and quality; reduce adversarial in-
       teractions; improve the experience of editors, reviewers, authors, and readers; and
       advance science.

1    Introduction

Human peer review is the default quality-control standard for scholarly publishing, and
yet problems with this system are deep, common, and well known (Aczel, Barwich, et
al., 2025), including self-serving reviewer conduct, weak incentives and lack of exper-
tise for some reviewers, and insufficient scrutiny; low inter-reviewer agreement (Born-
mann, Mutz, and Daniel, 2010; Mutz, Bornmann, and Daniel, 2012); bias due to author
characteristics, topics, and methods, with opacity and little accountability (Tennant and
Ross-Hellauer, 2020); failure to detect major errors and little overall evidence of im-
proved quality (Gasparyan and Kitas, 2012); frustrating delays (Farber, 2025); large costs
in reviewer time (Aczel, Szaszi, and Holcombe, 2021); reviewer recruitment difficulties
(Wright, 2024); unhelpful feedback (Chong and Lin, 2024); and resistance to heterodox
findings (Armstrong, 1997).
    These problems with the human peer review process have motivated scholars to eval-
uate large language models (now often called AI) as a replacement. But AI leads to
other worries, including hallucinated claims, fabricated sources, and confidently stated
errors (Ye et al., 2024); failure to recognize or reward novelty (Shin et al., 2025); biases
learned from human training data unfairly favoring authors based on institution, seniority,
or fame (Macharla Vasu et al., 2026; Hosseini and Horbach, 2023); excessive leniency
(Russo Latona et al., 2024); vulnerability to manipulation (Lin, 2025; Ye et al., 2024);
breaches of confidentiality (Naddaf, 2025); and unclear accountability for errors (Hos-
seini and Horbach, 2023; Porsdam Mann et al., 2025). The farthest scholars have gone
is to conclude that AI should supplement rather than replace human peer reviews (Liang
et al., 2024; Farber, 2025; Doskaliuk et al., 2025), including the largest deployment to
date, at the AAAI-26 conference, which added one clearly labeled AI review to the hu-
man reviews of each of the 22,977 papers sent for full review (Biswas et al., 2026). Two
recent comparisons of AI and human reviews of the same journal manuscripts point in
opposite directions. For 119 articles in BMJ Open, human reviewers’ reports raised more
detailed and varied substantive points than ChatGPT’s (Rajakumar et al., 2026). For 40
manuscripts at a cardiology journal, two editors who did not know which reviews were


                                             1
AI-written rated reviews by Claude, ChatGPT, and Gemini higher than the human reviews
on five of seven quality criteria (Zancanaro et al., 2026).
     In this paper, we provide head-to-head experiments, using an unusually high quality
set of human reviews, and show that AI now dominates human peer reviews at their best
— according to blind judgments by other AI models in every area and by (human) former
editors of major scholarly journals. Given prior research on earlier AI models, we had
expected AI to win some in some areas, some of the time, but we found that the latest
AI wins in them all, by every criterion we measured, by wide margins, with no fabricated
sources, and with about a third the rate of incorrect claims of the human reviews. We did
not measure the degree to which subsequent revisions improved the paper or the collective
consequences of switching to AI reviewing. We offer our research design in Section 2,
statistical results in Section 3, and the potential implications for journal policy in Section
4.


2     Research Design

We now discuss the papers reviewed, the AI and human paper reviewers, and how we
used AI and human judges to separately choose which review of each paper is better (see
Supplementary Appendix A for details).


Paper Submissions and Human Reviews For this study, we used papers submitted to
rapidPeer, a service developed by Harvard University’s Institute for Quantitative Social
Science (and then expanded to several other institutions) that gives university faculty high
quality double-blind reviews of their paper drafts prior to formal journal submission. The
service asks human reviewers to read the entire paper and provide constructive sugges-
tions throughout (as distinct from behaviors we sometimes see from journal reviewers,
such as searching for a fatal flaw on p.3 so they can stop reading, or making it more about
their own work than appropriate). Reviewers are told not to focus on whether the paper is
suitable for a particular journal or meets a specific standard (although publication sugges-
tions are welcomed) and instead asked to be comprehensive, to write at least 1.5-2 pages,



                                              2
and to be helpful to the author when revising. The instructions suggest a wide range of
ways for reviewers to help (see Supplementary Appendix B for the exact language).
   Reviewers are carefully chosen by experts in the field from institutions other than their
own and incented to provide highly constructive reviews: They are paid (well) for their
time and told they are likely to have another opportunity to be paid (or to submit a paper to
the process themselves) if they do a good job. All manuscripts are deidentified prior to the
review process. Whether judged by us, PhDs on the rapidPeer staff, or the paper authors
(who fill out evaluations upon receipt), these reviews are of exceptionally high quality:
88% of the 74 authors who answered a survey a week after delivery rated the review they
received as high or very high quality, and the median author rated their willingness to rec-
ommend the rapidPeer service to other faculty at 10 out of 10 (Supplementary Appendix,
Table S1). We also informally consulted former editors from major journals, all of whom
told us that the reviews are among the best they have seen in their times as editors. We
know of no collection of human reviews written under better conditions, and none that
authors or editors rated more highly.
   The corpus includes every eligible submission to RapidPeer, including 124 scholarly
paper submissions and 134 human reviews (10 submissions received two independent
reviews) collected between 2019 and 2026 and drawn from a wide range of research, in-
cluding statistical analyses of empirical data, formal mathematical modeling, and purely
qualitative studies. Near as we can tell, all reviews were written by humans: many pre-
dated ChatGPT; neither the quality nor style changed over time; and reviewers were com-
pensated and responsible for their contribution.


AI Reviews For each submission, we generated a single AI review with Claude Opus
4.8 (Anthropic, 2026) at its maximum reasoning setting, under two conditions. In the
matched condition the model received the anonymized manuscript (including figures and
tables) together with the verbatim rapidPeer reviewer guidelines (Supplementary Ap-
pendix B) as its instructions and no external tools. Like the human reviewers, it worked
from the manuscript and guidelines, with two differences: human reviewers could also
consult supplementary files, and the AI’s training data may include work published after


                                             3
the submission, the effects of which we test for in Section 3. In the tuned condition, the
AI model received the same manuscript but with a purpose-built agent prompt tuned to the
AI along with web retrieval abilities (Supplementary Appendix C). The tuned prompt was
written before any judging, was never tuned against judges’ scores, and does not contain
the judging rubric. Every tuned review invoked web retrieval (mean 12.4 searches per
review). Each review was produced in an isolated call — one review per submission per
condition.


AI and Human Judges         A model from a different family, GPT-5.5 (OpenAI, 2026),
served as the AI judge, so that generator and judge share no lineage. AI judges may
favor AI reviews for two reasons: AI models can prefer text written by other AI models
(Laurito et al., 2025), and aggregate quality scores can overstate the quality of AI reviews
that are fluent but generic or poorly grounded in the manuscript (Fichtl et al., 2026). We
test for the first by rewriting the human reviews in the AI’s style and for the second by
checking each review’s claims against the manuscript, and we also had human editors
judge a sample of pairs (Section 3). Before comparing the two reviews, we had the AI
decide which review topics were relevant to each paper (e.g., so a paper with no figures
was not judged on “figures and tables”). We locked in that list of applicable topics once
per paper, based only on the paper. Then we had the AI judge evaluate both reviews
on only that set of topics. The AI judge evaluated the reviews under three instruments
— an overall ranking of the two reviews, made from the reviews alone; a head-to-head
ranking of the two reviews in each of seven review areas; and an independent individual
rating score from 0 to 10 for each of the areas, the last two made with the manuscript’s
text in hand (Supplementary Appendix D). The overall ranking judged each review as a
whole, so it reflects how a review connects a paper’s framing, results, and figures together.
Every comparison was run in both presentation orders, and a win was credited only when
the same review was preferred under both orders; the few order-inconsistent pairs, and
pairs in which neither review addressed an area, were treated as undecided. (We gave
reviews silent on a relevant area a rating of 0; in head-to-head rankings, judges recorded
no winner for an area only when neither review usefully engaged it.) Per-area win rates


                                             4
are AI wins divided by decided pairs, and rating values are means, each computed only
over the submissions for which the area is relevant, so denominators vary across areas.
The seven review judgment areas — theory and idea, methods, weaknesses, framing,
communication, figures and tables, and sources — were taken from the explicit rapidPeer
reviewer guidelines rather than imposed externally (Supplementary Appendix E).
    Finally, we enlisted three former editors of leading scholarly journals (in similar areas
as the papers) to independently judge 10 randomly selected and blinded review pairs, one
AI and one human but ordered randomly, and under the same overall ranking instructions
given to the AI judge. The editors were unaware they were comparing AI to human
reviews (until we debriefed them afterward). We did not attempt to ask our human editors
to evaluate each review area separately (although debriefings with each editor revealed no
differences in any area from the overall results). We collected this information via a web
interface we designed for ease-of-use, with reviews for each pair presented side by side
with independent scroll bars. On the AI judge’s ratings, the ten papers resembled the rest
of the corpus: human reviews averaged 6.03 on the 0–10 rating scale in the editor sample
and 6.02 in the other 114 papers, and the AI-human gaps were 2.67 and 2.64 points.


3    Results

Overall We now summarize in Figure 1 an analysis of the 134 pairs of AI and human
reviews, each pair of the same manuscript. The figure covers pairwise choices (Panel A),
ratings of each item independently (Panel B), overall judgments (top), and AI ratings in
seven review areas (bottom).
    The results are unambiguous: No matter how we look at the data, AI wins. It wins
in 100% of the pairs overall when judged by another AI model (132 of the 132 decided
pairs) and 87% overall when judged by human journal editors (Panel A). It wins in 92% of
AI-Human review pairs for three of the review areas (theory and idea, framing, and com-
munication), 99% for one (figures and tables), and 100% in three (sources, weaknesses,
and methods).

Robustness Checks We also ran ten categories of robustness checks (Supplementary
Appendix F): (1) To rule out the possibility that the AI wins merely by writing at greater
length we restricted the overall comparison to pairs whose AI-to-human word count ratio
fell in [0.7,1.4]. On this length-matched subset of 44 pairs, AI was preferred in all 44
(100%), matching the full-corpus result. Nor does the AI win only by covering more
areas: comparing ratings from pairs where both reviews addressed an area, the AI review
had the higher mean rating in all seven areas. (2) AI wins even after biasing the sample
by post hoc selection of the top human reviews, measured in various ways. (3) The AI



                                                         6
judge made its overall ranking from the reviews alone, so we repeated it giving AI the
full manuscript and instructions to check each review’s claims against it. The AI review
was still preferred in 133 of 134 matched pairs and 132 of 134 tuned pairs; in the other
three, the judge’s choice reversed with presentation order, so the human review was never
preferred in both orders. (4) AI vs human writing style (according to formatting or writing
register) had no effect, which we showed by rewriting human reviews in AI style and
finding no improvement in overall judgments. (5) The AI reviewer’s training data may
include work published after a submission, which the human reviewer could not have
seen, but the evidence does not support this as a source of AI’s advantage: the advantage
does not differ between 2025–26 submissions and those from 2022 or earlier, and AI
reviews for which we did not grant web access cited work published after the submission
in only 2 of 124 reviews. (6) Because reviews can be preferred even if incorrect, we
checked accuracy in three ways and found that AI reviews cited no fabricated sources and
that 5.5% of their claims about the manuscript were judged incorrect, about a third of the
human reviews’ 15.9%. (7) To confirm that the AI judge can recognize a statement that
contradicts the manuscript, we made two new versions of every review. One added an
accurate sentence about an explicit statement in the manuscript, and the other a minimally
altered sentence contradicting it. Given the manuscript but not told which version, or
whose review, it was reading, the judge flagged all 258 false sentences and none of the
258 accurate ones. (8) AI reviews vary stochastically and may depend on the model that
writes or judges them, so we replicated the study with a different reviewer prompt and
scoring rubric, two AI reviews from each of two model families (Claude Opus and GPT),
and judges from both families. Across 416 comparisons with 104 human reviews, the AI
review was preferred in 97.1% of cases under the Opus judge and 94.2% under the GPT
judge. (9) The results above include the “tuned” AI reviews; the “matched” AI reviews
perform almost the same, with the exception of winning by only 62% in the review area
of “sources”; however, giving AI access to a browser (like the human reviewers had)
increased its win rate to 100%, as indicated in Figure 1. And finally, (10), AI reviews
received higher average ratings than human reviews in every group defined by manuscript

4    Journal Policy Choices

High quality scholarly reviews are intended to help (1) authors improve their papers and
(2) journal editors make publication decisions. Our results speak to (1): For constructive
advice to improve scholarly work, our results suggest that AI reviews dominate human
reviews at their best, an advantage that seems only likely to increase with advances in
AI. In addition, since we tried to select reviews from the high end of the distribution of
review quality that journals see in practice, our results may substantially understate the
advantages of AI over human reviewing in specific practical situations. Nevertheless, our
results do not speak to the second purpose, which is on what basis editors should make
publication decisions, but we can use what we learned about (1) to inform journal policy
decisions regarding both (1) and (2). We thus summarize here the dangers and then the
opportunities in journal policy changes that some may think our results suggest.
    We begin with the dangers of AI, which are insidious: Authors brainstorming ideas
for a paper with AI will have access to more diverse and creative topics than they would
without AI, and so are obviously drawn to use it, but their competitors brainstorming in
the same area, even using different AI models, will wind up with roughly the same ideas,
and all will tend toward the conventional, orthodox, and majoritarian (Hao et al., 2026;
Wenger and Kenett, 2026). In other words, AI benefits us individually but risks making
the collective efforts of science more homogenous and ordinary. Algorithms exist to ame-
liorate this problem, but they would need to be used (e.g., Luo et al., 2026). AI reviews
likely also have some of the same issues, potentially pushing individual papers and sci-
ence as a whole toward homogenous arguments and conclusions. The heterogeneity of
human reviewers, who disagree with each other so often, can thus be both advantageous
and damaging. Similarly, the potential of AI biasing journal acceptance decisions toward
certain hypotheses, questions, or styles is also real, but at least the process would be easier
to study and adjust than with human reviewers.


                                              8
   If the dangers of AI in reviewing can be constrained, then the opportunities may be-
come substantial; we describe these in the context of five possible journal policy changes.
First, some journals may begin to recommend or require that authors conduct repeated
AI reviews themselves before submission, iterating with AI to improve their papers (via
what are essentially self-supervised revise-and-resubmits), and continuing until authors
are convinced they have resolved all resolvable issues and improved the paper as much as
possible within time or resource constraints.
   Second, journals may consider adopting and publishing a specific AI prompt for au-
thors to use for evaluation of their papers (for a starting point, editors could combine the
rapidPeer prompt in Supplementary Appendix B with the current “instructions to con-
tributors” each journal already makes public). This policy could then use AI to better
align submissions with each journal’s specific editorial standards, reduce uncertainty in
the review process, improve quality, and potentially even reduce rejections, especially
desk rejects.
   Third, journals could require an AI review, generated by the journal’s official AI
prompt, be submitted with the paper, as well as a letter from the author explaining any
remaining open questions or disagreements with AI. The author’s letter, which is akin to
the “response to reviewers” authors are already often asked to write, is crucial so that AI
model creators do not get to determine the content of scholarly papers and because, as
the paper improves during the iterations, AI sometimes becomes absurdly pedantic even
though still technically correct (such as “yes 2+2 = 4, but you never defined the + sign”).
   Fourth, some journal editors may wish to go farther and end human reviews entirely
and use AI reviews instead, as our evidence shows that AI reviews give more constructive
advice, and AI reviews alone have been shown to improve quality (Thakkar et al., 2026).
However, if the journal’s only review is an AI review that authors can run themselves in
advance, authors will revise until that review is favorable, and the review will then tell
the editor little the author has not already addressed. Indeed, this decision may already
be a fait accompli: despite the efforts of some editors to stop AI use in (supposedly
human) reviews, AI is increasingly used by human reviewers (Naddaf, 2026). At the



                                             9
2026 International Conference on Machine Learning, which randomly assigned reviewers
to policies on AI use, 22.5% of the surveyed reviewers assigned to a policy forbidding AI
reported using it anyway (Kim et al., 2026).
   Finally, a more realistic policy change that some editors may adopt is to skip the
routine use of human reviews. Then the editor could review a dossier submitted by the
author (consisting of the paper, the AI review generated from the journal’s official AI
prompt, and the author’s response to the AI), a new AI review generated by prompts
the editor chooses and does not share publicly (such as including manipulation checks;
Murray 2025), and, if necessary for particular cases, a review by a chosen editorial board
member or even, in difficult cases, an outside traditional human reviewer. The editor
would then make a final publication decision.
   Some version of these ideas has the potential to greatly speed the process, enabling
publication decisions within days rather than months. If the dangers can be avoided, it
may remove the bias and painfully adversarial parts of the process for the author (such
as having to make unnecessary or sycophantic changes in papers and revise-and-resubmit
responses to avoid offending a human reviewer). Papers submitted using iterative AI
review will likely be more free of errors and weaknesses in logic, math, references, argu-
ment, scholarly literature, data analyses, graphic presentations, and even journal stylistic
choices (AI makes mistakes too, although in our study, only about a third as often as
human reviewers). Problems of special concern to each journal would be more easily
avoided by including them in the journal’s official AI prompt (as AI is considerably better
at following long lists of detailed “instructions to authors” than humans).
   If we can get this right, the job of the editor may then be improved and of more interest
to the high quality people the scientific community attracts for this job. Some use of AI
may also help with the framing, impact, overall importance of the paper, and suitability
for the journal, but these crucial decisions are the most important ones and so journals
will presumably wish these to be left to the journal editor. Even if all AI dangers are
avoided, authors are conscientious in iterating with AI to improve their paper, and paper
submissions are all correct or close to it, whether a correct paper meets the standards of



                                            10
any one scholarly journal can only be decided by a human journal editor with the proper
authority and responsibility.
   Any policy ideas such as these carry additional risks that journals will need to manage.
Authors who revise for a published prompt may wind up optimizing for the explicit and
implicit reward model, and so we may be left with biases unrelated to the quality of the
science. Some partial protections are already included — the author’s letter answering
the AI review, a second AI review from a prompt the editor does not disclose, and ma-
nipulation checks — but how they work in practice is an empirical question. Thus, we
suggest that an important component of adopting AI in any form should be the ongoing
study of authors’ disagreements with AI (that the journal editor agrees with) so they can
continuously improve by updating their disclosed and undisclosed prompts and choosing
among AI models.
   We encourage others to follow up our research in other substantive areas, and with
each new generation of AI models, to see how it might be used to continue to improve the
scientific process, increase the speed at which science advances, and avoid the inevitable
biases and problems.
   To conclude, we ask: is our title overstated? One AI says yes, others say no. We’re
sticking with it!
