                                                            Significant efforts are currently being directed toward the implementation of large-scale quantum
                                                         information processors, but realizing useful quantum advantage also requires the development of
                                                         efficient quantum algorithms. While for many practical problems the dominant resource costs
                                                         are associated with a small set of computational primitives, optimizing them in the quantum
                                                         setting presents unique challenges, including a reversibility constraint absent from ordinary classical
                                                         computation. Here we introduce an evolutionary framework in which large language models (LLMs)




arXiv:2610.22367v1 [quant-ph] 15 Oct 2026
                                                         propose circuit designs and an independent evaluator verifies and scores them. We apply this
                                                         framework to tasks spanning numerical computation, chemistry, optimization, quantum simulation,
                                                         and number theory. In seven of the eight tasks, the discovered circuits significantly reduce both Toffoli
                                                         and qubit counts relative to the state of the art. In the eighth, the search discovers a new reversible
                                                         architecture for prime-field modular inversion. By handling reversibility differently, the architecture
                                                         makes divsteps—a classical algorithm previously impractical for reversible integer arithmetic—viable
                                                         and substantially improves the Toffoli–qubit trade-off for modular inversion. These results open the
                                                         door for evaluator-guided LLM co-design of new algorithmic structures for quantum computation.

failures on atypical, costly inputs reduces resource requirements.

   The same choices that bound the search also make re-

each candidate must preserve and which resource trade-

our results show that evaluator-guided LLM search can go
beyond refining a fixed circuit to uncover new reversible
algorithms and representations. Related approaches have
produced new algorithms in combinatorics and linear alge-
bra [19, 20]. These observations suggest a practical crite-
rion for extending the approach: wherever a problem ad-
mits a sufficiently expressive task interface and its correct-
ness and resource costs can be evaluated cheaply enough
to sustain many rounds of search, the same evaluator-
guided search may provide a route to new algorithms.
   These results, together with related progress in rein-
forcement learning and LLM-guided discovery of quan-
tum error-correcting codes and encoding circuits [21–23],
create an opportunity for end-to-end co-design of fault-
tolerant computation. Choices that are usually made
separately—the algorithm and its logical circuit, the
error-correcting code and its method for universal logic,
the supply of magic states, and the physical execution

programmatic evaluator that propagates logical errors,

                                                          METHODS

   A classical function cannot generally be applied directly within a quantum algorithm, because quantum evolution
must be reversible. Instead, a function f is embedded in a reversible map |x⟩ |y⟩ 7−→ |x⟩ |y ⊕ f (x)⟩. A natural model for
these subroutines is the kickmix circuit model of Babbush et al. [8], which augments reversible classical computation
with measurement-based uncomputation (MBU) and diagonal sign gates. Despite these additional operations, kickmix
circuits remain efficiently classically simulable. This allows candidate circuits to be tested for their action on coherent
quantum superpositions, providing the reliable correctness signal needed for evolutionary search.
   Circuits that implement the same specification can have very different time and space requirements. We use Toffoli
count and peak qubit count as proxies for these costs. Toffoli gates require expensive non-Clifford resources, so their
count tracks a large part of the logical work and often the runtime [24, 25]. The peak qubit count measures the data
and ancillas that must coexist during execution and therefore approximates the required logical processor footprint [26].
The two resources also typically trade against each other: additional ancillas can avoid recomputation, while recycling
ancillas can reduce space at the cost of additional gates. We multiply these quantities to obtain the T Q-cost, which
provides a single objective for comparing such designs.
   Together, the correctness tests and T Q-cost define a constrained optimization problem over circuit-generating
programs. Motivated by recent advances in coding and reasoning agents and LLM-guided algorithmic discovery, we
use an LLM to propose coordinated, task-specific redesigns within an evolutionary search [20, 27, 28].


                                                   Circuit model and validity

  A compilation task specifies a reversible action on computational-basis states: a permutation π and, when needed, a
Boolean phase g. A circuit may use a ancilla qubits, but must return them to zero:

                                              V |x⟩ |0a ⟩ = (−1)g(x) |π(x)⟩ |0a ⟩ .                                   (10)

Every ancilla must return to |0⟩ because quantum operations must be unitary, and thus reversible. For a numerical
approximation, the required output relation may instead specify an error bound; interface preservation, phase
correctness, and ancilla uncomputation remain exact. We separately allow some circuits to fail on a bounded fraction
of inputs drawn from a stated distribution; this fraction is the failure budget. Following Babbush et al. [8], the kickmix
model permits three classes of operation:
   1. classical reversible gates: X, CNOT, Toffoli, and swap;
   2. diagonal sign gates: Z, CZ, CCZ, and global sign flips; and
   3. destructive X-basis measurements.
Before any measurement, the evolution of a computational-basis input is simple. Reversible gates update its stored bit
string, while diagonal gates update only its sign, so the state remains of the form (−1)ϕ |x⟩. A classical simulator
therefore need only track the current bits x and the sign ϕ, rather than a full state vector. The operations apply phase
kickback while mixing basis states, giving kickmix its name.
   To illustrate why uncomputation is costly, consider a temporary AND. A Toffoli computes t = uv into an ancilla.
After t has been used, applying the same Toffoli again returns that qubit to zero:

                                         Validation and statistical guarantees

    The circuits are ultimately applied to coherent superpositions, whereas the evaluator follows one computational-basis
trajectory at a time. Each test must therefore capture enough information to determine the trajectory’s coherent
action. For a basis input x and measurement record m, the evaluator checks that the output matches the target,
required inputs are unchanged, every ancilla has returned to |0⟩, and the phase agrees with the target exactly. These
checks compare the complete final state rather than only its classical output. An ancilla that retains input-dependent
information can become entangled with the logical registers, while an incorrect sign can alter subsequent interference
without changing any output bit. Resetting or tracing out a dirty ancilla is therefore not a substitute for uncomputing
it.
    If every pair (x, m) passes these tests, the measurement record factors from the logical output, and linearity extends
agreement on every trajectory to any coherent input. For large input domains, however, the evaluator samples these
trajectories rather than checking them   P all.

                                                 Cost model and scoring

  We define the T Q-cost of a circuit by
                                         T Q = Toffoli count × peak qubit count,                                         (22)
where T counts Toffoli and CCZ gates equally and Q is the largest number of logical qubits allocated at once.
   We weight space and time equally in this cost function for simplicity. In practice, their relative importance depends
on the hardware and application. When qubits are scarce, for example, a cost of the form (qubit count)κ × Toffoli count
with κ > 1 may be more appropriate; resource estimates for factoring have used κ between 1 and 3 [6, 30]. Either T or
Q can also be replaced with other measures of time or space. In particular, when there is limited magic-state-factory
throughput (as is typically the case), runtime is bottlenecked by the Toffoli count, motivating its widespread use as a
time proxy [24, 25]. However, with enough factories to supply magic states in parallel, Toffoli depth rather than Toffoli
count limits runtime. Similarly, for architectures with cheap cold storage for inactive qubits [31], Q can be replaced by
active volume [32], which counts only the qubits participating in logical operations.
   The search is not tied to the specific form of the T Q-cost: it can run with any computable objective. A later case study
illustrates the available trade-offs: the modular-inversion search (SI II) produces a family of circuits along a Toffoli–qubit
frontier (SI II.1), and an objective that weights qubit count more heavily selects a lower-qubit member of that family.


                                                   Evolutionary search

  We encode each candidate as Python code that constructs a circuit. Functions, loops, and named registers preserve
the organization of the computation, allowing the LLM to reason about arithmetic subroutines and their composition
without reconstructing that structure from a flattened gate sequence. For example, changing a shared arithmetic
routine changes every place where it is used, while reorganizing calls to that routine can change when intermediate
values are computed, retained, and uncomputed. The representation thus makes coordinated changes expressible as
revisions to the underlying algorithm.
  We search over these circuit-generating programs with OpenEvolve [33], using Claude Mythos 5 [28] to propose
architectures and program rewrites. Each of the eight searches uses a fixed, hand-written task specification, and all
share the same generic circuit-building interface. During ideation, the LLM receives only the specification, circuit
model, and correctness criteria, and proposes several distinct circuit architectures. The LLM has no access to the
evaluator beyond the feedback returned after each submission. Starting from a blank program, it chooses the algorithm,
representation, and circuit structure and produces the discovered circuits without substantive human intervention.
  Each architecture proposed during ideation founds a separate family of candidate programs. Later iterations improve
descendants of the first working implementation. Keeping the families separate at first gives each approach time
to mature, even if its earliest circuits are less efficient than those based on another approach. Periodically, leading
programs are shared across families, allowing successful ideas from different approaches to combine.
  The search retains candidate programs and their evaluation results, tracking which family each belongs to and which
earlier program it was derived from. It also saves feedback on failures and short notes in which the LLM records
useful insights. These programs, results, and notes carry information between iterations; the sessions do not share a
continuously growing conversation.
  Each iteration starts a new LLM agent session with the task specification, a parent program to improve, and its
evaluation feedback. The agent also receives alternative programs from the archive and their resource costs, brief
summaries of earlier attempts, and recorded insights where available. This context is limited; the agent never sees the
complete search history. Within the session, it may rewrite the program, run drafts on test inputs, query a classical
oracle for the required function on chosen inputs, and use the results to revise its design. It may also delegate focused
subtasks to subagents. The iteration ends with submission of a revised program. The evaluator rejects a circuit that
does not meet the task specification; a valid one is scored by its T Q-cost and considered for retention in its family.
Thus the LLM is free to propose broad changes without having to judge their correctness: the evaluator supplies
an independent test of every completed design. Extended Data Figure 1 shows the ideation and rewrite calls and a
concrete feedback-and-repair cycle for a small finite-field inversion task.
  To decide which valid candidates remain available within each family, the search retains circuits with different
trade-offs between Toffoli count and qubit count. We implement this selection with MAP-Elites [34], which divides the
(T, Q) resource plane into cells and keeps the circuit with the lowest T Q-cost in each cell. A space-efficient circuit can
therefore remain available for later development even when its higher Toffoli count gives it a worse T Q-cost.
  Because the families preserve alternatives, progress need not be monotonic in either resource. The Gaussian lineage
in Extended Data Figure 2, for example, contains descendants that temporarily worsen Toffoli count or qubit count
before reaching a region of lower T Q-cost. We report the lowest T Q-cost reached across all families at the end of the
search.

                                         I.   SYNDROME DECODING FOR DQI

   Consider an optimization problem over a finite field Fq . An assignment x ∈ Fnq produces m linear values Bx. Given
a set Fi ⊂ Fq of acceptable values for each constraint, let f (x) = |{i : (Bx)i ∈ Fi }| count the constraints satisfied by x;
the goal is to maximize f (x). This family, known as max-LINSAT, includes linear constraint-satisfaction problems
such as Max-Cut and max-XORSAT, as well as nearest-codeword problems and polynomial fitting over finite fields.
Decoded quantum interferometry (DQI) is a quantum algorithm that aims to solve these optimization problems by
arranging for constructive interference on assignments satisfying many constraints and destructive interference on
assignments satisfying fewer [1].
   DQI prepares a superposition over sparse vectors e ∈ Fm   q , each supported on at most ℓ constraints. Here H = B

Adaptive coefficient ranges. A naïve implementation would apply every BM update across all 36 possible coefficient
positions in every round. Even in the worst case, this is unnecessary: after r syndromes have been processed, only
coefficients through degree min(r, 35) can be active, so the update range need only grow by one position per round.
The dashed line in Figure S1(b) shows this worst-case bound. On the DQI input distribution, however, the candidate
polynomials typically grow more slowly still. A degree-L locator σ has L unknown coefficients after fixing σ0 = 1,
while its evaluator ω, whose degree is less than L, has at most L unknown coefficients. Since each syndrome supplies
one coefficient constraint, r syndromes typically determine a locator of degree L ≈ r/2, consistent with the samples in
Figure S1(b).

circuit, we classically simulate BM on the DQI input distribution to choose a cutoff k for each round. The circuit then
updates only the prefix σ0 , . . . , σk , treating higher coefficients as zero. The prefixes expand as the rounds progress,
which produces the rising per-round cost in Figure S1(a).
Shared BM updates. Compiled directly, the two correction cases of Equation (33) would produce two separately
controlled arithmetic paths. Only one path acts on any basis state, but both would appear in the fixed circuit and
contribute to its Toffoli count. The main text shows how the circuit avoids this (Equation (5)). Under a flag that
marks the replace case, it exchanges σ with the shifted template τe. One shared update then subtracts a multiple of
the template register from the locator register. The multiplier is α in the retain case and α−1 in the replace case. The
replace result differs from the standard update only by the nonzero factor −α−1 and therefore has the same roots.
  The circuit also stores no discrepancy δ. The rescaled update maintains the invariant

Reconstructing BM history. Reversing a round requires the discrepancy that the round used, which the round
computes into a temporary register. Keeping a twelve-bit history word for every round would raise the qubit count to
2,677. A replace round leaves σ0 = ∆−1 by Equation (34), so the locator determines the round’s discrepancy. The
circuit therefore erases the temporary discrepancy register by MBU, and the reverse pass recomputes the erased value
from the locator. A round with ∆ = 0 changes nothing and has nothing to recover.
   A retain round with ∆ ̸= 0 is the exception. No exchange occurs and σ0 is unchanged, so the constant coefficient that
reveals the discrepancy on a replace round carries no trace of it here. The current ∆ remains only in the temporary
discrepancy register, and the circuit stores it on the history stack before erasing that register. A stack that never
overflows needs 35 entries, or 665 qubits.
   Retain rounds are rare on the DQI input distribution. Starting from d = 0, a run of nonzero discrepancies repeatedly
selects replace and leaves d = 0. A zero discrepancy raises d; a later replacement can then make it negative, producing
retain rounds while d returns to zero. Such accidental zero discrepancies are uncommon over F212 before the locator
reaches its final degree. Once it has, subsequent discrepancies stay zero and do not start new runs of retain rounds.

                                    II.   PRIME-FIELD MODULAR INVERSION

  We benchmark modular inversion within a complete arithmetic circuit: point addition on an elliptic curve over a
256-bit prime field. Each point on this curve is a pair of integers (x, y) satisfying the curve equation
                                                   y 2 ≡ x3 + 7    (mod p),                                                 (51)
where p = 2256 − 232 − 977 is a 256-bit prime number. The curve defines point addition, an operation that combines two
points P and Q into a third point R = P + Q on the same curve (note that this addition is not the usual coordinate-wise
addition). Geometrically, one draws the line through P and Q, finds its third intersection with the curve, and reflects
that point across the horizontal axis. Algebraically, for the inputs considered here, with Qx ̸= Px , one first computes
the slope
                                           λ ≡ (Qy − Py )(Qx − Px )−1      (mod p),                                         (52)
where (Qx − Px )−1 denotes the inverse modulo p. The coordinates of the sum are then
                                     Rx = λ2 − Px − Qx ,          Ry = λ(Px − Rx ) − Py .                                   (53)
  We study the reversible point adder [52]: given an input point P and another, classically supplied point Q, it
performs
                                                      |P ⟩ 7−→ |P + Q⟩ .                                                    (54)
The modular inverse required by Equation (52) is the dominant cost of point addition: once the slope λ is known, the
remaining coordinate updates require only a fixed sequence of relatively cheap modular additions and multiplications.
In contrast, modular inversion follows a long input-dependent sequence of arithmetic transitions, which the circuit
must later recover to reverse the computation and clear its ancillas.
   Prior point-addition constructions [8, 38] use the binary greatest common divisor (gcd) algorithm for modular
inversion. The connection between gcd and inversion comes from expressing the gcd in terms of the original inputs.
Because p is prime and w = Qx − Px ̸= 0, there are integers α and β such that gcd(p, w) = 1 = αp + βw. Reducing
this identity modulo p gives βw ≡ 1, so β ≡ w−1 (mod p). The extended binary-gcd algorithm finds these coefficients
by tracking expressions for the two current values u and v as integer combinations of p and w. Binary gcd rests on two
elementary identities. If u is even and v is odd, then gcd(u, v) = gcd(u/2, v); if u and v are both odd, then, for u > v,
gcd(u, v) = gcd((u − v)/2, v). Applying these identities symmetrically gives the reduction

  Making this reduction reversible is difficult because its control flow is input-dependent: which line of Equation (55)
applies changes from one step to the next, and the reduced pair does not in general reveal which line produced it. A
quantum circuit therefore cannot simply run the reduction backward to restore its input and clear its workspace; it
must preserve enough information to identify each step. The prior constructions do so using the dialog introduced by
Khattar et al. [13]. The direct approach would update the coefficients that become α and β alongside the two values
throughout the reduction. The dialog avoids holding these growing coefficients in memory. Its first pass updates only
the pair being reduced and records a compact symbol identifying each subtraction, swap, and halving choice. A second
pass replays the same transformations to construct the required coefficient. Initializing this replay from the numerator
a = Qy − Py produces aβ = aw−1 mod p directly, without ever storing w−1 in a separate register. The simple binary
updates and the replay trick reduce the gate count, but the complete dialog record requires hundreds of additional

                                                     II.1.   Divsteps

   Rather than reorganize the binary-gcd constructions above, the search finds a new reversible prime-field inversion
architecture built on divsteps [15, 53]. In the divstep recurrence of Equation (6), a small signed counter z, called the
half delta, replaces the repeated magnitude comparisons in Equation (55): the sign of z and the parity of one value
alone determine the next update. We call the choice of update at a step its decision. Appendix A proves that every
decision can be computed exactly from the registers as they stand after the step. When a decision is cheap to decode,
the circuit decodes it from the updated state. Otherwise, the circuit stores the decision as a single bit on a fixed-size
history stack. We call the sequence of divsteps for one input a walk. The point adder uses this inversion twice, once to
form the slope λ and once to erase it after the coordinate update. Each use runs a forward walk and a reverse walk,
for four walks in total.
   Unlike the quotient-based extended Euclidean algorithm (EEA) of SI II.2, which divides one remainder by the other
at every iteration, a divstep never computes a multi-bit quotient or adds a multiple of one full register to another. A
divstep walk needs more steps than the quotient-based EEA needs divisions, but each step is much cheaper. Reversible
divsteps were previously developed over binary fields and polynomial rings [13, 54]. There, each step shrinks the
polynomial pair by one coefficient, and the freed space can hold that step’s record. Integer divsteps free no such fixed
space, so a stored history costs additional qubits. To our knowledge, this is the first reversible divstep inversion circuit
over a prime field.
   We recall the notation of the main text. The walk state is (z, f, g, fv , gv ), and each step applies one of the three
updates in Equation (6). Two bits label the update: s records whether the step swaps the roles of f and g, and b
records whether the incoming g is odd. To run a step backward, the circuit must know s and b, and the updated
state does not hold them explicitly. Cheap tests on a few bits of the updated state recover s at every step and b at
about two thirds of the steps. We call the remaining decisions ambiguous. Each ambiguous decision has a depth j, the
number of trailing zero bits of the updated coefficient fv′ , and we write fv′ = 2j q with q odd. A swap followed by a run
of ambiguous decisions is an excursion, and the depth counts the steps of the excursion so far, with the swap as step 1.
The cost of a depth-j decoder grows with j. The cutoff jstore is the depth from which the circuit stops decoding: it
decodes an ambiguous decision at depth j < jstore and stores one at depth j ≥ jstore .
   The cutoff jstore trades qubits for Toffolis: a higher cutoff decodes more and stores less. At jstore = 0, the circuit
uses only the cheap tests and stores every remaining decision, requiring 198 history qubits for the 533-step schedule
and 1,065 qubits in total. The discovered circuit chooses the intermediate cutoff jstore = 5: it decodes depths two
through four with the rules given below. Storing only the deeper decisions shrinks the history stack from 198 to 60
qubits. At the low-qubit extreme, jstore = 9 decodes through j = 8 and reserves 24 history qubits. Varying the cutoff
and the workspace produces a family of circuits that spans from 3.6 million Toffolis and 1,031 qubits to 7.9 million
Toffolis and 893 qubits; an additional higher-qubit configuration uses 3.3 million Toffolis and 1,191 qubits.

Branch decoders. A forward step computes b from the parity of g and uses it to control the update. After the
update, g no longer holds that parity, so the step is left with b in a qubit of its own. Uncomputing b there would
require the decoder on the forward walk as well. The forward step instead disposes of b in one of two ways. If the
decision is ambiguous at depth j ≥ jstore , the step pushes b onto the history stack. Otherwise, the step removes b by
MBU: it measures b in the X basis. The measurement frees the qubit but leaves a phase that depends on b and on the
measurement outcome.
   The reverse walk is the uncomputation of the forward walk. Once the circuit has used the inverse, it undoes the
forward steps in the opposite order, which restores w and returns the walk registers to zero. The matching reverse step
obtains b again, either by popping the stack or by applying the tests and decoders to the updated state. It applies the
phase correction for the measurement outcome and uses b to choose which update to undo. The restored g then holds
b as its parity, so a CNOT from the low bit of g clears it. Decoders therefore appear only in reverse steps. A walk that
stores more decisions than the stack holds overflows it and fails.
   The decoders evaluate the second form of Equation (A11), b = bitj−1 (p(f ′ q)−1 mod 2j ). Both f ′ and q are odd,

register bit costs at least one Toffoli. The benefit shrinks as the cost grows: among ambiguous decisions in sampled
walks, 50.1% occur at depth two, 25.0% at depth three, 12.5% at depth four, and 12.4% at all greater depths. Beyond
depth four, each further depth roughly triples the number of terms and removes half as much history as the depth
before it.
Early stopping. A walk terminates when g reaches zero: at that point f = ±1 and the inverse can be read from fv .
Different inputs terminate at different steps, typically between steps 505 and 530 (Figure 3(b)), but the circuit runs a
fixed schedule of 533 steps. The steps after termination must leave the state unchanged. Left alone, the recurrence
would treat g = 0 as an even value, so it would keep doubling fv and incrementing z until both outgrow their registers.
   The circuit therefore freezes a terminated walk. Each forward step tests whether g = 0. On the first step where the
test succeeds, the circuit sets a one-qubit frozen flag, and from then on the flag disables every update. The reverse
walk must clear the flag at that same step. A frozen state looks identical at every later step, so the state alone cannot
reveal when the walk froze. The circuit writes the step index into a small register when it sets the flag. Each reverse
step compares the register with its own index, and a match clears both the flag and the register.
   This machinery costs Toffolis at every step: a test that every bit of g is zero, and one more control on every update.
The test is most expensive in the early steps, where g is widest and termination is least likely. The first 498 steps omit
the test, the flag, and the flag’s controls. A walk that terminates within those steps fails. The measured probability is
1.4 × 10−6 per inversion, and Table S4 uses 3 × 10−6 as an upper bound.
   A walk whose counter z outgrows its register also fails, with probability 9 × 10−8 per inversion; the two events give
the early-stopping row of Table S4.
Removing the power-of-two scale. At termination, f fv equals the desired inverse times 2t , where t counts the
active steps, those before the walk freezes (Proposition A.2):
                                                 f fv ≡ 2t w−1   (mod p).                                               (57)
The stopping time t depends on the input, so different branches of a superposition carry different scales. Classical
implementations avoid this by running a fixed number of steps, which makes the scale a known constant; the circuit
                                                                                                                        13

instead freezes when g = 0. A direct approach would count t in a register, apply a multiplication by 2−t controlled on
that register, and then uncompute the count.
  The circuit avoids this count. It sets r = af fv ≡ 2t aw−1 (mod p), where a is the field value to be multiplied by the
inverse. For the slope of Equation (52), w = Qx − Px is the denominator and a = Qy − Py is the numerator. The
reverse walk then undoes exactly t active steps, and the frozen flag marks which steps are active. The circuit attaches
one modular halving of r to each active reverse step: r 7→ r/2 if r is even and r 7→ (r + p)/2 otherwise. After the
reverse walk, r = aw−1 , the input w is restored, and no record of t remains.
Step-dependent qubit reuse. A naïve layout would give each of the four signed integers f, g, fv , gv its own
permanent 256-bit register, consuming 1,024 qubits before the counter, controls, or history stack are included. These
registers do not all need their full capacity simultaneously: as the walk progresses, f and g need progressively fewer
bits, while fv and gv need more. Each step halves g, and swaps alternate which value plays the role of g. Each step
also doubles a coefficient. The packed Euclidean construction of Luo et al. [40] and its specialization in SI II.2 use
the same fact: a shrinking remainder and growing coefficient occupy opposite ends of one register, as visualized in
Figure S2(a). The discovered divstep circuit applies this idea to f, g, fv , gv using two 256-qubit registers rather than
four: g and fv share the register that initially holds w, and f and gv share the other. Within each register, the growing
coefficient claims positions vacated by its shrinking partner. The width and position of every value at every step are
fixed when the circuit is built. A walk in which a value outgrows its assigned width fails; this event is the integer-size
limit in Table S4.
Fused coordinate arithmetic. For the remaining coordinate arithmetic, the circuit does not call general multipli-
cation, squaring, or reduction circuits. It adds each term of the coordinate formulas straight into the register that
needs it. For the square, the coordinate update needs λ2 only while forming Rx . Splitting λ = λ0 + 2128 λ1 gives

                                        λ2 = λ20 + 2129 λ0 λ1 + 2256 λ21   (mod p).                                   (58)

This expresses the 256-bit square as three products involving only 128-bit halves. The circuit adds them one at a time
directly into the coordinate register and clears each temporary product before computing the next. It therefore avoids
both a register for the completed square and the second full multiplication of the usual approach, which computes the
square, copies it, and uncomputes it.
  The remaining coordinate products exploit the special form of the prime p. Since

                                               2256 ≡ 232 + 977    (mod p),                                           (59)

the circuit can replace any bits above bit 255 with a small correction to the low bits instead of performing a general
modular reduction. The circuit applies this correction while it forms each product, so it never holds a complete product
that it must later clear.
   We require the exact map in Equation (54), including its phase and the return of every ancilla to zero, except on at
most a 2 × 10−4 fraction of inputs [55]. The 16-bit-window algorithm of Babbush et al. [8] invokes the point adder 28
times; by a union bound, the failure budget adds less than 0.6% retry overhead.
   The four divstep walks use 2.5 million Toffolis, 63% of the circuit. The field multiplications around the walks use
1.4 million, 35% of the circuit. The fused square and the coordinate additions use the remaining 0.10 million. The
935-qubit peak occurs during a walk. The algebraic identities behind the recurrence, branch decoders, fused square,
and sparse-prime reductions are exact. Failures arise only from the finite stopping-time, integer-size, history, carry,
and comparison limits.
   Reducing modulo the sparse prime adds a short constant, 232 + 977 or a small multiple of it, to the low end of a
256-bit register. In principle the carry from this addition can travel up the whole register. In practice it rarely travels
far, because it keeps moving only while the bits above it are ones. The circuit therefore propagates each such carry
through only the low 58 bits (60 in the fused square) and drops any carry that would go further. This happens with
probability about 2−26 per reduction. The comparison limit uses the same idea: to decide whether a modular addition
overflowed, the circuit compares only the top 27 bits of the two operands, which gives the wrong answer only when
those bits all agree. Table S4 collects all contributions [56]; each entry is the per-use failure rate times the number of
uses in one point addition. Among 100,000 tests of the full circuit, four fail.

this test and arithmetic for every position that any input could require, even though a particular input uses only a
much smaller range of positions.

the packed values. The path that the boundary between a remainder and a coefficient traces during an inversion is the
input’s Euclidean walk.

its inverse, one multiplication forms the quotient in an accumulator, an X-basis measurement erases the old numerator,

Every step consists of in-place reversible operations on the packed registers and counters, so the reverse pass is the
inverse circuit and needs no stored history. By contrast, the divstep circuit must recover a decision at every step.

inputs, which count against the 2 × 10−4 failure budget per point addition. The third is exact.

cut adds 1.3 × 10−5 , and the truncated multipliers add at most 2 × 10−6 . The total is about 8 × 10−5 per point
addition. Among 100,000 tests of the full circuit, four fail.


                                 III.   NUMERICAL FUNCTION EVALUATION

   Elementary-function evaluation has been a central problem in numerical computing since the earliest mathematical
software libraries. The routines conventionally collected in libm, the mathematical-function library supplied by C and
Unix systems, evaluate roots, exponentials, logarithms, and trigonometric functions by combining range reduction, table
lookup, polynomial approximation, and iterative refinement [57]. These functions also appear throughout quantum
algorithms. They determine controlled rotation angles in quantum linear-systems algorithms [12, 58], set interaction
phases in real-space Hamiltonian simulation [59, 60], and enter the amplitudes prepared for Hamiltonian simulation [4].
   These classical techniques cannot simply be ported into coherent quantum circuits. Data-dependent choices must be
compiled into a fixed gate sequence, and all temporary state must ultimately be returned to |0⟩. The latter reversibility
constraint is particularly costly for numerical routines because many classical methods proceed through a long sequence
of large, high-precision intermediate values. An ordinary implementation can overwrite each value and reuse the same
storage as soon as the next value is formed. A reversible implementation must instead retain enough information to
reconstruct the overwritten value, or recompute it when clearing the circuit. Consequently, composing individually
efficient routines can accumulate many large live registers and may require running the complete computation backward
after its result has been consumed. Iterative Newton-style methods repeatedly replace one full-precision estimate with
another, so their reversible implementations carry large intermediate registers through every step [61]. Floating-point
representations add exponent bits to every such register to track a wide range of magnitudes [62]. The approximation,
fixed-point representation, and uncomputation schedule therefore become inseparable parts of circuit design [12].
   We consider three numerical subroutines:         Harrow–Hassidim–Lloyd (HHL) rotation-angle evaluation (arcsin),
                                                                               2
Coulomb-potential evaluation (1/ ∥x∥2 ), and Gaussian evaluation (e−x ) [2, 58, 59]. Each implements an ad-
                                       p

ditive fixed-point map |x⟩ |k⟩ 7→ |x⟩ |k + fe(x)⟩, with a uniform error bound over its promised domain [63]. The additive
form serves both common uses of the result. An ordinary accumulator k holds the value for later bitwise-controlled
operations, while a phase-gradient accumulator converts the addition directly into a phase.
   Our baselines use the piecewise-polynomial construction of Häner, Roetteler, and Svore (HRS) [12]. HRS partitions
the domain, fits a low-degree polynomial on each interval, and coherently loads the selected polynomial’s coefficients.
It evaluates the polynomial in general fixed-point arithmetic by Horner’s rule, which uses one multiplication per degree.
It adds the result to the output and reverses the computation to clear its temporary registers. Its cost has four sources:
locating an interval with arbitrary boundaries; the wide multiplications of the Horner polynomial-evaluation stage;
the registers that hold intermediate products and the polynomial value; and the reverse pass that erases the loaded
coefficients. For each task we tune the polynomial degree, fixed-point format, and published recomputation trade-offs,
and widen each interval until it reaches the error bound. We then use the complete gate-level implementation with the
lowest T Q-cost.
   The three discovered circuits still implement piecewise-polynomial approximations. They keep the HRS approximation
family but reorganize it for reversible execution, addressing the four costs in turn. They choose finer partitions whose
intervals can be identified from the input bits. No comparison is then needed, and a polynomial of lower degree on
fewer input bits suffices: the HHL and Gaussian circuits replace cubics by quadratics, and the Coulomb circuit replaces
quadratics by lines. Where the intervals differ in width, as in the HHL circuit, a rearrangement of the input bits maps
them into one shared local coordinate, so that one arithmetic kernel serves every interval. This is the conditional
routing of the main text: a cheap, interval-dependent rearrangement of the data surrounds one shared kernel. The
arithmetic accumulates directly into its destination, avoiding registers for full products and for the function value.
MBU (Methods) then clears the loaded coefficients without reversing the lookup.
   One supporting technique makes these choices safe. An offline search fits the coefficients to the exact quantized and
truncated integer computation that the circuit performs, rather than rounding an ideal real-valued approximation
afterward. Coefficient widths, omitted product columns, shifts, truncation, and rounding therefore share one end-to-end
error budget.
   All three circuits use these techniques. They differ in how the input reaches the arithmetic. The HHL circuit, which
we describe in full, uses intervals of unequal width and cyclically shifts the input bits into a shared coordinate. The

Coulomb circuit first builds the squared distance and normalizes it. The Gaussian circuit folds its input onto the

Frame-block Select. Many Pauli operators in this Hamiltonian are related by fixed Clifford transformations,
which we call frames. Suppose, for example, that two commuting frames generate four related Pauli operators from
one representative. Instead of selecting four operators independently, the circuit selects the representative once and
uses two bits of the address (the selected row index) to control the two frames. More generally, k such bits can identify
up to 2k operators in a block. The circuit pays for one block representative plus its controlled frames, rather than
every Pauli operator separately.
   The circuit applies a frame by conjugation. It applies σ † to the system register under a control, then the representative
P , then σ under the same control. The result is σP σ † when the control is set and P otherwise. Each frame is applied
once for the whole table rather than once per block. Blocks that use the same frames are contiguous, so a few
comparisons of the address with fixed boundaries determine which frames are active.
   These relations have a physical origin: the model contains two equivalent spin channels, two similar iron sites, and
corresponding orbitals on repeated parts of the cluster. These repetitions create Pauli operators that are related by
a fixed transformation. Each frame is a permutation or change of basis of the spin-orbitals. The Jordan–Wigner
mapping turns it into a fixed Clifford transformation of the 40 system qubits.
   Two of the frames used here come from exact spin symmetries of the Hamiltonian. Because the model treats the
spin-up and spin-down channels identically, σαβ exchanges the spin-up and spin-down modes of every spatial orbital.
Spin-rotation symmetry produces a second pairing after the Jordan–Wigner mapping: σXY exchanges the X and
Y factors and adjusts the accompanying Z strings required by that mapping. Both transformations preserve the
corresponding coefficient magnitudes.

are not symmetries of the full Hamiltonian. The frame σFe is the approximate left–right reflection of the cluster,

 distinguishes them. For example, the eight-row pattern A, A, B, B, A, A, B, B consists of two identical halves, so the
 top address bit is irrelevant and the circuit looks up only A, A, B, B. Together, this scheduling and repeated-subtree
 pruning reduce the cost of the alias-table QROM to 10,000 Toffolis, compared with 41,000 for a row-by-row traversal
 and 13,000 for the baseline lookup with merged repeated alias words.
    The rest of the circuit follows the baseline. A comparator computes h, a controlled swap moves the selected index
 into place, and both are reversed after Select, for 64 Toffolis. The circuit then clears the keep and alt fields by MBU
 and corrects the resulting phase in one pass over the address i, for 782 Toffolis.
  We test every table row and a further 224 uniformly random inputs. The discovered circuit uses 15,000 Toffolis and
126 qubits, for a T Q-cost of 1.9 × 106 . Relative to the baseline, this reduces the Toffoli count by 3.7×, the qubit count
by 9.4%, and the T Q-cost by 4.1×.
    The amount of compression is specific to this Hamiltonian, but the preprocessing can search for such relations in
 other sparse Pauli tables, and it falls back to conventional Select and QROM costs when it finds none. Exact spin
 symmetry is common in spin-independent molecular Hamiltonians, so the scheduling strategy should carry over. The
 opportunity is smaller when a Zeeman term or spin–orbit coupling breaks the spin symmetries. In DFTHC, analogous
 choices arise in the factorization itself [69, 72]: the factorization is not unique, and a circuit-aware choice could make
 its coefficient data repeated or sparse and therefore cheaper to load.

                              V.     NONABELIAN LATTICE-GAUGE SIMULATION

   In Hamiltonian lattice gauge theory, space is replaced by a grid. A gauge-field configuration assigns a group element
Ui (x) to each directed edge, or link, from site x in direction i. This element describes how the gauge field transports a
state between neighboring sites; traversing the link backward contributes its inverse Ui (x)−1 . Multiplying the links
around a closed path therefore describes the net transport along that loop. Although this product depends on an
arbitrary convention at each site (the gauge), the real part of its matrix trace does not, and these loop traces form the
magnetic energy [73]. Because the gauge group is nonabelian, its elements need not commute, so the links must be
multiplied in the order in which the path traverses them.
   We consider the group-register formulation of Hamiltonian lattice gauge theory [73], in which a register holds the
group element of each link. The Hamiltonian separates into an electric (kinetic) term HE and a magnetic (potential)
term HB . A Trotter step evolves these terms separately. Here we implement the magnetic evolution e−i∆tHB for the
improved Hamiltonian of Carena et al. [74]. The smallest loop contributing to HB goes around one square face of the
lattice and is called a plaquette; the standard magnetic term sums a trace over these four-link loops. The improved
Hamiltonian adds weighted 2 × 1 rectangles, which cancel the leading discretization error. Thus the magnetic evolution
requires the same basic operation at many locations: follow a short closed path, multiply its links in order, extract a
trace, and add the weighted result to the phase. The compilation task studied here is one application of the magnetic
evolution to the whole lattice, which we call the magnetic layer.
   We use a periodic 4 × 4 × 4 lattice [75]. The lattice has 192 links, and the layer contains 192 four-link plaquettes
and 384 six-link rectangles. For directions i and j, representative loop products are

Specialized running-product representation. Repeatedly multiplying the running product W by links dominates
the cost, so the circuit uses a representation tailored to the restricted update W ← W g rather than to arbitrary group

Reusing loop products. The baseline constructs each loop product from the identity and then reverses the
construction. Neighboring loops, however, often share a sequence of factors. The discovered circuit therefore retains
W after one trace evaluation and edits it into the product needed for the next, reusing the shared factors instead of
rebuilding them.
   More loops share factors than first appears, because the real trace is invariant under cyclic rotation and inversion:

                                 f (h1 h2 · · · hn ) = f (h2 · · · hn h1 ) = f ((h1 h2 · · · hn )−1 ).                (86)

real part. For example, to change abcde into abcxy, the circuit right-multiplies by e−1 and d−1 to cancel the old suffix,
then appends x and y, retaining the common prefix abc. Inverting W , which costs 21 Toffolis, makes the other end of
the product available for the same operation. Cyclic rotations and inversion also give several equivalent ways to write
each loop, which we call its representatives, all with the same value of f . As Figure S5(a) illustrates, a good choice can
expose a longer segment shared with the next loop. A classical dynamic program chooses the representatives and the
order in which the loops are evaluated, minimizing the cost of transforming W between consecutive representatives.
The chosen schedule uses 3,136 link multiplications and 480 inversions of W for the 576 loops, about 5.4 multiplications
per loop. By comparison, building and reversing each loop separately takes 6 multiplications for a plaquette and 10 for
a rectangle.

the seven values occurs. In the specialized representation, a small Boolean circuit acting directly on (Pe, M, υ, Q, R)

Corollary A.7 (Finite dependence). For each fixed odd p, the bit b at depth j is determined by at most 2j bits of the

Proof. By Theorem A.6, the residues of f ′ and q modulo 2j determine b, and each residue is represented by j bits.
