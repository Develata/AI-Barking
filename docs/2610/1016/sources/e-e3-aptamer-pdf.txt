Despite recent breakthroughs in AI-driven molecular generation, existing discovery pipelines operate
largely in silico, with wet-lab feedback reserved for final validation rather than iterative improvement.
Whether large language model (LLM)-based reasoning agents can meaningfully accelerate de novo discov­
ery by iterating with real-world lab data remains unproven.
Here we use protein-binding aptamer design as a high-throughput sandbox (1–2 week validation cycles,
$1–$3 per candidate) to demonstrate the potential of a wet lab in a closed loop with AI systems. We tasked
agentic pipelines with developing aptamer binders against the protein targets 𝛼-thrombin and myoglobin
across three design–test–improve cycles with a budget of ∼1,000 candidates per round (∼4,400 total
sequenced constructs). Statistically significant, bead-corrected on-target hit rates among newly designed
candidates rose across rounds from 0.0% in Round 1 to 26.0% in Round 2 and 42.1% in Round 3 on 𝛼-
thrombin (37.1% excluding literature motif descendants), and from 10.2% in Round 1 to 3.7% in Round 2
and 33.5% in Round 3 on myoglobin. Orthogonal single-clone bead-binding titrations of eight full-length 𝛼
-thrombin candidates confirmed quantitative concordance with pooled sequencing enrichment (Pearson
𝑟 = 0.90). In these assays, literature-derived motifs engineered by the agents to reduce off-target reactivity
in the pooled screen reached preliminary apparent affinities of 𝐾𝐷 = 24.6–133 nM, whereas strictly de
novo scaffolds showed low-micromolar binding (𝐾𝐷 > 1 μM).
We also report how our pipelines used binding data on myoglobin to initially exploit the high-affinity
G-quadruplex binder space, then diagnose cross-reactivity as on-target specificity dropped from 75.0%
to 19.1%, and subsequently recover by developing new strategies that restored myoglobin specificity to
72.4%. We demonstrate that empirical lab data is much more valuable than existing computational proxies,
showing how iterative experimental feedback enables AI agents to diagnose assay artifacts and refine
molecular designs across rounds.



Introduction
Multi-agent systems for scientific discovery, often termed “AI co-scientists,” have begun to accelerate bio­
logical research by generating testable hypotheses, mining sequence databases, and designing candidate
molecules [1, 2, 3, 4]. Although active learning is well established in high-throughput screening [5, 6],
initial studies with large language model (LLM)-based reasoning agents have validated their designs in
only a single experimental pass at the end of the workflow [1, 2, 3, 4]. Extending these systems into multi-
round experimental loops, where agents formulate hypotheses, diagnose failures from empirical data, and
revise their strategy across successive wet-lab cycles, therefore represents a natural next frontier.
AI agents operate within software harnesses that interleave reasoning with tool execution, code gener­
ation, literature retrieval, and persistent memory, allowing them to update their decisions as new data
arrive [7, 8, 9]. As both general-purpose and biology-specific agent harnesses rapidly proliferate [1, 2, 8,
9], physical wet-lab validation has become the primary bottleneck in biological discovery. In chemistry
and materials science, robotic laboratory automation has helped overcome this bottleneck, giving rise to

“self-driving laboratories” where AI systems plan, physically execute, and analyze iterative experiments
with minimal human intervention [10, 11]. In biomolecular design, closing the loop is far harder because
synthesis, expression, and functional validation remain slow and expensive [12].
In domains such as games, mathematics, and software engineering, self-play and iterative agentic search
succeed because verification is purely computational: rulesets, compilers, and formal proof assistants
return ground-truth feedback in seconds [8, 13, 14]. In biomedicine, systems such as AI co-scientist [1] and
Virtual Lab [2] have shown that in silico debate, tournament ranking, and tool execution can effectively
surface high-value hypotheses and candidate designs from literature priors and computational models.
In molecular modalities where computational predictors are still maturing, however, pairing this in silico
reasoning with rapid physical measurement offers a way to ground agentic search directly in empirical
data. Computational predictors sit on a spectrum of reliability dictated by their training data and cannot
replace wet-lab validation for any modality [17, 29]: even in protein engineering, where structure-based
design succeeds on canonical rigid interfaces [15, 16, 17], predictors struggle on flexible antibody loops and
out-of-distribution targets [29, 30], and models for nucleic acids and aptamers remain poorly calibrated for
affinity and specificity due to sparse structural data [18, 34]. Demonstrating closed-loop learning requires
a design space where computational predictions alone fall short, paired with an experimental assay that is
fast enough for multi-round iteration and returns quantitative, multiplexed readouts across both binders
and non-binders.
Here we establish a closed-loop framework for agentic biomolecular discovery using single-stranded DNA
aptamers. While earlier aptamer studies trained supervised or generative models on large SELEX datasets
or coupled microarray screening with genetic algorithms and Bayesian optimization [31, 32, 33, 36, 37],
we ask whether LLM-based reasoning agents can design de novo binders without prior target-specific
training data, learn iteratively from pooled measurements, and compress the time and resources required
by conventional selection [19, 20]. Across three closed-loop design–test–refine rounds against 𝛼-thrombin
and myoglobin (∼1,000 candidates per round), agentic pipelines improved statistically significant, bead-
corrected on-target hit rates among newly designed candidates from 0.0% (0/167) to 26.0% (54/208) to
42.1% (131/311) on 𝛼-thrombin (37.1% [106/286] across strictly de novo candidates free of known literature
motifs) and from 10.2% (20/196) to 3.7% (9/244) to 33.5% (184/549) on myoglobin. Rather than screening
1013 –1015 random molecules by blind selection as in classical SELEX, this approach tests targeted pools
of ∼1,000 designed sequences per round to return quantitative positive and negative feedback on every
hypothesis. Orthogonal single-clone bead-based fluorescence titrations confirmed that pooled sequencing
enrichment scales with physical binding (𝑟 = 0.90), yielding preliminary nanomolar affinities (apparent
𝐾𝐷 = 24.6–133 nM) for agent-stabilized literature motifs and micromolar binding onset (𝐾𝐷 > 1 μM)
for strictly de novo scaffolds. When multi-target counter-screening exposed a collapse in specificity driven
by promiscuous binding motifs, the agents diagnosed the failure mode from raw sequencing data and
pivoted to alternative structural families to restore target selectivity. These results establish the feasibility
of closed-loop, agent-guided biomolecular discovery across multi-round experimental campaigns.

Results
R.1 Aptamers are the ideal sandbox for lab-in-the-loop molecular design
To test whether AI agents can learn iteratively from wet-lab data, we built a closed-loop pipeline around
single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) aptamers (Fig. 1). In each cycle, an agentic design system equipped with
literature search, target-structure lookup, biophysical folding tools, and a Python environment for writing
code and running scientific libraries analyzes sequencing data from prior rounds and proposes a pool
of ∼1,000 candidate sequences (Fig. 1B). Those candidates are synthesized as oligonucleotide pools,
screened against immobilized target proteins by magnetic-bead pulldown, and read out by next-generation
sequencing (NGS), returning quantitative enrichment profiles to the agents for the next round of design
(Fig. 1B, C).

Aptamers are well suited for closed-loop molecular design because the nucleotide sequence is the
functional molecule (Fig. 1A). In protein and antibody campaigns, moving from a candidate sequence
to a binding readout requires gene synthesis, cloning, transformation, cell culture and expression, cell
lysis, column purification, and the binding assay itself. This seven-step cascade takes 3–6 weeks per
round and introduces dropouts across expression, folding, solubility, and purification between what the
model designs and what gets tested (Fig. 1A). Conventional SELEX, the traditional method for aptamer
discovery, avoids cloning but relies on blind physical enrichment from random libraries (1013 –1015
molecules) across 8–15 six-step selection rounds of binding, PCR amplification, and 𝜆-exonuclease single-
strand regeneration before sequencing readout [19, 20], yielding minimal information on why individual
sequences succeed or fail (Fig. 1A). By contrast, an agentic pipeline can evaluate a wide range of structural
ideas and sequence variants in silico before committing to a focused synthesis pool of ∼1,000 candidates.

Our workflow pairs pooled oligo synthesis with deep sequencing of both the pre-selection input library
and triplicate target and bare-bead pulldowns, compressing each wet-lab cycle from candidate to readout
to three bench steps completed in ∼10 days (including oligo pool synthesis and delivery) at a cost of ∼$3 per
candidate (pooled oligo synthesis and reagent cost), decoupling synthesis bias from binding and returning
quantitative positive and negative labels for every designed sequence in the pool (Fig. 1A, C).
The aptamer sandbox turns molecular discovery into an iterative reasoning problem well suited to modern
AI agent harnesses, returning clean positive and negative labels for ∼1,000 designs every ∼10 days within
a fixed experimental budget. We asked whether agents could operate in a closed loop with the bench to
discover binders de novo and systematically improve their designs across rounds.
R.2 Hit rate improves from 0 to 42% with the lab in the loop, outperforming zero-shot predictors
We evaluated the closed-loop pipeline across three design–test–refine rounds on two proteins of contrast­
ing difficulty: human myoglobin, a compact eight-helix bundle lacking a natural nucleic-acid binding cleft
(de novo target), and human 𝛼-thrombin, a serine protease with two electropositive grooves that naturally
bind nucleic acids (positive-control benchmark; Fig. 2A; Methods; Supplementary Table S3).
Across rounds, statistically significant, bead-corrected binder hit rates among newly designed candidates
rose sharply on both targets (Fig. 2B). On myoglobin, agents discovered de novo binders in Round 1 at a
10.2% hit rate (20/196), dipped to 3.7% (9/244) in Round 2 as non-specific G-quadruplex matrix binders were
subtracted by counter-screening (Section R.3), and surged 9.1-fold in Round 3 to 33.5% (184/549, 𝑝 = 9.6 ×
10−24 ; 100% strictly de novo; Fig. 2B). On 𝛼-thrombin, where uninsulated Round 1 designs failed due to
primer–core hybridization (0.0%, 0/167), hit rates climbed to 26.0% in Round 2 (54/208) and 42.1% in Round
3 (131/311; 37.1% [106/286] across strictly de novo candidates free of known thrombin motifs; Fig. 2B;
Supplementary Table S3). All random-sequence negative controls remained at 0.0% across every round.
Directly beneath the hit-rate curves, raw target-versus-input enrichment distributions shifted toward
stronger binding with each cycle (Fig. 2C). Prior to bare-bead subtraction, the fraction of each pool
enriched at least twofold (log2 FC ≥ 1) rose monotonically in every round—climbing from 6.5% to 12.4%
to 29.9% on myoglobin and from 0.3% to 53.1% to 80.7% on 𝛼-thrombin (Fig. 2C)—confirming that the
Round 2 dip in myoglobin hit rate (Fig. 2B) reflected stringency filtering of sticky sequences rather than
a loss of library enrichment.
To test whether computational predictors could have replaced these wet-lab cycles, we benchmarked 12
open-license predictor metrics (spanning 9 tools) zero-shot across the Round 3 myoglobin library (𝑁 =
1,441; Fig. 2D). Target-conditioned 3D co-folding (Boltz-2 iPTM and Δ iPTM: ROC-AUC = 0.499
and 0.391), 3D inverse-folding and structure models (ESMFold2-NA, RhoDesign, RiboDiffusion: 0.496–0.510),
nucleic-acid foundation models (DNABERT-2, HyenaDNA, RNA-FM: 0.364–0.523), and G-quadruplex scorers
(G4Hunter 0.366, ViennaRNA ΔΔ𝐺G4 0.476) all performed near or below random chance (0.50). Only target-
agnostic 2D DNA thermodynamic stability (−Δ𝐺MFE , ROC-AUC = 0.729) and folding purity (−ED,
0.763) from ViennaRNA [22] showed meaningful signal (Fig. 2D): ViennaRNA filters out unstructured coils
and primer-invaded misfolds, but has no knowledge of the protein target and therefore cannot substitute
for empirical wet-lab selection.

R.4 Agentic pipelines combine structural hypotheses with synthesis, assay, and statistical rea­
soning
Beyond improving aggregate hit rates, inspecting the design rationales and campaign analyses across
Rounds 1–3 reveals that the agents reasoned across the entire experimental stack (Table 1). Rather than
acting as black-box sequence generators, the pipelines: (1) explored atypical de novo structural topologies
tailored to target surface geometry; (2) developed statistical counter-selection filters to separate specific
binding from matrix noise and evaluated which computational scoring tools to trust; (3) proposed practical
tweaks to wet-lab assay protocols, including liquid-handling automation and buffer optimization (logged
as autonomous experimental recommendations while wet-lab buffer conditions were held constant across
Rounds 1–3); and (4) connected in silico sequence design to physical bottlenecks across solid-phase DNA
synthesis, full-construct primer folding, and PCR amplification/sequencing recovery. The excerpts in
Table 1 illustrate reasoning traces and intermediate hypotheses generated by individual pipeline stages,
documenting the prospective decision chain from Round 2 failure diagnosis to Round 3 library synthesis
and validation. These are distinct from our post-hoc analyses (Methods) presented in Figs. 2 and 3, and
were often refined or superseded in later steps. Progress likely came not from any single design rationale,
but from iteratively pairing broad structural exploration with biophysical filtering and wet-lab feedback.

Discussion
Our results demonstrate the feasibility of executing multi-round biomolecular discovery in a closed loop
between AI agents and wet-lab assays, showing how iterative experimental feedback enables recovery
from initial design failures. Across all biomolecular modalities, computational structure and binding
predictors vary widely in accuracy depending on their training data, and none can replace experimental
validation in the lab [17, 29]. While structure-based models perform well on rigid, well-represented protein
interfaces [15, 16, 17], their accuracy drops on out-of-distribution targets, flexible loops such as antibody
CDRs [29, 30], and nucleic-acid aptamers where solved complex structures and negative binding data are
scarce [18, 24, 34]. Most agentic studies to date have been limited to a single experimental pass because
protein and antibody campaigns require multi-week cloning, expression, and purification cascades [1, 2,
3, 4, 12]. Single-stranded DNA aptamers, chemically synthesized affinity reagents used in diagnostics,
biosensors, and analytical assays [19, 20, 24], are well suited as a model modality to establish and stress-
test the lab-in-the-loop paradigm. Within three experimental cycles and ∼1,000 candidates per round, our
pipelines lifted statistically significant, bead-corrected on-target hit rates among newly designed candi­
dates from 0.0% (0/167) to 26.0% (54/208) in Round 2 and 42.1% (131/311) in Round 3 on 𝛼-thrombin (37.1%
across strictly de novo candidates free of published thrombin cores) and from 10.2% (20/196) in Round 1 to
3.7% (9/244) in Round 2 and 33.5% (184/549) in Round 3 on myoglobin, reaching 80%–100% hit rates within
top-performing structural families and up to a 140 × ratio of on-target to off-target hit rates. Rather than
relying on blind selection over 1013 –1015 random molecules as in classical SELEX [19, 20], testing targeted
pools of ∼1,000 designed sequences per round returned a complete, counter-screened sequence–activity
dataset of both positives and negatives across all ∼4,400 tested candidates (Supplementary Table S3).
Whereas earlier computational aptamer methods trained supervised or generative models on large SELEX
datasets or mutated sequences under a fixed evolutionary or Bayesian objective [31, 32, 33, 36, 37], the
agents’ search space within our loop extended to both the analytical workflow and atypical structural
topologies that human designers rarely prioritize (Section R.4). Acting like bench experimentalists, the
agents derived a two-stage statistical filter (differential enrichment gated by linear bead-background
regression) that removed 36–55% of apparent volcano pre-hits across full pools (and up to 71–85% of
raw enriched G-quadruplexes; Table 1; Supplementary Figs. S1 and S2; Supplementary Table S3),
modeled full primer-flanked constructs [22] to diagnose primer–core hybridization, built pass/fail quality-
control gates to separate assay failure from design failure, and revised their own hypotheses after a
single round of contradicting wet-lab data. When early selection rounds rewarded G-quadruplexes, which
dominate published aptamer databases, multiplexed counter-screening exposed that 97% of cross-reactive
sequences were G-quadruplexes acting as promiscuous polyanionic surface stickers [24, 26]. This finding
prompted the agents to pivot toward designs predicted to form three-way junctions and H-type pseudo­
knots (80.4% hit rate on myoglobin; Fig. 3B). This ability to dial between literature-guided exploitation
on tractable targets (𝛼-thrombin) and atypical structural exploration on recalcitrant targets (myoglobin)
gives human scientists high-level directional control over the discovery campaign.

These dynamics explain why computational models cannot yet substitute for physical measurement in
nucleic-acid discovery, and they highlight the hazard of active learning on confounded data. Three-
dimensional co-folding and structure-based models (Boltz-2 [21], ESMFold2-NA, RhoDesign, RiboDiffusion) and
nucleic-acid foundation models performed near random chance (ROC-AUC = 0.36–0.52; Fig. 2D), con­
sistent with known limitations caused by scarce aptamer crystal structures and lab-to-lab irreproducibility
in public training corpora [18, 24, 25, 26, 34]. In fact, raw Boltz-2 interface confidence scores anticorrelated
with specific binding because the model rewarded compact G-quadruplex folds that adhered non-specif­
ically to solid supports (Fig. 2D). While 2D thermodynamic folding (ViennaRNA [22]) showed moderate
enrichment discrimination (ROC-AUC = 0.73–0.76; Fig. 2D), it is a purely sequence-intrinsic, target-
agnostic stability calculator: it verifies that a DNA sequence folds into a clean secondary structure rather
than a random coil, but has no knowledge of the protein target and therefore cannot predict target affinity
or specificity. When a supervised classifier was trained on early selection rounds prior to bead-background
subtraction, it learned G-tract frequency as a positive feature and inverted its accuracy on subsequent
pools. Active learning built on un-decoupled assay data will therefore converge on experimental artifacts
rather than genuine affinity. Chemically synthesized DNA pools partially mitigate this risk once paired
with counter-selection: unlike protein screens where 60–80% of designs fail to express or fold in cells [15,
23], sequencing the input library alongside triplicate pulldowns verifies that every synthesized candidate
that fails to enrich genuinely failed to bind, providing clean negative and positive labels for downstream
model training.
The specificity recovery in Round 3 resolved indiscriminate cross-binding to streptavidin beads and
unrelated proteins, restoring myoglobin on-target specificity from 19.1% to 72.4% while lifting 𝛼-thrombin
specificity from 76.7% to 98.5% (Fig. 3A). More broadly, this progression illustrates a key advantage of
iterative closed-loop discovery over one-shot screening: rather than forcing agents or assays to satisfy
all design constraints simultaneously, each cycle can layer on a new selective pressure. The loop can
optimize first for target affinity, then for counter-screened specificity, and in subsequent rounds for
activity in complex biological matrices such as serum, cell lysate, or cellular uptake. Catching and
correcting assay-level matrix artifacts is an essential first gate, although fine discrimination against close
structural homologues (such as myoglobin versus hemoglobin, or 𝛼-thrombin versus prothrombin) was
not tested here and remains a next step for counter-selection campaigns. Similarly, after an initial screen
across a broader protein panel spanning clinical relevance and surface charge, the three proteins profiled
in our rounds spanned a clear biophysical difficulty spectrum for nucleic-acid recognition: 𝛼-thrombin
[27], which presents two strongly electropositive polyanion-binding grooves (pI ≈ 7.0–7.6) and four
active literature controls; myoglobin, a compact neutral-to-weakly-basic 8-helix bundle (17 kDa, pI ≈
7.2) lacking a natural nucleic-acid cleft where zero of four published controls bound; and the human
HER2 ectodomain introduced in Round 2 counter-screening, a large (70 kDa), heavily glycosylated, net-
electronegative receptor (pI ≈ 5.6) that electrostatically repels the polyanionic DNA backbone and where
all 24 published or literature-seeded controls failed (Supplementary Fig. S3, Supplementary Note 1).
Final Round 3 hit rates and peak enrichments scaled monotonically across this gradient—from 𝛼-thrombin
(42.1% on-target hit rate [37.1% strictly de novo], peak log2 FC = +16.27) to myoglobin (33.5% hit rate,
+3.61 log2 FC) to strictly selective HER2 binders (5.1% hit rate, +4.59 log2 FC, 3.8-fold gain over Round 2)
—while matching the distinct agentic strategies required at each tier: construct insulation on 𝛼-thrombin,
a topology pivot to non-G4 pseudoknots and hairpins on myoglobin, and cross-target seed repurposing
with Watson–Crick clamping on HER2. The agents themselves diagnosed why the published myoglobin
controls were inactive in our standardized pulldown assay [24, 25, 26], tracing the discrepancy to primer-
flank hybridization, physiological Na+ /K+ buffer balance, and bead immobilization versus solution-phase
selection (Table 1, Rows 3 and 4). They then recommended adding exogenous heme cofactor, substituting
K+ , or adjusting wash stringency (Section R.4). We held physiological buffer conditions constant across
all rounds to evaluate sequence-level learning on a fixed benchmark, though future loops should allow
agents to co-optimize sequences and assay protocols.

On targets with prior literature such as 𝛼-thrombin, the agents leveraged literature search and model priors
to retrieve known TBA and NU172 cores and re-engineer their flanking stems to survive full-construct folding,
lifting their hit rate from 0.0% in Round 1 to 100% in Round 3 (25/25; Table 1, Row 5; Supplementary
Table S3). Separating these literature-guided designs from strictly de novo candidates confirmed that de
novo discovery also succeeded independently: strictly de novo 𝛼-thrombin designs reached a 37.1% hit
rate (106/286), and myoglobin—where no published controls bound—yielded a 33.6% hit rate (184/547),
with all hits strictly de novo. Orthogonal single-clone bead-fluorescence titrations on 𝛼-thrombin (T01–T08)
further verified that Round 3 pooled sequencing enrichment translates quantitatively into single-clone
equilibrium binding (𝑟 = 0.90, 𝑝 = 0.0021; Fig. 4; Supplementary Table S4), calibrating the pooled
sequencing assay against physical binding: 6 of 8 individually synthesized full-length constructs showed
concentration-dependent binding, with agent-engineered stem clamps and loop edits of known motifs
yielding preliminary nanomolar apparent affinities (𝐾𝐷 = 24.6–133 nM, T01–T04), moderately enriched
de novo G4 scaffolds (T05–T06) exhibiting clear binding onset at the 1 μM assay ceiling (𝐾𝐷 > 1 μM) even
prior to constant-primer truncation, and lower-enrichment non-G4 de novo designs (T07–T08, log2 FC =
+2.41–3.29) remaining below the 1 μM detection threshold. Whereas prior supervised and generative
machine learning methods yielded single-digit nanomolar binders (𝐾𝐷 = 1.5 nM) by training deep
neural networks on nearly a million physical screening measurements [36, 37], our closed-loop agents
operated without any prior target-specific training data. In this zero-library context, achieving micromolar
binding onset (𝐾𝐷 > 1 μM) for strictly de novo G4 scaffolds (T05–T06) within only three rounds (∼3,000
focal candidates) represents an efficient initial discovery seed, contrasting with the nanomolar apparent
affinities (𝐾𝐷 = 24.6–133 nM) achieved when the agents were provided a known starting motif (TBA/
NU172) for targeted local optimization. As practical study caveats, commercial licensing constraints
restricted our computational evaluations to open-license tools (Boltz-2, ESMFold2-NA, ViennaRNA, and
open foundation models), and our single-clone titrations were performed exclusively on 𝛼-thrombin and
capped at 1 μM on full-length (75–85 nt) bead-immobilized constructs without reaching an empirical
saturation plateau (𝐵max ), rendering the fitted 𝐾𝐷 values preliminary. Extending single-clone validation to
truncated minimal cores, additional closed-loop affinity-maturation rounds, and higher-concentration or
label-free kinetic assays—specifically bio-layer interferometry (BLI) and surface plasmon resonance (SPR)
(𝑘on , 𝑘off , 𝐾𝐷 ) across myoglobin, 𝛼-thrombin, and HER2—will be essential to biophysically confirm strictly
de novo leads, enabling physical affinity to be fed directly back into the closed loop alongside pooled NGS
enrichment and counter-target specificity.
Our findings establish that coupling general-purpose and domain-specialized reasoning agents [1, 7, 8,
9] with fast, counter-screened physical feedback enables autonomous scientific problem-solving across
the full design–test–analyze loop. Across the multi-round campaign, we paired Google’s AI co-scientist
[1] for initial zero-shot ideation in Round 1 with a tool-equipped aptamer harness for data-driven
iteration in Rounds 2–3 rather than running a controlled comparison between agent architectures. While
our campaign establishes closed-loop feasibility, prospective trials with matched-budget no-feedback and
non-reasoning control arms will be needed to isolate the specific contribution of agentic reasoning, and
head-to-head benchmarking against evolutionary, Bayesian, and supervised machine learning baselines
[31, 32, 33, 36, 37] is an important direction for future work. As reasoning models and agent harnesses
continue to advance rapidly, the decisive determinant of discovery success is the speed and fidelity of
the empirical feedback loop. Our three-step wet-lab workflow can be mapped end-to-end onto robotic
liquid handlers coupled directly to sequencing because DNA pools and selection buffers are stable at room
temperature [10, 11, 12, 38], allowing agents to co-optimize sequences, buffers, cofactors, and stringency
in a self-driving loop. Beyond aptamers, the closed-loop principles demonstrated in this aptamer sandbox
are modality-agnostic, setting the stage for extending multi-round agentic discovery to more complex and
resource-intensive assays such as protein–protein binder design [12, 15, 16].

Methods
Closed-loop pipeline overview
Aptamer discovery was performed using a closed-loop pipeline in which AI agents designed candidate
sequences, candidates were synthesized and experimentally screened, and sequencing read count matrices
and sample metadata were returned to the agents to inform the next round of design. The pipeline
comprised five stages executed in series: (i) agentic candidate design, (ii) pooled oligonucleotide array
synthesis, (iii) multiplexed bead-based affinity pulldown, (iv) next-generation sequencing, and (v) return
of count matrices and sample metadata to the agentic pipeline for iterative analysis and redesign.
Three complete design-test-improve cycles were performed, each completing in approximately 10 days
of bench turnaround (Supplementary Table S3). Agents were tasked with generating approximately
640–1,450 candidate aptamer constructs per synthesized pool: 642 in Round 1, 981 in Round 2, and 1,450
ordered constructs in Round 3 yielding 1,441 sequenced library entries (1,405 after deduplicating secondary
controls, comprising 1,208 newly designed constructs—549 primary myoglobin candidates, 311 primary 𝛼-
thrombin candidates, 200 exploratory HER2 candidates, 88 mutational seed variants, and 60 calibration tier
constructs—plus 197 longitudinal positive, literature, and negative controls; 2,937 unique sequences across
the three focal pools). Including the supplementary single-target myoglobin expansion pool synthesized
between Rounds 1 and 2 (Round 1b, 1,353 ordered / 1,341 sequenced constructs), the campaign totaled 4,405
sequenced library constructs (4,425 ordered; 4,279 unique variable-region sequences after deduplicating
cross-pool controls; Supplementary Table S3). Between rounds, human researchers executed the wet-
lab pulldown, PCR amplification, and sequencing workflow, mapped sequencing reads to the designed
reference library, and returned the resulting per-sequence read count matrices and sample metadata to
the agent pipeline. Within the data-driven cycles (Rounds 2–3), differential enrichment analysis, cross-
target and bare-bead background regression, structural classification, failure-mode diagnosis, constraint
derivation, and candidate scoring were executed independently by the agent pipeline from the raw count
data without receiving human-derived enrichment tables or analytical hypotheses, while wet-lab assay
conditions were held constant across Rounds 1–3 by the human operators.
Agentic design platforms
Two agentic platforms contributed sequentially across the three design rounds, with experimental
sequencing counts and top-performing seeds shared across rounds so that each stage built directly on
prior wet-lab outcomes.
AI Co-Scientist. Google’s AI co-scientist [1], a general-purpose multi-agent system designed for scientific
ideation, multi-agent debate, and literature-grounded hypothesis generation, generated the initial Round
1 candidate library (𝑁 = 642) from target protein sequence and structural context, establishing de novo
binder discovery from zero prior experimental data. Top-performing Round 1 binders were carried forward
into subsequent rounds as exploitation seeds and longitudinal controls.
Custom aptamer pipeline. For Rounds 2–3, where iterative improvement required ingesting prior-round
sequencing count matrices, running independent statistical enrichment analysis in Python, performing
structural classification and full-construct thermodynamic folding, and generating candidates subject to
programmatic design constraints, we deployed a domain-specialized four-stage pipeline implemented on a
modular Python agent orchestration framework with direct access to the computational toolbox described
below. The pipeline employed a four-stage sequential architecture: (1) an analyst agent that received
raw per-sequence read count matrices and sample metadata and independently performed differential
enrichment analysis (PyDESeq2), bare-bead background regression, ratio gating, cross-target profiling,
and dropout/failure-mode diagnostics without receiving human-derived analytical results or structural
hypotheses; (2) a strategist agent that translated the analyst’s report into target-specific design strategies
with explicit explore-versus-exploit allocation; (3) parallel designer agents, each assigned a specific strat­
egy, that iteratively generated, folded, and filtered candidate sequences using the computational tools; and
(4) a scoring stage, executed outside the agent loop on cloud GPUs, that applied computationally intensive

Target proteins
Three target proteins spanning a gradient of surface electrostatics, glycosylation, and nucleic-acid recog­
nition tractability were profiled across the campaign. Human alpha-thrombin (36.7 kDa, pI ≈ 7.0–7.6;
PDB 1PPB) is a serine protease presenting two strongly electropositive anion-binding exosites (Exosite I
and Exosite II) that constitute natural grooves for nucleic acid recognition, including well-characterized G-
quadruplex aptamers. Thrombin served as a positive-control benchmark with known aptamer solutions.
Human myoglobin (17.2 kDa, pI ≈ 7.2; structural modeling reference PDB 3RGK, an engineered K45R/
C110A crystallization variant sharing > 98% sequence identity and an identical eight-𝛼-helix holo-
myoglobin fold with wild-type human myoglobin assayed in the wet lab) is a compact globular protein
coordinating a heme prosthetic group. Its surface presents only localized cationic patches and lacks any
natural nucleic acid binding groove, making it a substantially more challenging target for de novo aptamer
discovery. Human HER2 (ErbB2) extracellular domain (ECD; subdomains I–IV, 630 aa, ∼70 kDa, pI ≈ 5.6;
PDB 1N8Z), introduced in Round 2 as a multiplexed counter-screen and continued in Round 3, is a large,
heavily glycosylated receptor tyrosine kinase ectodomain whose net-electronegative surface at physiolog­
ical pH electrostatically repels the polyanionic DNA backbone and lacks a canonical nucleic-acid binding
cleft, serving both as an off-target specificity control and as a high-difficulty test case (Supplementary
Fig. S3, Supplementary Note 1).
Biotinylated human alpha-thrombin was obtained from Assay Pro (catalog no. PT62011BIO), biotinylated
human myoglobin was obtained from Molecular Depot (catalog no. P2010022B), and biotinylated human
HER2 extracellular domain was obtained from Sino Biological (catalog no. 10004-HCCH-B-100).
Aptamer library design and synthesis
Each round of the pipeline produced a pool of candidate aptamer sequences with variable regions
of 30–50 nucleotides (target length ∼40 nt), flanked by a fixed 23-nt 5′ forward constant region (5'-
TTGACTAGTACATGACCACTTGA-3') and a fixed 22-nt 3′ constant region (5'-TTCTGTCGTCCAGTCTGATGTG-3', written
5′ → 3′ on the synthesized sense ssDNA strand) required for PCR amplification and sequencing library
preparation, yielding full constructs of approximately 75–95 nucleotides (∼85 nt). Variable region lengths
were selected by the designing agents based on the structural architectures they chose to explore.
Candidate pools were synthesized as oligonucleotide arrays by Integrated DNA Technologies (IDT oPools).
Synthesis orders were placed as received from the agentic pipeline without manual curation of individual
sequences.
Multiplexed bead pulldown assay
Binding selection was performed across parallel reaction wells as previously described [35] with minor
modifications. Dynabeads M-280 Streptavidin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, catalog no. 11206D) were washed
and prepared following the manufacturer’s instructions using 50 pmol of biotinylated target protein
conjugated to the beads. Across all selection rounds, for each reaction, 5 pmol of refolded ssDNA was
combined with the protein-conjugated (or bare control) beads. Following incubation, unbound aptamers
were washed away, and bound aptamers were immediately amplified directly off the beads. PCR products
were purified using Mag-Bind TotalPure NGS beads (Omega Bio-tek, catalog no. M1378) as previously
described [35] and quantified by Qubit fluorometry and Agilent TapeStation. Samples were then indexed
using custom NGS primers, purified and pooled as previously described [35], and sequenced on an Illumina
MiSeq instrument (1 × 52 bp single-end reads).
Each pool was assayed simultaneously in parallel reactions against three core bead conditions: (i) alpha-
thrombin-coated beads, (ii) myoglobin-coated beads, and (iii) bare streptavidin beads carrying no target

protein (matrix background control), with each target-coated bead condition also serving as the off-target
protein counter-screen for candidates designed against the other target. In Rounds 2 and 3, human HER2
extracellular domain (ECD; subdomains I–IV, ∼70 kDa, PDB 1N8Z) was also included in the multiplexed
panel as an additional off-target protein reference (Supplementary Fig. S3, Supplementary Note 1).
The technical replicate structure per sequencing run was: Round 1, 𝑛 = 3 target / 𝑛 = 3 bead / 𝑛 = 3
input replicates per protein; Round 1b, 𝑛 = 2 target / 𝑛 = 2 bead / 𝑛 = 3 input; Round 2, 𝑛 = 3 target
and 𝑛 = 3 bead for 𝛼-thrombin and HER2, 𝑛 = 4 target and 𝑛 = 4 bead for myoglobin, and 𝑛 = 3 input
across all assays; Round 3, 𝑛 = 3 target / 𝑛 = 3 bead / 𝑛 = 3 input per protein. This multiplexed design
enabled discrimination between specific target binding, non-specific matrix adhesion, and cross-reactive
promiscuity.
Controls and reproducibility
Literature aptamer controls. Eight published aptamers with reported binding activity against the study
targets (four targeting alpha-thrombin and four targeting myoglobin) were synthesized alongside the AI-
designed candidates and tested in the identical assay format. Of these, thrombin-binding aptamers TBA
(log2 fold-change [LFC] +8.22 in Round 3), HD22 (+3.03), RE31 (+3.87), and NU172 (+4.15) showed
strong enrichment on thrombin beads, verifying assay validity for that target. Zero of four myoglobin
literature aptamers showed measurable enrichment on myoglobin beads.
Random sequence controls. Fifty-three random-sequence oligonucleotides of matched length and
primer composition were included as negative controls. None (0/53) exceeded the enrichment threshold.
Biological replicates. To assess inter-operator reproducibility, the Round 3 candidate pool (𝑁 = 1,441
sequenced constructs; 1,405 unique sequences) was independently screened by two operators in separate
pulldown and sequencing runs. Continuous target-versus-input log2 FC enrichment scores correlated
across operators (𝑟 = 0.84–0.85 across the full pool [𝑟 = 0.840 for myoglobin, 𝑟 = 0.835 for 𝛼-thrombin,
and 𝑟 = 0.849 for HER2]; 𝑟 = 0.920 and 𝑟 = 0.964 within the newly designed myoglobin [𝑛 = 549]
and 𝛼-thrombin [𝑛 = 311] candidate cohorts; and 𝑟 = 0.74–0.76 on bare streptavidin beads and high-
abundance subsets). Binary DESeq2 enrichment pre-hit calls (log2 FC ≥ 1.0, FDR < 0.05) agreed between
operators on 90.4% of library constructs for myoglobin (Cohen’s 𝜅 = 0.740; 345 vs. 357 pre-hits), 88.1% for
HER2 (𝜅 = 0.700; 393 vs. 390 pre-hits), and 77.4% for 𝛼-thrombin (292 vs. 301 pre-hits; 84.9% agreement
[𝜅 = 0.439] after bare-bead regression, with 𝑟 = 0.868 between bead-subtracted residuals).
Next-generation sequencing, data return to agents, and uniform manuscript statistical evalu­
ation
Following bead pulldown and direct on-bead PCR amplification, indexed libraries across Rounds 1–3 were
sequenced on an Illumina MiSeq platform (1 × 52 bp single-end reads). Sequencing reads were mapped to
the designed reference library using a two-pass bioinformatics pipeline: Pass 1 assigned all exact (𝑑 = 0)
variable-region matches (~85–90% of mapped reads), and Pass 2 applied 𝑘-mer-indexed Levenshtein fuzzy
matching (edit distance 1 ≤ 𝑑 ≤ 3 nucleotides) exclusively to reads without an exact match, assigning
each read to its unique minimum-distance reference construct and discarding reads tied at the minimum
edit distance between two reference sequences. In Rounds 1 and 2, only 8.2% (55/669) and 4.4% (43/981) of
unique variable regions had any library neighbor within edit distance ≤ 3; in Round 3 (𝑁 = 1,405), where
single- and double-mutant maturation series (𝑛 = 88) were intentionally included, 28.2% (396/1,405) had a
neighbor within 𝑑 ≤ 3, and exact-first priority matching with tie discarding prevented read bleed between
point-mutant siblings.
The resulting raw per-sequence read count matrices and sample metadata were provided to the agentic
pipeline as structured input for the subsequent design round. Within the closed loop between rounds,
the agents wrote and executed their own analysis scripts (PyDESeq2 differential enrichment, bare-bead
regression, and ratio gating, as quoted in Table 1). Separately from the agents’ internal between-
round scripts, all hit rates, specificity trajectories, volcano plots, and predictor benchmarks
reported in this manuscript (Figures 2–3, Supplementary Table S3, and Supplementary Figures

S1–S3) were computed by the authors using a standardized two-stage statistical pipeline applied
across all rounds:
1. Stage 1 (DESeq2 differential enrichment over input): For each sequencing run and target condi­
   tion 𝑡, raw replicate read counts for constructs passing the minimum detection filter (≥ 10 total
   reads across input and pulldown replicates) were normalized using PyDESeq2 median-of-ratios size
   factors, modeled under Cox–Reid negative binomial dispersion estimation, and tested via Wald
   statistics with Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) adjustment to obtain target-versus-
   input enrichment 𝑦𝑖,𝑡 = log2 FC𝑖,target vs. input (padj𝑖,𝑡 ) and bare-bead-versus-input enrichment 𝑥𝑖 =
   log2 FC𝑖,beads vs. input (padj𝑖,beads ).
2. Stage 2 (Ordinary least-squares bare-bead background regression): The empirical slope 𝛽1,𝑡
   relating 𝑦𝑖,𝑡 to 𝑥𝑖 is systematically sub-unity (𝛽1,𝑡 = 0.40–0.73 across Rounds 2 and 3) because target-
   protein immobilization partially passivates exposed streptavidin matrix sites. We therefore fit an
   ordinary least-squares (OLS) linear model 𝑦̂𝑖,𝑡 = 𝛽0,𝑡 + 𝛽1,𝑡 𝑥𝑖 (calibrated in Round 3 on the 𝑛 = 102
   embedded negative, random, scrambled, and structural baseline controls: 𝑦̂𝑖,myo = −0.454 + 0.430𝑥𝑖 ,
   𝑦̂𝑖,thr = −0.960 − 0.067𝑥𝑖 , and 𝑦̂𝑖,her2 = +0.130 + 0.681𝑥𝑖 ) and computed each construct’s bead-cor­
   rected residual 𝑟𝑖,𝑡 = 𝑦𝑖,𝑡 − 𝑦̂𝑖,𝑡 . A construct was called a statistically significant, bead-corrected on-
   target hit (is_binder, Supplementary Table S3) if it satisfied padj𝑖,𝑡 < 0.05, positive target enrichment
   over input (log2 FC ≥ 1.0 for volcano pre-hits), and a bead-corrected OLS residual 𝑟𝑖,𝑡 > 1.00 across all
   rounds (corresponding to ≥ 2-fold enrichment above expected bare-bead background). For 𝛼-thrombin,
   to prevent negative control intercepts or slopes from calling sequences that enriched on bare beads,
   Round 2 required bare-bead enrichment 𝑥𝑖 < 0.5 (yielding an empirical minimum observed residual
   among called hits of 𝑟𝑖,thr = +1.15), and Round 3 required any construct that was also a significant
   bare-bead pre-hit (padj𝑖,beads < 0.05, 𝑥𝑖 > 1.0) to exceed bare-bead enrichment by at least twofold
   directly (𝑦𝑖,thr − 𝑥𝑖 > 1.0; combined with the negative control intercept 𝛽0,thr = −0.960 and 𝑦𝑖,thr ≥

   0.05, 𝑦𝑖,𝑡 > 1.0, and direct fold-change difference Δ log2 FC𝑡− beads = 𝑦𝑖,𝑡 − 𝑥𝑖 > 1.0, which fixes
   𝛽1,𝑡 = 1) yielded 98.5% binary hit-call agreement in Round 2 𝛼-thrombin (Jaccard = 0.907), 94.4% in
   Round 2 myoglobin, 98.0% in Round 3 𝛼-thrombin (Jaccard = 0.903), and 97.0% in Round 3 HER2

Single-clone fluorescence magnetic bead-binding assay and preliminary 𝐾𝐷 estimation
To validate target binding, 𝛼-thrombin aptamer candidates were tested as previously described with minor
modifications [35]. Briefly, 𝛼-thrombin was immobilized on Dynabeads M-280 Streptavidin and refolded
5′-6-carboxyfluorescein (FAM) aptamers were incubated against the thrombin-coated beads across a
logarithmic concentration series (0, 1, 10, 100, and 1,000 nM) for 1 h at room temperature with gentle
agitation. Unbound oligonucleotides were removed by magnetic separation and single endpoint fluores­
cence was carried out using a plate reader. Preliminary apparent equilibrium dissociation constants (𝐾𝐷 )
and fitted amplitude spans (Δ𝐵max ) were estimated computationally as previously described [35]. Fitted
𝐾𝐷 values should be considered preliminary estimates, as fluorescence signals did not reach an empirical
saturation plateau (𝐵max ) within the 0–1,000 nM concentration range tested.

Supplementary Note 1 | Counter-screen discovery and continuation of HER2-selective binders
across Rounds 2–3
To evaluate target specificity and cross-reactivity across an expanded protein panel, human HER2 extracellular domain (ECD;
subdomains I–IV, ∼ 70 kDa) was introduced as an off-target reference in the Round 2 multiplexed counter-screen (𝑁 = 968) and
continued in Round 3 (𝑁 = 1,405) alongside 𝛼-thrombin, myoglobin, and bare streptavidin beads (Supplementary Fig. S3A).
While most G-quadruplexes in Round 2 behaved as non-specific matrix or polyanionic cross-binders, all-by-all profiling surfaced 13
strictly HER2-selective candidates (1.3% of the pool; Supplementary Fig. S3B), including a failed myoglobin candidate (bef5bce6520a,
★) carrying a 6-bp antiparallel Watson–Crick stem (5'-GAGCGG-3' paired with 5'-CCGCTC-3') clamping a compact G-quadruplex core
(5'-AA-GAGCGG-GGTGGGTTTTCTTTGGGTTGGG-CCGCTC-AAAA-3') that showed no enrichment on myoglobin or bare beads (≤ −0.46 log2 FC) but

enriched selectively on HER2 (+1.66 log2 FC raw, +1.19 log2 FC bead-corrected, FDR = 6.7 × 10−5 ; Supplementary Fig. S3C).
