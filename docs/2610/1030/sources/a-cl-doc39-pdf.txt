 1                                             INTRODUCTION
 2          Plaintiffs ignore what Anthropic actually tells subscribers about its platform, Claude, in a failed
 3   effort to accuse Anthropic of deception. Claude is a versatile, industry-leading large language model
 4   capable of interpreting and responding to a wide range of inputs, including analyzing and summarizing
 5   text, writing and debugging code, and reasoning through complex problems. Usage of Claude—
 6   providing a question or prompt and obtaining a response—requires computing resources. Anthropic
 7   offers subscriptions to Claude at several tiers of cost and usage (or capacity) limits: a free version, an
 8   individual Pro plan, and premium Max plans (Max 5x and Max 20x). As Plaintiffs’ Consolidated
 9   Complaint confirms, Anthropic makes clear that its Max 5x and Max 20x plans provide 5 or 20 times
10   more usage “per session” than the Pro plan, as opposed to the separate “weekly usage limits.” And for
11   each of these plans, Anthropic discloses that, while it will maintain the 5 and 20 times “per session”
12   usage multipliers, it may increase or decrease the total amount of usage available.
13          Plaintiffs’ false-advertising theory ignores Anthropic’s actual statements altogether and strips
14   them of their explanation and context. Plaintiffs assert that Anthropic promised subscribers “5 times
15   more usage . . . [or] 20 times more usage than Pro on a monthly (or at least weekly) basis” without any
16   “capacity constraints.” But Anthropic never promised a fixed total amount of usage or capacity.
17   Instead, all that Anthropic promised is that Max subscribers would receive 5 or 20 times proportionally
18   more per-session usage “than Pro” subscribers, while disclosing that Anthropic may alter or limit the
19   total amount of usage. Plaintiffs never allege (nor could they) that they actually received less than 5 or
20   20 times the amount of per-session usage that a Pro user received. And Plaintiffs concede that
21   Anthropic’s Consumer Terms (“Terms”), purchase flow, and support pages reiterate that Anthropic can
22   alter the total amount of usage. Accordingly, Plaintiffs’ own pleadings preclude their theory of
23   deception.
24          Plaintiffs assert six California-law claims—under the CLRA, FAL, and UCL, and for negligent
25   misrepresentation, breach of contract, and breach of the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing.
26   Each of these claims fails.
27          First, no reasonable consumer would be misled by clear statements distinguishing per-session
28   limits from weekly limits, particularly where the clarifying materials (reproduced in the Complaint)

11   dealing claim identifies no contractual provision that was frustrated. And Plaintiffs’ request for
12   restitution fails because they do not plausibly allege that legal remedies are inadequate.

15          A.      Anthropic’s Subscription Plans for Claude
16          Anthropic, PBC is a Delaware public benefit corporation headquartered in San Francisco,
17   California. Dkt. 37 (“Compl.”) ¶ 20. In March 2023, Anthropic launched Claude, a state-of-the-art AI
18   assistant and industry-leading large language model. Id. ¶¶ 2, 23. Subscribers can prompt Claude to
19   “conduct research, draft and edit text,” and “generate computer code, among other tasks.” Id. ¶ 2.
20   Anthropic offers Claude subscriptions at different levels of usage, including a free version, individual
21   plans, and commercial—team and enterprise—plans. Id. ¶¶ 3, 24.
22          At issue here are the paid individual plans, which are sold in three tiers “that have increasing
23   usage allowances”: Pro, Max 5x, and Max 20x. Id. ¶ 26. A monthly subscription to Pro costs $17 to
24   $20 per month, while Max 5x and 20x cost $100 and $200 per month, respectively. Id. ¶¶ 4–5, 26.
25   Anthropic began offering individual Pro subscriptions in September 2023 and announced individual
26   Max subscriptions in April 2025. Id. ¶ 23.

11           To survive a motion to dismiss under Rule 12(b)(6), “a complaint must contain sufficient factual
12   matter, accepted as true, to ‘state a claim to relief that is plausible on its face.’” Ashcroft v. Iqbal, 556
13   U.S. 662, 678 (2009) (quoting Bell Atl. Corp. v. Twombly, 550 U.S. 544, 570 (2007)). “Threadbare
14   recitals of the elements of a cause of action, supported by mere conclusory statements, do not suffice.”
15   Id. Instead, Plaintiffs bear the burden of pleading facts that “allow[] the court to draw the reasonable
16   inference that the defendant is liable for the misconduct alleged.” Id. at 678.
17           Under Rule 12(b)(1), Plaintiffs bear the burden of establishing Article III standing to pursue
18   injunctive relief. Kokkonen v. Guardian Life Ins. Co. of Am., 511 U.S. 375, 377 (1994). To establish
19   Article III standing and survive a Rule 12(b)(1) challenge, Plaintiffs must show a concrete and
20   particularized injury in fact that is caused by the defendant and likely to be redressed by a favorable
21   decision. TransUnion LLC v. Ramirez, 594 U.S. 413, 423 (2021).

18          A.      California Law Bars Tort Claims that Duplicate Contract Claims Seeking Identical

19

20
     as the breach of contract claim, precisely what California’s economic loss rule prohibits. The law is
21
     clear: “[T]here is no recovery in tort for negligently inflicted purely economic losses” where the claim
22
     arises from—or is not independent of—the parties’ underlying contract. Sheen v. Wells Fargo Bank,
23
     N.A., 12 Cal. 5th 905, 922–23 (2022). The rule “prevents the law of contract and the law of tort from
24
     dissolving one into the other” and limits a plaintiff to trying to “recover in contract for purely economic
25
     loss due to disappointed expectations, unless he can demonstrate harm above and beyond a broken
26
     contractual promise.” Robinson Helicopter Co. v. Dana Corp., 34 Cal. 4th 979, 988 (2004).
27
            Here, Plaintiffs’ alleged damages consist entirely of overpayment for a subscription that
28
     allegedly delivered less than what was “advertised,” Compl. ¶¶ 93, 169–170—the same disappointed-

 1   expectations injury they allege in their breach of contract claim, id. ¶¶ 175–176. They do not allege
 2   personal injury, property damage, or other harm independent of the subscription payments they made.
 3   Those payments are purely economic losses purportedly flowing directly from Anthropic’s alleged

13   Id. at *1. The court dismissed his implied-covenant and unjust enrichment claims under California law
14   because he received every benefit his membership promised, nothing in the agreement obligated the
15   gym to exclude lower-tier members from accessing certain amenities, and the agreement reserved the
16   gym’s right to make reasonable changes to what it offered. Id. at *2–4. The same is true here.
17          Plaintiffs’ dilution theory is also untethered from their own allegations: Plaintiffs measure their
18   claimed injury in weekly hours of usage, Compl. ¶¶ 73–82, and plead no facts suggesting that the price
19   of a Max plan reflected a promise of feature exclusivity rather than more “usage,” id. ¶¶ 25–27, 35–36.
20   Finally, the rule Plaintiffs propose—that a company owes refunds or credits to its paying subscribers
21   whenever it improves its free tier—would discourage any improvements to lower-priced tiers.
22   Accordingly, Plaintiffs’ implied covenant claim should be dismissed.
23   VIII. Plaintiffs Fail to Plausibly Allege Entitlement to Equitable Relief (Counts II and V)
24          Plaintiffs’ demand for equitable restitution in connection with their FAL and UCL claims is
25   barred by Sonner v. Premier Nutrition Corp., 971 F.3d 834, 845 (9th Cir. 2020). Because remedies
26   under the UCL are “equitable in nature,” a plaintiff “must establish that she lacks an adequate remedy
27   at law before securing” equitable relief. Id. at 839 n.2, 844; see also Phan v. Sargento Foods, Inc., 2021

 3          Here, Plaintiffs make no attempt to allege that the damages they seek in connection with their
 4   other claims are inadequate. They offer only the boilerplate allegation that they “have no adequate
 5   remedy at law,” Compl. ¶ 193, which is nothing more than a conclusory legal conclusion, see Iqbal,

 7   Cal. June 9, 2026) (dismissing FAL and UCL claims where plaintiff failed to plausibly allege
 8   inadequate remedy at law). Their restitution request fails.
 9   IX.    Dismissal Should Be with Prejudice
10          Leave to amend should be denied where “no amendment would allow the complaint to withstand
11   dismissal as a matter of law.” Kroessler v. CVS Health Corp., 977 F.3d 803, 815 (9th Cir. 2020).
12   Plaintiffs have already amended once and further revised their allegations when they filed their
13   Consolidated Complaint following Anthropic’s submission of motions to dismiss the amended
14   complaints. No further amendment would save their claims from dismissal.
15          The defects identified above are not pleading oversights that further factual allegations could
16   cure. Plaintiffs cannot rewrite what Anthropic said: Graphic H states that the Max plans provide 5 or
17   20 times “more usage per session,” and separately that the plans “also have two weekly usage limits.”
18   Compl. ¶ 61. Because Plaintiffs reproduced that language in their own pleading, no amendment can
19   unsay it, and no amendment can make a per-session statement into a weekly-hours guarantee. Where,
20   as here, the Complaint’s allegations fail as a matter of law, dismissal with prejudice is appropriate. See
21   Brown, 172 F.4th at 1120 (“[N]o amendment could save Brown’s complaint [because] reasonable
22   consumers have not been misled.” (cleaned up)).

24          For the foregoing reasons, the Court should dismiss all of Plaintiffs’ claims with prejudice.
