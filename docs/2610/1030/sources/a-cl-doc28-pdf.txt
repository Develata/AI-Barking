1                                               INTRODUCTION

2            Plaintiff’s complaint ignores what Anthropic actually tells its subscribers.          Anthropic’s

3    platform, Claude, is a versatile, industry-leading large language model capable of interpreting and

4    responding to a wide range of inputs—including analyzing and summarizing text, writing and

5    debugging code, and reasoning through complex problems. Usage of Claude—providing a question or

6    prompt and obtaining a response—requires computing resources. Anthropic offers subscriptions to

7    Claude at several tiers of cost and usage (or capacity) limits: a free version, an individual Pro plan, and

8    premium Max plans (Max 5x and Max 20x). Contrary to Plaintiff’s assertions, Anthropic explains how

9    much Claude usage subscribers receive at each plan level. Anthropic tells subscribers that Claude’s

10   Max 5x and Max 20x plans provide 5 or 20 times more usage per session than Pro—not 5 or 20 times

11   more cumulative hours per week—and explains that a session is a five-hour window, not a week.

12   Anthropic also discloses that Max plans have separate limits on a subscriber’s weekly usage, which

13   operate independently of the per-session limits. Plaintiff’s own First Amended Complaint (“FAC”)

14   confirms Anthropic’s distinction between the per-session usage and weekly usage limits. Indeed, the

15   FAC reproduces Anthropic’s disclosures expressly distinguishing per-session usage from weekly usage

16   limits, identifies explanatory materials available during the purchase process, and acknowledges that

17   Anthropic emailed subscribers plan-specific weekly-limit expectations.

18           Plaintiff cannot plausibly allege a false-advertising theory given Anthropic’s statements. A

19   plaintiff must challenge the defendant’s actual statements, not alternative versions stripped of

20   explanation and context. Here, Plaintiff’s claim of deception requires the Court to disregard the “per

21   session” qualifier, treat two separately disclosed metrics as one, and conclude that Anthropic promised

22   something it never said. In fact, Plaintiff’s own experience with Claude shows that he was not misled

23   by Anthropic or dissatisfied with the usage he purchased: Although he admits that he was able to

24   “budget[] and monitor[] his usage,” he never alleges that he canceled his subscription, and after being

25   a Max 5x subscriber for nearly four months, he decided to upgrade to Max 20x, rather than exercising

26   his right to cancel.

27           Plaintiff asserts five claims against Anthropic, each under California law: violations of the

28   Consumers Legal Remedies Act, False Advertising Law, and Unfair Competition Law; negligent

1    misrepresentation; and breach of contract. These claims fail on several independent grounds.

2           First, a reasonable consumer would not be misled by unambiguous and transparent statements

9           Second, Plaintiff’s fraud-based claims do not satisfy Rule 9(b)’s heightened pleading

10   requirement because he does not (and cannot) identify with particularity the specific statements he saw

11   and relied upon.

12          Third, Plaintiff lacks standing to seek injunctive relief because he faces no plausible threat of

13   future injury. Plaintiff is a long-time and ongoing subscriber who understands how Claude works and

16   occurred in California—yet his only allegation is that Anthropic maintains a San Francisco

17   headquarters. This Court has already held insufficient precisely this kind of conclusory assertion.

3           A.     Anthropic’s Claude Product and Subscription Plans

4           Anthropic PBC is a Delaware public benefit corporation headquartered in San Francisco,

5    California. Dkt. 20 (“FAC”) ¶ 16. In March 2023, Anthropic launched Claude, a state-of-the-art AI

6    assistant and industry-leading large language model. Id. ¶¶ 2, 17. Subscribers can prompt Claude to

7    “answer research questions, draft or edit text, and write code, among other things.” Id. ¶ 2. Anthropic

8    offers Claude subscriptions at different levels of usage, including a free version, individual plans, and

9    commercial—team and enterprise—plans. Id. ¶¶ 3, 18.

10          At issue here are the paid individual plans, which are sold in three tiers “that have increasing

11   usage allowances”: Pro, Max 5x, and Max 20x. Id. ¶ 21. A monthly subscription to Pro costs $17 to

12   $20 per month, while Max 5x and 20x cost $100 and $200 per month, respectively. Id. ¶¶ 4, 21.

13   Anthropic began offering individual Pro subscriptions in September 2023 and announced individual

14   Max subscriptions in April 2025. Id. ¶ 17.

15          Anthropic offers subscriptions to Claude on its website and through its desktop application. Id.

16   ¶ 19. A person interested in subscribing must first create an account, and before doing so must check

17   a box stating: “I agree to Anthropic’s Consumer Terms and Acceptable Use Policy and confirm that I

18   am at least 18 years of age.” Id. ¶ 22. The Consumer Terms are hyperlinked at that point in the purchase

19   process, and again on the payment screen, where the person must check a second box acknowledging

20   that “Anthropic will charge your card in the amount above now and on a recurring monthly basis until

21   you cancel in accordance with our terms. You can cancel at any time in your account settings.” Id.

22   ¶¶ 28–29. Only subscribers who have paid for Pro and Max 5x or Max 20x subscriptions may access

23   tools like Claude Code and Claude Cowork. Id. ¶ 18.

3                                              LEGAL STANDARD

4            To survive a motion to dismiss under Rule 12(b)(6), “a complaint must contain sufficient factual

5    matter, accepted as true, to ‘state a claim to relief that is plausible on its face.’” Ashcroft v. Iqbal, 556

6    U.S. 662, 678 (2009) (quoting Bell Atl. Corp. v. Twombly, 550 U.S. 544, 570 (2007)). “Threadbare

7    recitals of the elements of a cause of action, supported by mere conclusory statements, do not suffice.”

8    Id. Instead, Plaintiff bears the burden of pleading facts that “allow[] the court to draw the reasonable

9    inference that the defendant is liable for the misconduct alleged.” Id. at 663.

10           Rule 9(b)’s heightened pleading standard applies where a claim is grounded in fraud, regardless

11   of the claim’s label and even if fraud is not an element. Kearns v. Ford Motor Co., 567 F.3d 1120,

12   1125 (9th Cir. 2009). Rule 9(b) requires Plaintiff to plead “the particular circumstances surrounding”

13   the challenged “representations,” id. at 1126, including the “who, what, when, where, and how” of the

14   alleged misconduct, Cafasso v. Gen. Dynamics C4 Sys., Inc., 637 F.3d 1047, 1055 (9th Cir. 2011).

15           Under Rule 12(b)(1), the applicable standard for an argument that Plaintiff lacks Article III

16   standing to pursue injunctive relief, Plaintiff bears the burden of establishing federal

17   jurisdiction. Kokkonen v. Guardian Life Ins. Co. of Am., 511 U.S. 375, 377 (1994). In a facial

18   jurisdictional attack, the defendant accepts the allegations as true and asserts that the allegations are

19   insufficient on their face to invoke federal jurisdiction. Safe Air for Everyone v. Meyer, 373 F.3d 1035,

20   1039 (9th Cir. 2004). To establish Article III standing and survive a Rule 12(b)(1) challenge, Plaintiff

21   must demonstrate a concrete and particularized injury in fact that is caused by the defendant and likely

22   to be redressed by a favorable decision. TransUnion LLC v. Ramirez, 594 U.S. 413, 423 (2021).

26           A.     California Law Bars Tort Claims that Duplicate Contract Claims Seeking Identical

27

26          Lastly, Plaintiff’s demand for equitable restitution in connection with his FAL and UCL claims

27   is barred by Sonner v. Premier Nutrition Corp., 971 F.3d 834, 845 (9th Cir. 2020). Because remedies

28   under the UCL are “equitable in nature,” a plaintiff “must establish that she lacks an adequate remedy

2    2021 WL 2224260, at *5 (N.D. Cal. June 2, 2021) (no monetary equitable relief where “[p]laintiffs

10   X.      Dismissal Should Be with Prejudice

11           Leave to amend should be denied where “no amendment would allow the complaint to

12   withstand dismissal as a matter of law.” Kroessler v. CVS Health Corp., 977 F.3d 803, 815 (9th Cir.

13   2020). Plaintiff has already amended once, and no further amendment would save his claims from

14   dismissal.

15           The defects identified above are not pleading oversights that further factual allegations could

16   cure. Plaintiff cannot rewrite what Anthropic said: Graphic H states that the Max plans provide 5 or

17   20 times “more usage per session,” and separately that the plans “also have two weekly usage limits.”

18   FAC ¶ 43. Because Plaintiff reproduced that language in his own pleading, no amendment can unsay

19   it, and no amendment can make a per-session statement into a weekly-hours guarantee. Where, as here,

20   the FAC’s allegations fail as a matter of law, dismissal with prejudice is appropriate. See Brown, 172

21   F.4th at 1120 (“[N]o amendment could save Brown’s complaint [because] reasonable consumers have

22   not been misled.” (cleaned up)).

24           For the foregoing reasons, the Court should dismiss all of Plaintiff’s claims with prejudice.
