Restoring digital independence

The investigational Telepathy product aims to enable people living with paralysis to control their computers using only their thoughts.

How it works

The Neuralink listens to the part of the brain that controls the hand.

Participants learn to control a cursor through simply imagining moving their hand.

These imagined motions are then calibrated into computer mouse actions.

Through their mind-controlled mouse, participants may be able to fully operate their computer.

From messaging friends and family to watching videos, writing code, 3D modeling, creating art, and gaming, participants use Neuralink to potentially restore digital independence.

Frequently asked questions

Study basics

What is the purpose of Neuralink's clinical trials?

Neuralink is conducting investigational clinical trials to evaluate the safety of the N1 Implant and how well people can use the system to control devices like a computer just by thinking.

What is a brain-computer interface?

It is a system that reads activity from your brain and turns it into commands for a computer. The N1 Implant is a brain-computer interface that reads brain activity through fine threads, each thinner than a human hair, with thousands of sensors. In Neuralink's clinical trials for Telepathy, the threads are placed the part of the brain that controls movement, called the motor cortex. When you think about moving, the implant reads those brain signals and turns them into actions on the screen. This lets you control a device without moving your body.

What can the device do, and will it help me move again?

The N1 Implant lets you control a computer just by thinking about moving. You can move a cursor, click, and type on a screen keyboard. With it you can do things like browse the web, send messages, and use everyday apps.

The N1 Implant is not a cure for paralysis. It will not bring back movement, feeling, or speech, and it does not work your muscles or nerves. Instead, it gives you a new way to act on your thoughts and use digital tools more on your own.

Is the device approved by the FDA?

No. The N1 Implant is an investigational device. This means the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has not approved it for general use yet. It can only be used in this study, under an FDA rule called an Investigational Device Exemption (IDE). The study follows strict safety rules known as Good Clinical Practice. A group called an Institutional Review Board reviews and approves the study.

Has anyone used a Neuralink implant before?

Yes, several people living with ALS, brain stem stroke, and spinal cord injury.

Product

What level of control does Telepathy provide?

Currently, Neuralink participants are generally able to navigate a cursor and execute three clicks (left, right, and middle), mimicking a computer mouse.

Performance of the cursor may vary across participants with some participants achieving performance comparable to able-bodied control.

While some participants test experimental features such as keyboard, game console, wheelchair, and robotic arm control, these features are not currently available for all participants.

Neuralink participant testing their cursor control.

How long does it take to learn how to use Telepathy?

The learning curve of Telepathy varies. Some participants are able to gain fluent control on their first day while others may take a few weeks. During this onboarding phase, the Neuralink team is closely involved to support the participant as they learn to use the device. Additionally, maintaining cursor control may require up to a few hours of calibration per week.

What devices am I able to control with Telepathy?

The Neuralink implant works with a MacBook laptop that will be provided to participants. You will also have the ability to control other devices via the MacBook, such as a phone, Windows computer, or gaming console.

Is any part of the device visible?

No. The Neuralink implant sits in the skull and the electrode threads are inside the brain. There are no wires, no connectors, and no external battery pack. Once the incision heals and your hair grows back, nothing is visible.

The implant talks to your computer wirelessly and charges wirelessly through the scalp.

How do I charge my implant?

The Neuralink is charged wirelessly by placing the charger near the area of the implant. For convenience, participants are provided headwear (such as a hat) that houses a pouch to store the charger.

The Neuralink implant can be used for approximately four hours on a full charge. The device can be used continuously while charging.

Eligibility

Who may be eligible?

We are looking for individuals who:

Have little or no use of both hands and arms due to:

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), or

Spinal cord injury, or

Brain stem stroke

Are at least 18 years old

Have a consistent and reliable caregiver

Can I participate if I use a ventilator?

Maybe. Using a ventilator does not rule you out, but there are some safety rules. If you use a ventilator through a tube in your neck (a tracheostomy), it usually needs to have been in place for at least 3 months. If you use one another way, your settings need to be steady for at least 1 month before you join. You may not be able to join if you are likely to need a breathing tube in the next year but do not plan to get one (or a feeding tube) if you need it. A study doctor will look at your own situation.

Can I participate if I already use assistive technology?

Yes. Many people who join already use tools like eye-tracking, switches, or voice control. Using them now does not stop you from joining. In fact, the study looks at how well you do tasks with the N1 System compared with the tools you already use. So your experience helps the research.

Surgery & safety

What does the surgery involve?

A trained brain surgeon places the N1 device while you are asleep under anesthesia. First, they make a small opening in the skull. This is much like a surgery doctors already use, called deep brain stimulation. Then Neuralink's R1 robot places the implant's very thin, soft threads into the surface of the motor cortex. The implant fits into the opening in the skull, and the cut is closed. It sits under your scalp, with nothing cosmetically visible. It works wirelessly and charges through the skin.

What are the risks?

Like any brain surgery and implant, there are risks. The surgery can cause bleeding, infection, seizures, brain swelling, a bad reaction to anesthesia, or trouble healing. The device could stop working, move out of place, or cause swelling where it sits. The battery could also have problems.

Using the system may make you tired. It may not respond the same way each time while you learn how to use it. There are also privacy and security risks. Taking the device out later has its own risks. The study team watches participants closely the whole time. A separate safety group, called the Data and Safety Monitoring Board, checks the safety data often. As with any investigational device, some risks may not be known yet.

How long is recovery?

Most participants stay in the hospital at least one night so the team can watch them closely. You usually go home about one day after surgery. The first part of healing takes about 10 days. During this time, the team checks how your cut is healing and how you feel. They turn the device on only after they are sure you have healed enough.

Participation

How many visits are required?

First you have a screening visit and some checks before surgery. Then you have the implant surgery. Plan to stay near the study site until your first follow-up visit, about 10 days later, so the team can make sure your cut is healing well. After that, you come in for visits at about 45 days, 90 days, 6 months, 1 year, and 2 years. Check-ins in between can be done from home, by phone or video. The study offers flexible scheduling and help at home. In all, you take part for about 24 months.

What does participation cost?

There is no cost to participate. The study device, surgery, and study-related visits are provided at no charge, and travel and lodging for visits to the study site are supported.

Can I leave the study?

Yes. Taking part is your choice. You can leave at any time, for any reason. There is no penalty, and it will not change your regular medical care. You can stop using the device, have it turned off, say no to certain steps, or leave the study for good. If you stop using the device, the team will ask you to keep coming to safety visits when you can. You can also ask to have the device turned off or taken out.

Privacy

What data is collected?

The study collects brain signal data from the implant. It also collects health information, such as your medical history, details from your surgery and recovery, tests of how your brain and thinking are doing, how well the device works and how you use it, and surveys about how you are doing day to day. The data is kept in a secure system. It uses a code number instead of your name.

Who can access my information?

Your medical information is kept private. It is not shared with people who are not allowed to see it. The coded study data can only be seen by Neuralink and its team, the study site staff, the safety group (the Data and Safety Monitoring Board), and, when required, agencies like the FDA. The data is handled under privacy laws, including HIPAA.

Will my identity be public?

No. Your identity is protected. Study records use code numbers, and who you are is kept private. Study results may be shared for science, for regulators, or to teach others. But this follows your consent and does not name you in public.
