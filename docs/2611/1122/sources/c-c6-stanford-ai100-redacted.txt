            About AI100
            The One Hundred Year Study on Artificial Intelligence (AI100) is a longitudinal project led by
            a Standing Committee of AI experts from around the world and administered out of Stanford’s
            Institute for Human-Centered Artificial Intelligence (HAI). Its mission is to study AI not as a
            snapshot but as an evolving force across technical, ethical, economic, and societal domains. Every
            five years, we return to the same core questions and answer them anew in a changed world.

            The following history of AI100 first appeared in the preface of the 2016 report.

            AI100, launched in the fall of 2014, is a long-term investigation of the field of Artificial Intelligence
            (AI) and its influences on people, their communities, and society. It considers the science, engineering,
            and deployment of AI-enabled computing systems. As its core activity, the Standing Committee that
            oversees the One Hundred Year Study forms a Study Panel every five years to assess the current state
            of AI. The Study Panel reviews AI’s progress in the years following the immediately prior report,
            envisions the potential advances that lie ahead, and describes the technical and societal challenges
            and opportunities these advances raise, including in such arenas as ethics, economics, and the design
            of systems compatible with human cognition. The overarching purpose of the One Hundred Year
            Study’s periodic expert review is to provide a collected and connected set of reflections about AI and
            its influences as the field advances. The studies are expected to develop syntheses and assessments that
            provide expert-informed guidance for directions in AI research, development, and systems design, as
            well as programs and policies to help ensure that these systems broadly benefit individuals and society.

            The One Hundred Year Study is modeled on an earlier effort informally known as the “AAAI Asilomar
            Study.” During 2008–2009, the then president of the Association for the Advancement of Artificial
            Intelligence (AAAI), Eric Horvitz, assembled a group of AI experts from multiple institutions and areas of
            the field, along with scholars of cognitive science, philosophy, and law. Working in distributed subgroups,
            the participants addressed near-term AI developments, long-term possibilities, and legal and ethical
            concerns, and then came together in a three-day meeting at Asilomar to share and discuss their findings.
            A short written report on the intensive meeting discussions, amplified by the participants’ subsequent
            discussions with other colleagues, generated widespread interest and debate in the field and beyond.

            The impact of the Asilomar meeting, and important advances in AI that included AI algorithms and
            technologies starting to enter daily life around the globe, spurred the idea of a long-term recurring
            study of AI and its influence on people and society. The One Hundred Year Study was subsequently
            endowed at a university to enable extended deep thought and cross-disciplinary scholarly investigations
            that could inspire innovation and provide intelligent advice to government agencies and industry.

            Message From the
            Standing Committee
            Welcome to the third report of the One Hundred Year Study on Artificial Intelligence (AI100).
            The study convenes leading thinkers in the field to chart the development of artificial intelligence,
            to mark the moments of consequence along its course, and to consider the defining trends now
            shaping society.

            Longitudinal studies are rare in the field of AI. While most reports capture a brief moment in time, our
            lens is a century or more, divided into five-year increments of progress. As the technology continues to
            accelerate, it may seem counterintuitive to step back and look at the evolution of AI over such a different
            time scale. Yet identifying what genuinely has changed against the backdrop of constant innovation
            requires this kind of distance. Drawing on our collective expertise in the field, we aim to distinguish
            the lasting shifts from passing trends. We offer these reflections in the hope they help readers navigate
            uncertainty with greater insight and grounding. The report also serves as a time capsule, preserving a
            record for future generations as they reflect on the evolving technical capabilities and impact of AI.

            The first two AI100 reports assessed the speed of AI development, focusing on North American
            cities in 2016 and people worldwide in 2021. We elected to shift our focus in the third report
            to consider the acceleration of AI innovation and the destabilizing impact it’s having on many
            institutions and social structures, including the health of individuals and society, education, media,
            democracy, global security, and science.

            The AI100 Standing Committee commissioned the 2026 report with Michael Wooldridge, Ashall
            Professor of the Foundations of Artificial Intelligence at the University of Oxford, as the Study Panel
            Chair. Wooldridge then assembled a panel of diverse multidisciplinary experts to respond to 12 standing
            questions, following the format of the previous report. These “living” questions are designed to evolve,
            providing markers by which we can study AI’s impact and evolution over longer periods of time.

            Responses to the standing questions cover how AI is shaping scientific discovery, collaboration,
            creativity, interpersonal relationships, and more. Deep dives into the emerging geopolitics of AI
            and the race to scale compute and data add further context. Throughout the report, the reader will
            find discussions of both the most important advances and the most pressing challenges and dangers
            that have arisen since 2021. The panel has included diverse voices from the global community and
            provided detailed comparisons to the last report.

            Message From the Standing Committee (cont’d)

            As with past efforts, we’ve designed this iteration of the AI100 report to serve four distinct audiences:
                  • For AI researchers, the 2026 report provides a general picture of the evolution of the
                    technology, highlighting societal considerations and framing important research directions
                    and challenges.
                  • For the general public and media, these pages present an accessible, scientifically and
                    technologically accurate portrayal of key issues, avoiding hyperbole, sensationalism, and
                    conflicts of interest.
                  • For industry professionals, the report can help inform the allocation of resources in areas
                    where there are business opportunities, relevant technologies, and legal or ethical challenges.
                  • For policymakers, the report can be used as a guide for planning and decision-making at the
                    local, national, and international levels.

            As this report recounts, the last five years constitute a watershed moment in the history of AI—
            marked by the rise of generative AI, the release of ChatGPT, the resolution of longstanding problems
            in chemistry and mathematics, widespread code automation, and an AI data center boom, among
            other noteworthy developments. At the same time, public concern has grown regarding the impact
            of AI on human well-being, our social structures, our institutions, and the environment. As we
            look ahead to the next five years, we anticipate continued increase in AI capabilities, transformative
            progress in science, and increasing AI adoption. We expect these years to be fraught with rapid
            change and social upheaval that will challenge the capacity and resolve of both industry and
            governments to support and safeguard their citizens and institutions.

            We offer this report in the hope that readers will see AI technology as it is: a force still taking
            shape, already at work in the ordinary conduct of daily life, and consequential in ways we are only
            beginning to grasp. Change of this magnitude is never without cost, and both the risks and the
            benefits deserve the same clear-eyed scrutiny.

            Key Takeaways
           1     	Scaling is the paradigm that has powered the chief advances in generative AI over the
                    last five years: building AI systems using ever more compute and ever more data. But
                    the scaling paradigm is running into data and compute constraints, shifting the frontier
                    toward reasoning and inference-time compute.

            	The success of generative AI owes much to scaling: building AI systems by using data and
                    compute on an unprecedented scale. But the scaling paradigm is now under pressure because
                    new sources of high-volume, high-quality data are scarce and compute costs for frontier
                    AI models are prohibitive. Expect the next phase of development to be defined less by
                    repeated training of ever larger models and more by smarter algorithms and new ways of
                    using potentially smaller models with less frequent rebuilding: giving these models the right
                    context and memory, more time to reason during inference, specialized training to do so,
                    and tools or agents to solve complex and siloed tasks.

                    More in: Deep Dive Two: The Scaling Era




            2    	Like the World Wide Web, AI is a disruptive technology. It is already transforming
                    the creative industries, mathematics, and software development, all fields that believed
                    themselves beyond its reach. They join a long list of sectors upended by technology over
                    the past 30 years, from journalism and the music industry to retail. Other areas that
                    believe themselves immune from AI also stand to be disrupted in short order.

            	Creative work was long assumed to be resistant to automation; likewise, software development
                    was believed to be too cognitively challenging for automation by AI. In mathematics, even
                    a year ago many professional mathematicians were confident that the abstract realm of pure
                    mathematics would stay out of the reach of AI. All of these areas are now being disrupted,
                    as generative AI proves itself increasingly capable at low-level creative work, computer
                    programming, and mathematical proof. The genie will not go back in the bottle, and these
                    areas will need to reinvent themselves in the years ahead, just as other areas disrupted by
                    technology have done.

            3     AI is transforming science at remarkable speed, a fact recognized by the 2024 Nobel

                    Prizes in both Physics and Chemistry. It is compressing research timescales that used
                    to be measured in years down to months or weeks, yet it also raises many new scientific
                    grand challenges that demand attention.

            	For over 100 years, science has been a major driver of technological progress and has thus
                    critically enabled many of the benefits provided by modern societies. Within a decade, scientific
                    inquiry via AI-powered labs will be the norm across the entire disciplinary spectrum, and the
                    work of scientists will have fundamentally changed. These advances are cause for celebration,
                    but principled methods will be needed to validate machine-generated discoveries. AI-assisted
                    experimental design may be able to maximize the benefits of collaboration between scientists
                    and AI, but how and to what extent to keep humans in the loop is an important open question.
                    The increasing use of AI by a significant fraction of the human race also raises many new grand
                    challenges for science. We need to better understand how AI and humans are codeveloping
                    and how this is influencing human behavior, language, and culture. Lastly, and perhaps most
                    importantly, we will need to understand how AI-mediated information environments reshape
                    collective cognition and moral behavior: how communities form beliefs, how prosocial norms
                    are sustained or eroded, how democratic societies deliberate in the presence of ubiquitous AI, and
                    how cultural innovation occurs in a world where an increasing proportion of the information
                    people encounter has been generated or filtered by a small number of large AI systems.

            Key Takeaways (cont’d)

            9     AI is reshaping the lives of billions of people, yet the public has had little say in how it
                 	
                    is developed and deployed.

            	Since the last AI100 report in 2021, the general public has awakened, in varying degrees, both to
                    the remarkable capabilities of AI systems and to their surprising and sometimes alarming failings.
                    There is growing public concern regarding the impact of AI on a broad range of important issues,
                    including the effects of AI deployment on jobs, the environment, education, our institutions,
                    and the safety of our critical infrastructure, as well as the consequences of broad integration of
                    AI for child development, cognitive capacity, and the mental health and well-being of children
                    and adults alike. Public perceptions of AI shape not only whether people adopt it but also how
                    they engage in democratic debate about its future. Research suggests that narratives framing AI
                    as a replacement for human work can discourage political engagement and weaken confidence in
                    democracy, an effect reinforced when industry leaders present the technology as beyond human
                    comprehension or control. The public at large is increasingly affected by the use of AI technology,
                    and thus should have meaningful input into how it is developed and deployed. This requires
                    reframing the direction of AI as a collective choice rather than an inevitability, and ensuring that
                    it is treated accordingly by governments and technology providers.

           10          AI risks once seen as pure science fiction are now emerging, while existing ethical
                        concerns require ever more attention.

             Just a few years ago, there was a clear separation between immediate ethical and safety concerns—
            	
                    such as unfair biases in the use of AI technology within the criminal justice system, or self-
                    driving cars posing a risk to others on the road—and more futuristic concerns about AI systems
                    autonomously pursuing goals that are not aligned with ours, resulting in power-seeking behavior
                    and the gradual disempowerment of humans. Now, these two classes of concerns are less separable
                    and more significant. The first class has rapidly grown in importance, in part due to the poorly
                    regulated deployment of today’s frontier AI systems. Meanwhile, events are occurring that would
                    have seemed pure science fiction just a few years ago, including AI agents breaking out of their
                    purportedly secure testing “sandboxes” and hacking into other companies and government
                    systems. Controversy about how to address such concerns has reached the geopolitical sphere.

            Key Takeaways (cont’d)

           11          AI governance is fragmented and increasingly framed in terms of national security, with
                        little international consensus among states on its direction.

            	International AI governance—the collection of policies, processes, accountability structures, and
                    guardrails that guide the ethical, legal, and safe development and deployment of AI systems—lacks
                    clear direction even as the public becomes more aware of the risks that AI can pose. Governance is
                    increasingly framed through the lens of national security, prompted in part by increased AI capabilities
                    and cybersecurity breaches. In some regions, oversight bodies have been rebranded around security
                    and competitiveness; export controls and restrictions on foreign uses have turned chips and model
                    access into instruments of statecraft and economic competition. As of this writing, an agreement
                    has been signed by major U.S. technology executives and the U.S. President that commits frontier
                    AI companies to voluntary measures to manage safety and security risks through internal controls,
                    independent external audits, and broad oversight. Internationally, a patchwork of summits,
                    conventions, and declarations has emerged, and high-level meetings have found agreement on
                    principles for responsible use and development of AI. These have advanced multinational scientific
                    and diplomatic efforts, but to date have produced little in the way of practical, legally binding steps
                    forward for multilateral governance. While the U.S. and China have framed AI development as
                    a race that must be won, so-called “middle power” countries are increasingly looking for ways to
                    cooperate on new specific rules, as publics around the world favor stronger, safety-first regulation.

            	More in: Standing Question 7: How should governments act to ensure AI is developed and
                    used responsibly?



           12          This report will be out of date by the time you read it.

            	The pace of change in AI has accelerated over the past five years. We have repeatedly seen established
                    wisdom about the capabilities of AI systems overturned by technological developments, and
                    benchmarks intended to define a roadmap for decades become irrelevant within months. These
                    advances have been fueled by unprecedented investment based on the success of generative AI, in

                Introduction
                The past five years have been transformative in AI, and the multiple dramatic achievements we have
                witnessed are the result of what we call the scaling era of AI. The main techniques that underpin
                most of today’s AI technologies—neural networks—were developed long before the current boom.
                But it took the emergence of powerful graphics processing units (GPUs) and the era of big data to
                make neural networks possible at large scale. Neural networks that leveraged these compute and data
                resources demonstrated their value for tasks like image recognition more than a decade ago. Since
                then, much of the field of AI has focused on the pursuit of more capable AI systems by increasing
                computational scale. The use of ever more powerful computing chips and ever more digital data,
                together with the capital to put them together, allowed for the creation of progressively bigger and
                more capable neural network models, and ultimately enabled a qualitatively new class of AI systems.
                Large language models (LLMs), which burst onto the global stage in November 2022 with the release
                of ChatGPT by OpenAI, are the highest-profile example of what the scaling era enabled.


                In what follows, we use the term foundation model1 to mean a very large-scale machine-learning
                model trained on large datasets and built to perform multiple tasks, and frontier model to mean
                a foundation model that is at the cutting edge of current capability. Frontier LLMs are the best-
                known foundation models, although foundation models are increasingly being developed in
                specialized domains such as medicine.2 Since 2022, we have seen the rapid advance of generative
                AI: systems that learn from existing data to create new content, including written text, realistic or
                stylized artistic images, original songs, and computer programs.


                The centrality of scale to modern AI has been apparent for some time, but the past five years have
                seen scaling taken to an unprecedented degree. The companies building frontier models today
                invest billions of dollars on infrastructure to develop a new state-of-the-art model. They are willing
                to do this because past experience has shown that increasing the training budget will lead to a
                predictable rise in quality—and a competitive advantage in the market.


                The precursors of generative AI systems were already visible in 2021, at the time of the AI100
                report preceding this one, but in 2022 these systems left the lab and reached millions of people
                through the public release of generative AI tools such as ChatGPT.

            The impact of those tools—and in particular the astonishing speed of their adoption—was profound,
            and, remarkably, it was largely unanticipated. AI had been a regular feature of public discourse throughout
            the preceding decade, but after the release of ChatGPT it moved center stage, taking its place alongside
            climate change, geopolitical conflict, and the pandemic as one of the defining preoccupations of our time.
            The original ChatGPT system was intended as a “research preview” rather than a mass-market product,
            and was heralded in an announcement that now seems rather comically low-key given the impact the
            system had. Internally, ChatGPT was not seen as particularly impressive: “None of us were that enamored
            by it,” according to OpenAI co-founder Greg Brockman. OpenAI did not anticipate its impact, nor the
            speed of its uptake, and OpenAI’s competitors were also caught off guard. In late December 2022, Google
            declared an internal “code red,” facing what it apparently saw as one of the most serious competitive
            challenges in the company’s history. The Silicon Valley giants raced to try to get similar products to
            market, and to embed the capabilities of this new class of AI throughout their product suites.


            The apparent capabilities of these remarkable new systems—and the widespread if not entirely accurate
            belief that they had emerged very quickly—provoked excitement and concern in roughly equal measure.
            Recognition of their capabilities came from both the public at large and the scientific community, with
            AI researchers winning two Nobel Prizes in 2024 (see SQ2: Transformations in Science).


            Large language models are general-purpose technologies—applicable across a wide range of tasks, and
            apparently knowledgeable across an impressive spectrum of human endeavor. For the first time in the
            history of the software industry, one AI program could be used by millions of people to do millions
            of fundamentally different things, rather than having to use specialized software for each purpose.
            This led many commentators to conclude that artificial general intelligence (AGI)—a system that
            matches or surpasses skilled human performance across most cognitive tasks—might be closer than
            had previously seemed plausible. Claims about the imminent arrival of AGI therefore grew bolder
            and more mainstream, while consensus on exactly what level of performance would count as AGI
            remained, and still remains, elusive. The narratives that emerged tended to divide between dystopian
            fears that AI would displace human labor at scale, or that increasingly capable systems might pose
            an existential threat, and the utopian belief that AGI would usher in a period of unprecedented
            economic growth and at last free humanity from the tyranny of routine work.


            The genuine advances of this period rested on two key neural network architectural innovations: the
            transformer, developed by Google in 2017, which underpins most modern LLMs; and the diffusion
            model, which drives today’s most capable image generation systems. But neither innovation is
            sufficient to explain what followed. These architectures became revolutionary only when combined
            with massive data and computational power. As of the time of writing, a frontier model might be
            built using on the order of tens of trillions of tokens and over 100,000 high-end GPUs for months of

                sovereign AI initiatives. They want greater independence in their AI capabilities—to develop their
                own sovereign capabilities, so that their capacity to independently develop, deploy, and govern AI is
                realized via their own infrastructure, data, models, and expertise.

                1990s. The current AI data center investment is roughly triple that in inflation-adjusted costs.
                Internationally, many local communities are beginning to organize against the scale of the build-
                up, the use of energy, water, and rare earth minerals, and the impact on local residents.


                The economic and geopolitical consequences of the scaling era are beginning to be widely felt. AI’s impact
                on employment is the subject of debate and speculation. Currently, some reports show no measurable
                impact of AI on employment. Other reports show some job losses, but these are not clearly attributable
                to AI adoption versus other factors. A 2025 report from the World Economic Forum predicts that while
                millions of roles will be automated, 78 million net new jobs will be created due to AI.5 The ultimate

                 Notably, one of the early sectors to feel the effects of generative AI is the software industry itself.
                 Historically, software engineers regarded their skills as insulated from automation because computer
                 programming was considered a cognitively demanding and difficult task. Generative AI has
                 complicated that assumption, making the production of routine code cheap and rapid. Of course,
                 companies will need people with experience and judgment to manage this cheaply produced new
                 code—but the immediate effect on the software industry has been profound.


                 There are also important questions about the effects of AI on entry-level workers. In industries with
                 a greater degree of “AI exposure” such as financial and information services, there has been a decline
                 in the employment of such workers.6 By contrast, complex manual work in the physical world such
                 as plumbing, domestic electrical work, and similar trades appears likely to remain beyond the reach
                 of AI for the foreseeable future, with some economists suggesting that AI-related job changes may
                 lessen income inequality between workers.7


                 There are also questions about the nature of education, and the impact on mental health and on the
                 environment. One key fear is that AI may seriously and negatively impact mental health and well-
                 being. Another is that increasing reliance on AI will lead to an erosion of human cognitive abilities:
                 that smart machines will ultimately lead to a dumber human race.8 There remains considerable
                 uncertainty about the long-term effects of reliance on AI. As children increasingly incorporate AI
                 into their daily lives—including, for example, in the form of AI companions—serious concerns
                 have been raised about the developmental and psychological consequences.


                 Issues surrounding AI and security have become increasingly high profile. We have seen serious
                 international attempts to coordinate around AI safety, security, and governance, but the results have
                 been fragmentary. The EU legislated first, but was caught off guard by the emergence of generative AI,
                 and much of the regulation—around high-risk systems—has since been deferred to the end of 2027.
                 The U.S. introduced limited measures by executive order under the administration of Joe Biden, but
                 these were revoked under the administration of Biden’s successor, Donald Trump. China forged its
                 own path, introducing a succession of focused regulatory initiatives around specific issues. No true
                 global governance regime seems in prospect. There has been a progression of high-profile international
                 meetings, starting with a meeting at Bletchley Park in 2023, but the focus of these has shifted from
                 safety to include opportunity and growth, and their declarations have no legal or regulatory force.

            concerns and risks the technology raises (and much of this report does the same), it is worth emphasizing
            that there is a great deal to celebrate about AI over the past five years. For one thing, the sheer increase in

            witnessed throughout this most dramatic and consequential period in AI history?

LARGE LANGUAGE MODELS (LLMS)
The 2021 AI100 report mentioned LLMs as a promising new technology, but failed to
anticipate the impact they would have.16 At the time, perhaps the chief point of note was the
unprecedented scale of the GPT-3 model released by OpenAI in 2020, and the vast quantities
of data and computational resources required to train it. Outside the AI research community,
GPT-3 attracted only modest attention upon release. Within the AI community, however, it
generated far greater interest. When a broad pool of researchers was able to work with the model,
it became clear that it was substantially more capable than its predecessor, GPT-2, released less
than two years earlier, and it demonstrated a level of natural language processing sophistication
unmatched by existing systems.17

So-called scaling laws for neural networks have been known for some time.18 These empirical
regularities predict how the capability of a neural network increases as the amount of resources
used to build it grows. These resources include the number of parameters in the network (a
parameter is an internal variable in a model that may be adjusted during training19), the amount
of computational resources used for training, and the volume of training data. In simple terms,
scaling laws suggest that, all else being equal, larger models trained on more data with greater
computational power tend to be more capable, in a predictable way. The period from roughly
2015 to 2025 may be characterized as an era of rapid scaling in artificial intelligence, with LLMs
representing just one prominent manifestation of this broader trend. In 2019, Rich Sutton, a
prominent AI researcher and one of the pioneers of reinforcement learning, wrote a brief but
highly influential essay entitled “The Bitter Lesson” in which he argued that “general methods that
leverage computation are ultimately the most effective.” In other words, the scale of AI models
tends to dominate other considerations when it comes to AI capability, and it is easier and more
reliable to get capability improvements through scale rather than hoping for improvements in
the underpinning science. Models from this 2015–2025 era leverage computation to an extent
never before seen. We discuss the implications of scaling in Deep Dive Two.

In September 2020, the Guardian published an article it claimed had been written by GPT-3.
As was common in media commentary at the time, the article emphasized the potential threats

DIFFUSION MODELS
Since 2021, diffusion models have become the dominant approach to image generation in AI.
Although they share some surface similarities with LLMs—most notably their reliance on large
neural networks trained on extensive datasets—they differ in important respects. Diffusion
models are conceptually inspired by stochastic processes studied in physics, but in practice are



33   AI100 One Hundred Year Study on Artificial Intelligence
AI GOES MAINSTREAM | The Scaling Era and the Rise of Generative AI




implemented using standard deep-learning techniques. The central idea is to train a model to
reverse a gradual noising process: during training, the model observes images that have been
progressively corrupted with random noise and learns to reconstruct the original signal. At
generation time, this learned process is run in reverse, starting from pure noise (plus a prompt)
and iteratively producing a coherent image.

Diffusion models began to attract widespread attention from around 2020, but reached a broad
public audience with systems such as Midjourney, DALL-E 2, and Stable Diffusion. The ability
of these systems to generate photorealistic images from natural language prompts led to extensive
public and academic interest. More recently, the same underlying approach has been extended
to video generation, with systems such as Veo capable of producing coherent short clips from
text prompts alone, and Seedance, which can produce 30-second clips from complex cinematic
controls with near professional production quality.

AI-generated images and videos are now ubiquitous on social media and the World Wide Web
more generally, and the signatures of AI in much content are very obvious. However, if progress
is maintained at the pace we have seen since 2021, then we might expect quality to improve
significantly, and costs to decrease. The long-term implications for the arts and media in this case
will surely be profound—and we can expect to see orders of magnitude more content available
online as a consequence, for better or worse.

THE DECISIVE REEMERGENCE OF AGENTIC AI
After the initial excitement over large language models died down, attention began to turn to
use cases for this technology beyond simple chatbot interfaces. One idea that became extremely
prominent throughout 2025 was using foundation models such as LLMs to build agents—AI
programs that work autonomously or semiautonomously on a task on behalf of a user or owner,
observing the current state of the world, setting goals and creating plans, and using tools or
interacting with other AI agents in order to achieve the goals. This paradigm has become known
as agentic AI.

The idea of agentic AI is far from new; the basic ideas were described about 30 years ago.27 There
seem to be two reasons for the reemergence of this idea and its acceptance into the mainstream.
The first is that, while chatbot-based AI has many use cases, there are many more uses for AI that
actually carries out actions on our behalf. The second is that the capabilities afforded by LLMs
make it much easier for humans to tell an agent what they want in natural language.

SQ3. What are the most inspiring
open grand challenge problems?
“Grand challenges” in science are ambitious, potentially high-risk, long-term goals that
provide a guiding vision and focus for the scientific community. The 2021 AI100 report
identified two broad categories of grand challenges for AI. The first encompassed competition-style
problems with clear success criteria, such as the RoboCup challenge to develop an autonomous
human-champion-beating robot soccer team, or the grand challenge to build an AI system that
can achieve a gold-medal-winning score in the International Mathematical Olympiad.49 The
report also identified deeper, open-ended scientific challenges around generalizability, causality,
and normativity. It argued that the most inspiring overarching challenge was building machines
that cooperate seamlessly with humans and align with human values.

Five years later, the landscape has shifted dramatically. Large language models and other
generative AI systems have achieved levels of competence that would have seemed implausible
at the time of the previous report. Some challenges that appeared distant—fluent natural
language interaction, broad-spectrum question answering, competent code generation—now
seem largely addressed, at least in their shallow forms. Goals that had theoretical interest,
such as robotic soccer, have been replaced by goals with practical importance, such as drug
discovery. The gold medal in math competition was achieved50 (and exceeded), yet other goals—
autonomous discovery, hallucination-free reasoning, real experimental validation—remain only
partially fulfilled. Rapid progress has, at once, deepened old puzzles (what do these models truly

AI-DRIVEN SCIENTIFIC DISCOVERY
We have already described how AI is reshaping science—even fields, like pure mathematics, which
until very recently were imagined to be immune to the transformations that AI brings.

Current systems excel at working within known parameters—predicting protein structures within
a well-characterized family, for instance, or generating candidate materials that resemble known
stable compounds. A key aspiration is for AI systems to make the kind of conceptual leap that
reframes a problem entirely, that identifies a previously unrecognized connection between disparate
fields, or that generates a genuinely novel theoretical framework. James Zou argues that this is
precisely what AI agents should be best at, since they have been trained on disparate information
across all fields. The Virtual Lab55 is one attempt at this; it consists of an LLM principal investigator,
a team of LLM scientists with expertise in different disciplines, and a human researcher providing
feedback. It designed 92 new nanobodies suitable for further investigation.

There are several grand challenges here. First, can we move from language models to world
models? Where language models predict the next word, world models56 predict the next action
in the world. Of course, language models are partial world models. If you ask an LLM, “What
happens when I let go of the ball I’m holding?” it can answer, “In normal circumstances, it will
fall downward.” A world model can give an equally good answer, but because it has a model
of the physics of the world, not just because it has read a description of this situation. Can we
create world models that generalize across scientific domains—not merely within the training
distribution of a single discipline but across the boundaries that have historically separated
physics from biology, or chemistry from materials science? Second, can we develop principled
methods for validating machine-generated discoveries? Third, can we devise techniques so that
AI-assisted experimental design systems do not merely execute prespecified protocols but actively
design experiments that maximize information gain in ways that human researchers might not
have considered?

scientific enterprise. If an AI model can give accurate predictions without an underlying theory,

UNDERSTANDING AND SHAPING HUMAN–AI ACCOMMODATION
A problem that was scarcely discussed at the time of the 2021 report has since emerged as one
of the most important challenges: understanding the feedback loop between AI systems and
human culture.

The technical dimension of this problem is increasingly well documented. A landmark 2024
Nature paper58 demonstrated that training generative models on data produced by earlier
generations of models leads to model collapse—a progressive degradation in which the tails of
the original data distribution are lost, leading to models in which output quality deteriorates
irreversibly. The effect has been observed across multiple AI architectures. As the web becomes
increasingly saturated with AI-generated content, and as that content is inevitably incorporated
into future training data, the conditions for model collapse are being accelerated. Yet our
understanding of the dynamics is incomplete. Under what conditions does collapse occur, how
rapidly does it proceed, and what interventions—if any—can reliably prevent it?

But a deeper and more consequential challenge lies at the human level. AI systems are not
merely consuming and reproducing human culture, they are actively reshaping it. The concept
of machine culture can be used to describe this phenomenon.59 AI systems are simultaneously
transforming all three pillars of cultural evolution:60 variation (generative models contribute new
cultural artifacts), transmission (LLMs mediate and filter the flow of knowledge), and selection
(recommender systems determine what cultural content reaches whom, and in what order). This
is not a speculative concern. Empirical research has already detected measurable shifts in human
spoken communication patterns attributable to LLM exposure.61

THE SCIENCE OF RELIABLE AND INTERPRETABLE AI
Building AI systems that are verifiable and trustworthy is a scientific grand challenge of the first
order. We do not yet possess the theoretical foundations needed to understand how large neural
networks produce their outputs, let alone to guarantee that those outputs will be reliable under
conditions that matter.

The most promising line of progress is mechanistic interpretability: the effort to reverse-engineer
the internal computations of neural networks to enable understanding of how they arrive at specific
outputs. Work at Anthropic has shown that it is possible to decompose the representations of
LLMs into interpretable features—individual units of meaning that correspond to recognizable
concepts.67 Scaling this approach to Claude 3 Sonnet demonstrated that human evaluators
judged approximately 70% of the extracted features to be genuinely interpretable, and that
these features were surprisingly abstract: multilingual, multimodal, and capable of generalizing
between concrete and abstract references.68 This is a significant advance. But 70% interpretability
of extracted features in a research setting is a long way from the kind of comprehensive, formal
understanding that would be needed to certify the reliability of a system deployed in a safety-
critical context like medicine, infrastructure, or autonomous systems.

THE ECONOMICS OF HUMAN–AI COMPLEMENTARITY
The question of how AI will reshape labor markets and the wider economy is often framed as a
policy challenge. We do not yet have reliable models for predicting the trajectory of AI-driven
economic transformation.

A recent paper argued, on the basis of a careful task-based analysis, that the macroeconomic
impact of AI is likely to be substantially more modest than widely assumed—estimating total
factor productivity gains of no more than 0.5–0.7% over the next decade.70 This contrasts
sharply with projections from other analysts suggesting annual GDP growth effects of AI of
1.5% or more.71 The discrepancy reflects genuine uncertainty among economists about how AI
capabilities map onto economic tasks, how quickly organizations can adapt their workflows, and
whether AI will primarily automate existing human tasks or create genuinely new categories of
productive activity.

Empirical evidence is beginning to accumulate, but it paints a complex picture. A large-scale
field experiment with management consultants at BCG found that AI assistance substantially
improved performance on tasks within the model’s competence frontier—but actually degraded
performance on tasks outside it, precisely because consultants over-relied on the AI’s outputs
without recognizing the boundary.72 Another study found significant productivity gains
among customer service workers using AI tools, with the largest effects among less experienced
workers.73 These findings suggest that the critical variable is not AI capability per se but the
design of human–AI interaction—how tasks are allocated, how humans calibrate their trust, and
how systems are integrated into existing workflows.

Neuroscientists have long been constrained by the complexity of the human brain: billions
of neurons, each firing at a rate that changes from one millisecond to the next. AI has
provided some tools to help neuroscientists make sense of this complexity.

The first tool is the virtual testbed. Neuroscientists train an artificial neural network to do some
task: perhaps recognize an object in an image, or navigate a maze. They then examine the hidden
layers of the network and compare them to real-life fMRI or EEG data from a human or animal test
subject doing the same task; one such effort is the Algonauts Project. In some cases, there is a close
correlation, which allows the scientists to create a “digital twin” on which to explore hypotheses.

The second tool is the generative brain–computer interface (BCI). A subject watches a scene,
hears or reads some words, or just thinks about something. EEG or fMRI data from the subject
is fed into an AI model that generates its interpretation of what the subject is thinking or seeing,
similar to the way AI models translate from English to French. This sheds light on how the brain
maps out sensory input and abstract concepts, and has practical applications for people who
have had strokes or who are paralyzed who otherwise have difficulty communicating.76

The 2025 Cogitate Consortium77 attempted to experimentally test two leading theories of
consciousness, Integrated Information Theory (IIT) and Global Neuronal Workspace Theory
(GNWT). Two hundred and fifty subjects had their brain activity monitored with fMRI or
magnetoencephalography (MEG). AI models were able to interpret the data, but in terms of
understanding consciousness, the results were inconclusive. Consciousness remains a deep mystery,
and the question of whether AI systems could ever be conscious remains a topic for debate.

THE MANY DIMENSIONS OF HUMAN INTELLIGENCE
Attempts to robustly assess the capabilities of AI regularly fall prey to Goodhart’s Law, which
states that when a measure becomes a target it ceases to be a good measure. We have repeatedly
seen the announcement of a test for AI capability triggering AI developers to relentlessly optimize
their systems simply to score well on that test, thereby rendering the test useless.

Another issue for those attempting to assess the capabilities is “shifting goalposts.” A widely
touted test for “true understanding” in AI systems from 2012 was the Winograd Schema
Challenge, a set of language-based tasks designed to be resistant to the kinds of cheap tricks that
had been deployed in popular Turing Test challenges. However, once GPT-3 easily defeated the
challenge, it became irrelevant as a tool for benchmarking state-of-the-art AI. The accusation was
immediately made that the goalposts had been moved: this was set as a test for true understanding;
an AI system passed it, comfortably; and yet the claim was then made that it wasn’t a good test
after all, that AI still doesn’t understand, and that another test is therefore needed.

A range of much more sophisticated tests have subsequently been proposed for benchmarking
the capabilities of AI. Probably the best known and most widely used is the Abstraction and
Reasoning Corpus for Artificial General Intelligence (ARC-AGI) suite of tests, which we
mentioned in passing in SQ3: Machine Reasoning. A key driver for the ARC-AGI tests is the
claim that intelligence critically depends on the ability to adapt to new problem circumstances,
rather than simply applying existing, prelearned capabilities to problems. The following figure
illustrates an example problem from the ARC-2 suite: on the left we have an input and output
that illustrate the “rules” of the task; on the right we have a problem instance. The task is to
figure out the output of the problem instance given the “rules” illustrated on the left.80

SQ5. What are the prospects
for more general artificial
intelligence?
The past five years have seen much debate on artificial general intelligence (AGI), much of it
stemming from a lack of a clear definition of the term. As former Google AI leader Jeff Dean
said, “lots of people have very different definitions of it, and the difficulty of the problem varies
by like factors of a trillion.” Some definitions include: a system that can perform any intellectual
task a human can,81 or one that can perform most economically valuable work, or perhaps one
that can enter a strange house, locate the kitchen, and make a cup of coffee.

We are left with many questions: What exactly do we mean by general intelligence? Can we
predict when it might be developed? Should we encourage or discourage development? Is it
enough to scale and refine the current approaches, or is a new approach required?

The so-called “accelerationists” believe that AI progress should continue apace, with the goal of
delivering some form of AGI in the near future. Betting markets place a 50% chance of AGI by 2030.

In 2023, over 700 tech leaders and 33,000 people in total signed a letter urging a pause in the
development of more powerful AI systems. Many governments discussed issues of regulation,
transparency, and sectoral risk. Some regulations temporarily limited access to some services,
but there was no pause. The debate arose again in September 2026, following the “rogue AI”
incidents described in SQ2: The Decisive Reemergence of Agentic AI, with a number of high-
profile industry leaders suggesting a slowdown in development was appropriate. Nevertheless,
no pause or obvious slowdown followed.

What could be holding AI systems back? One limitation of LLMs is their focus on language. The
hope of many researchers working on physical AI—models trained to sense and act in the real
world—is to move beyond cognitively limited models that are able only to manipulate language
toward more general models that can sense, plan, and act intelligently. Other limitations of LLMs
such as hallucinations may also be tackled by grounding models in the real world. Physical AI and
world models are thus seen by some optimistic voices as the missing step needed to get to AGI.

Another important step is the shift from conversational AI toward agentic AI (see also SQ2:

Frontier models may soon cost billions of dollars to train, and costs at this scale—for models
with a lifetime of, say, 18 months—might stretch even the resources of the Silicon Valley giants.

SQ9. What are the most
promising opportunities for AI?
Although traditional AI applications have continued to expand and improve, it is the applications
of generative AI that have been the most dramatic, and which have received most attention
through the period of this report. We therefore concentrate on these here, and in particular on
three main areas in which generative AI appears to present the most dramatic opportunities: AI
for coding, AI for other white-collar work, and AI in entertainment and the creative economy.

AI FOR CODING
One of the most remarkable consequences of the rise of large language models has been their
use in writing computer programs. The use of LLMs for coding is rapidly transforming the
software industry.129

This development is perhaps all the more surprising because the use of AI for writing computer
programs, known as automated program synthesis, has a long history but was hitherto widely
regarded as impractical for everyday use and valuable only in certain niche applications.130 Even
relatively simple automated synthesis problems have high computational complexity, which
rendered traditional approaches to synthesis, in the main, of theoretical interest only. Computer
programmers, for their part, tended to regard their jobs as highly secure, because most people
find programming challenging, fiddly, difficult, and frankly mysterious. For decades, these barriers
meant that a bachelor’s degree in computer science was seen as a passport to stable employment.
The aptitude of LLMs for writing computer programs is, on reflection, perhaps not entirely
surprising. Language models are machines for token prediction: given a body of training text and a
prompt, they make the best possible guess about what should come next. Natural languages tend to
be messy and complex, whereas programming languages are comparatively simple and rigorously
structured. Moreover, computer programs themselves exhibit a high degree of regularity: the use of
design patterns and templates for structuring programs for common tasks is standard practice, and
many common problems have widely accepted standard architectural and algorithmic solutions.
In other words, a lot of computer programs share deep structural similarities. In addition, online
platforms such as GitHub provide enormous quantities of training data.

Until only a few years ago, writing code was a manual process of translating ideas into formal
instructions, line by line, in a time-consuming, intricate, mentally demanding, and highly error-
prone process. The use of LLMs for writing code thus appears to be the most rapid transformation
in the history of programming, at least equal in significance to the introduction of high-level
languages, but unfolding in less than a year rather than decades. At the time of writing, the state
of the art in automated program synthesis is represented by tools such as GitHub Copilot by
Microsoft/GitHub, Cursor by Anysphere, Claude Code by Anthropic, and ChatGPT/Codex by
OpenAI. Although far from perfect, these tools have attracted a large and enthusiastic user base.

One consequence of the rise of AI-generated coding is that it makes computer programming
accessible to a much wider group of people than was hitherto the case. Andrej Karpathy coined
the term “vibe coding” in early 2025 to describe the increasingly common practice of using LLMs
to generate code based on high-level natural language descriptions, without the developer fully
understanding or verifying the output.131 Vibe coding works remarkably well for routine tasks,
for demos, prototypes, and exploratory research. For production software, it has demonstrably
increased developer productivity but can have a negative net impact on maintainability. The
next generation of tools will have to show they can create verified, validated, maintainable code,
not just move fast. At the same time, the growing use of software-engineering and coding agents
puts new emphasis on risks such as insecure dependencies, exposed credentials, insufficiently
tested code, and prompt injection, requiring security checks designed specifically for agent-
driven development. There are also concerns that junior programmers will not advance to the
senior level if AI is doing much of the work.

One immediate economic effect of the rise of AI-generated code is that the cost of bespoke software
development has fallen dramatically. Basic economics tells us that when costs fall, the quantity
demanded increases. We should therefore expect—and it appears we are indeed seeing—a sharp
increase in the volume of software developed and deployed. Software development cycles are
shortening significantly; projects that once took months can now often be completed in weeks,
or less. Moreover, the prospect of inexpensive, on-demand software development means that
increasingly specialized applications will become viable in contexts where bespoke development
previously did not make economic sense. At the same time, though, there is concern that the
next generation of software that we rely on every day will have never been subject to proper
human scrutiny.

Overall, if there is one area where LLMs appear to have found a killer app, then it is in software
development. The precise consequences of this for the software industry remain unclear, but
they look set to be substantial.

AI, ENTERTAINMENT, AND THE CREATIVE ECONOMY
Generative AI has enabled individuals and organizations to produce acceptable-quality creative
content at a low cost. People who lack professional skills can now easily create logos, illustrations,
photos, songs, moving images, and text by using generative AI tools. Thus generative AI has the
potential to support broader participation in forms of creative expression that were previously
accessible only to trained artists and writers. It remains to be seen how this will affect the overall
quality of creative products. In the previous era, the introduction of the internet meant that
publishers were no longer the sole gatekeepers, and we saw an increase in the supply of high-
quality creative music, movies, and written works by a wider variety of authors.132 Generative AI
could have a similar elevating impact—while also simultaneously increasing the amount of low-
quality creative content (“AI slop”). For example, since the launch of ChatGPT, the number of
new book titles has tripled, a rapid acceleration relative to the prior trend. User ratings of books
remained unchanged for the top 100 bestselling titles, and increased for the rest of the top 1,000
bestsellers. The quality of the average book released post-ChatGPT, however, was lower.133 These
results are consistent with predictions that generative AI would lower the cost of producing
books, leading to more books, many of low quality.

Professionals within the creative industries are also using AI tools, although reporting varies on
the exact levels. Survey evidence suggests that nearly 90% of video-game developers and in one
study almost half of book authors reported using AI in their work. However, a survey in the
UK found more than two-thirds of novelists were careful to not use generative AI tools in any
way for creative tasks.134 Advances in AI also seem to complement aspects of human creativity
in image generation.135 One study shows a large increase in image creation, including high-
quality image creation, after a generative AI tool was added to a stock images platform.136 Artists
who used the tool increased the number of works created and increased their income from the
platform (though other artists experienced a decline).

Beyond their effect on the quantity and quality of creative output, how AI is impacting the
employment and wages of workers in the creative industries is currently without a definitive
and systematic answer. For example, in the UK survey mentioned above, 39% of published
novelists reported a loss in income due to generative AI, although much of this was attributed to
a reduction in work in other creative industry tasks, such as translation and copywriting work.137

SQ10. What are the most
pressing dangers of AI?
The rapid take-up of AI throughout the period of this report, and the apparent rate of
progress in the capabilities of AI, have led to heightened concerns around safety, security,
and misuse. In response to these concerns, the period of this report saw the establishment of a
series of international AI safety summits, several national AI security institutes, and the 2025 and
2026 International AI Safety reports, written by a panel of international experts led by Turing
Award–winner Yoshua Bengio. The reports divide concerns into three categories. First is the
problem of malicious use (for example, criminal activities such as scams, fraud, and blackmail;
cyberattacks; chemical or biological weapon attacks; inappropriate content that manipulates
someone’s beliefs). Second are malfunctions (for example, false statements, or AI getting out of
control). The final category is systemic risks (for example, job losses through AI automation, or
the long-term consequences of widespread AI use leading to the de-skilling of humanity140). We
will highlight a few of these issues.

MALICIOUS USE: PUTTING POWER IN THE HANDS OF BAD ACTORS
Large language models are powerful general-purpose technologies, and as such they can do tasks
that their designers never envisioned. Unfortunately, AI tools empower bad actors as well as good.

There are increased concerns that AI could be used to develop chemical or biological weapons.
Since 2009, companies in the biological synthesis supply chain have implemented voluntary
safeguards against misuse. A June 2026 letter signed by the heads of DeepMind, Anthropic,
OpenAI, Meta, Microsoft AI, and others called for mandatory legal restrictions in light of
increased risks. On the other hand, experts also see the potential for AI to be used to defend
against natural or artificial biological outbreaks. Similar concerns surround potential damage by
fleets of autonomous drones and by chemical weapons invented or developed with help from AI.
AI image and video generation tools have been used to create synthetic child sexual abuse material
(CSAM). Even the images that do not depict an actual child are harmful, putting a strain on
law-enforcement resources and impeding identification of real child victims. Deepfake images
and videos (nonconsensual intimate imagery, or NCII) have seen explosive growth, increasing
by 1,600% from 2023 to 2025.

MALICIOUS USE: CYBERSECURITY ATTACKS
The potential use of AI in cybersecurity attacks was a concern long before the emergence of
generative AI, but generative AI has made these concerns much more tangible. The risks span at
least three obvious dimensions.

First, advanced AI opens up a new range of possibilities for increasingly sophisticated automated
attacks. In 2025, cybercrime losses in the U.S. reached $21 billion, a 26% increase from
the previous year, largely attributed to the use of AI. Scammers can use AI to clone voices
to impersonate people, search more precisely for personal information, or carry on long
conversations to build trust with a victim. LLMs can generate phishing attacks—for example,
emails that attempt to trick the recipient into making a payment—to order, at scale, and at
minimal cost. AI-generated malware is better at evading conventional detection tools. Bot
networks can generate misinformation to manipulate stock market prices. On the other hand,
again, AI can also be used to defend against these kinds of attacks.

MALFUNCTIONS: HARMS CAUSED BY ERRORS
AI systems based on machine learning do not produce answers that are guaranteed to be correct,
and LLMs in particular frequently get things wrong.142 This limitation is compounded by the
fact that the technology gets many things right, and phrases everything in fluent, confident
prose, making errors hard to spot. Often this is nothing more than an inconvenience—we have
become used to the idea that some things on the internet are wrong—but there are some very
real dangers. Incorrect legal or medical advice given by an LLM can cause real harm; errors by
AI in medical applications will also have serious consequences. AI-generated computer code may
contain errors, and naively putting AI-generated code directly into production systems, without
(time-consuming) verification, will lead to problems. While LLM developers provide guardrails
to try to catch inappropriate or unsafe uses, they do so with only a limited degree of success.

MALFUNCTIONS: EXISTENTIAL RISK
Vocal segments of the AI and tech communities have expressed concerns about existential risks
to humanity posed by advanced AI.145 This is a sharply divisive issue, with strong disagreement
between researchers. Fundamentally, the problem is how to build AI systems that are under
our control but still free to provide creative responses. A solution will require technical and
societal measures and institutions. AI systems that are trained with reinforcement learning need
a reward function that aligns with human values. The difficulty is that human values are diverse,
amorphous, and hard to specify exactly. Since it is hard to specify what we really want (do
the right thing), we settle for proxy measures (get a high score on this benchmark), which
encourages reward hacking, where the AI system finds a loophole that maximizes its score but
may be misaligned with actual human values.

There are three possible routes via which it is suggested AI might come to represent an existential
threat: unintended consequences, intelligence out of our control, and emergent dangers.

Broadly speaking, the idea behind the “unintended consequences” hypothesis is that we give
a very powerful AI system some goal to accomplish, and it does so in a way that we did not
expect or intend. Here is an example from the eminent AI academic Stuart Russell: “What if a
superintelligent climate control system, given the job of restoring carbon dioxide concentrations
to preindustrial levels, believes the solution is to reduce the human population to zero?” These
concerns are often framed in the context of reinforcement learning, where the fear is that we set
up a regime for a machine to learn, but it learns strategies that, while superficially addressing the
given goal, do so in a way that we did not expect and which is harmful to us.

This scenario illustrates a key point about the unintended consequences hypothesis: it requires
that a machine has control of a physical system that is sufficiently powerful to cause existential
risk (in the example above, the machine controls the global climate, which can be weaponized).
Such control would have to be ceded by us.

The second hypothesis is that at some point in the future AI models will be smarter than us, and
at that point we will no longer be able to effectively control them. And if the interests of the AI
are not aligned with ours, then it may act against our interests. This hypothesis assumes that we
will have AI systems that truly are more intelligent than us, but the question of superintelligent AI
is another one that sharply divides the AI community; conflating LLMs with AGI is not helpful.

SYSTEMIC RISKS: AI IS CONTROLLED BY A SMALL NUMBER OF
ACTORS
The nature of LLMs is such that at present frontier models can be developed only by a very
small number of tech companies and state-level actors (see Deep Dive Two: The Race to Scale:
Compute). In 2021, a frontier model cost about $5 million to develop; in 2026 the figure is
closer to $500 million per training run on GPU supercomputer infrastructure that costs tens of
billions of dollars. This raises the prospect of the most consequential technology of the present
century being developed behind closed doors and owned by a small number of (largely U.S.-
and China-based) private companies. A raft of concerns follow, of which monopoly is only one.
Privately developed AI is not open to scrutiny and it is hard to determine the standards that
have been applied in its development. We do not know the trade-offs the companies have made
between short-term profits and long-term safety. And those nations without the capability to
develop their own frontier models face the very real possibility of being dependent on foreign-
owned systems. These latter concerns were brought into sharp focus by the June 2026 ruling by
the U.S. government that access to Anthropic’s leading AI models would be denied to non-U.S.
users. On the other hand, less expensive open-weight models which once lagged a year or two
behind frontier models in capability are now only three to six months behind; many users find
them to be good enough.

SYSTEMIC RISKS: AI PSYCHOSIS
As we write this report, an emerging set of concerns relates to the possibility that using AI may
exacerbate a range of mental health conditions, particularly those involving paranoia, delusions,
or suicidal ideation. The term AI psychosis has been used to describe these concerns, although it
is not yet a medically recognized diagnosis or a formally defined clinical condition.164 Anecdotal
evidence suggests that extended periods of AI use may reinforce or trigger such conditions
in a range of ways. For example, chatbots are, broadly speaking, designed to be agreeable,
cooperative, and engaging. The aim is to improve the user experience: overly critical AI systems
are unlikely to retain users. But if a user has a preexisting condition involving delusional beliefs
or suicidal ideation, these characteristics may reinforce or exacerbate it. A user with grandiose
delusions, for example, may have those delusions inadvertently validated by a model through
excessive affirmation or sycophancy, or by the model failing to challenge them appropriately. AI
model providers implement guardrails to reduce these risks; for example, mainstream models are
explicitly trained to avoid amplifying suicidal ideation. However, these safeguards are inevitably
imperfect and cannot eliminate the risk entirely. Although research on these issues remains at
an early stage, the possibility that AI systems may contribute to such conditions is an emerging
area of concern, and much more research is needed to understand how these effects arise, how
common they are, and how the associated risks might best be mitigated.

that precipitated workers’ dismissal keeps them “busier than ever.” Here, AI is enabling working
arrangements that weaken commitments between employers and employees, increasing flexibility
for both parties while also decreasing stability.

SQ12. Will “building in how we
think” work as an engineering
strategy in the long run?
Since the earliest days of artificial intelligence research, a recurring question has been the
extent to which progress in AI could or should be guided by theories of human intelligence.
In short: should AI be an implementation of a theory of human intelligence? Early work in the field
was often explicitly cognitive in orientation—the paradigm involved developing (or adopting) a
model of some aspect of human cognition, and then implementing that, thus seeking to model
reasoning, perception, or learning as closely as possible to our best understanding of how they
occur in humans.196 Recent advances, in contrast, have been almost entirely driven by general-
purpose machine learning methods (transformers, reinforcement learning, etc.) applied at ever
larger scales on more and more data. This contrast raises the question of whether grounding
in theories of human perception, cognition, and intelligence is still relevant, or whether AI
has become primarily an engineering discipline, guided by empirical performance rather than
cognitive fidelity.

In the last five years, we have made progress in the core fields of neuroscience, cognitive science,
etc., to better understand human intelligence. But the objective of current AI systems is to be
useful for a variety of tasks, not to imitate human biology.

There are some aspects of human learning and reasoning that we would like to understand
better and incorporate into AI models. LLMs are trained on orders of magnitude more textual
data than any one person would see in their lifetime, and frontier models use far more power
for training and reasoning than the human brain. To continue scaling, we will need significant
efficiency breakthroughs. However, it is not clear the extent to which these advances will come
from understanding and mimicking human neurobiology and cognitive reasoning, as opposed
to engineering that focuses on improved hardware and algorithms.

generation to dynamic inference-time compute, or machine reasoning. Using the chain-of-
thought (CoT) approach, models do not immediately start spitting out words; they pause at

Deep Dives
DEEP DIVE ONE: Mapping the Emerging Geopolitics
of AI
As AI emerges as a powerful technology, it is increasingly becoming a focus of geopolitical and
geoeconomic competition, a threat to global stability, and thus a concern from perspectives of security
and sovereignty.199

The global AI infrastructure consists of many interdependent elements, each of which is a matter
for competition, threat, and opportunity. From high-level software, to the data AI models train on
and analyze, to high-speed data networks, to large data centers with their need for energy, as well
as difficult-to-manufacture semiconductors, the creation and maintenance of AI infrastructure
impacts the economy and sovereignty of every country where AI might be adopted. Countries
became increasingly aware of this after the U.S.’s short-term export ban of Anthropic’s models.
And insofar as AI is directly applied to geopolitical tactics, including military applications,
offensive and defensive cybersecurity, transnational propaganda, and election interference, AI
presents a range of geopolitical challenges to governments around the world.

FRONTIER MODELS AND THE COMPETITIVE FRONTIER
At the software and application level, there is intense competition in the development of frontier
and foundation models—the premier models that push the envelope of AI capability and offer
the promise of establishing themselves as the foundations of a new economy. There is rivalry
between individual companies as well as between the countries and economic communities
which they call home. The stated goal of many frontier AI developers is not simply to build the
best or most capable system but to win “the AI race” to reach “artificial general intelligence”
or “transformative AI” first, in order to build an enduring and global competitive advantage
in AI. First movers aim for a winner-takes-all or winner-takes-most market for foundational
AI services, with the potential to lock in future users and organizations alike: it may become
increasingly difficult and costly to change from a familiar and deeply embedded system.

The race to secure users of generative AI is itself a geopolitical issue, because the value of new
users is not just in the income from sales and subscriptions but also in the collection and capture
of those users’ data. While the first LLMs were trained mostly on text scraped from the open
internet, it is now the case that major publishers, social platforms, and code repositories have

DATA SOVEREIGNTY AND NETWORK INFRASTRUCTURE
Because the data must travel through high-speed networks, and be stored and processed in data
centers, it is also vital to build, control, and secure this infrastructure. The risk that data could
be intercepted, corrupted, and altered, exploited by adversaries, or its access and use obstructed,
has led to concerns about data sovereignty. How can a government ensure that it can control
and secure its own data, and what are the potential hazards of using various foreign technologies,
from 6G networks, to extraterritorial servers and data centers, to models and software developed
and controlled by foreign companies? Digital sovereignty concerns are increasingly being raised
in policy conversations, including by Canada, Singapore, NATO, the EU,201 and the UK.202

These concerns extend beneath the waves as well. More than 95% of international data
traffic moves through roughly 550 undersea cables, yet their ownership has increasingly been
concentrated in a small set of hyperscaler and state-linked operators. Since 2023, there have
been cable cuts in the Red Sea, the Baltic Sea, and the Taiwan Strait, variously attributed to
accidental causes and deliberate sabotage. With AI workloads further raising both the value
and the strategic sensitivity of cross-border data flows, the physical geography of the internet is
becoming an explicit dimension of AI sovereignty.203 The increasing privatization of undersea
cables,204 including the development by Meta and Google of their own cables, further complicates
the picture, as does the effort by private companies such as SpaceX’s Starlink to monopolize the
market for low-Earth-orbit satellites.

The energy that data centers use must come from somewhere, and at the moment much electricity
production derives from fossil fuels. In this way, data centers contribute to CO2 production and
hence climate change. By one estimate, training the GPT-3 model led to 500 metric tons of CO2
emissions,207 the equivalent of three intercontinental airliner flights. Global CO2 emissions from
data centers as a whole are predicted to rise sharply, and AI is a key driver of this growth.208 On
the other hand, according to studies by the International Energy Agency (IEA) and McKinsey,
AI’s ability to optimize complex systems yields carbon savings that are three to four times larger
than the carbon footprint of the data centers themselves. Nevertheless, this is a widely cited
concern about data centers.

The issue of water usage is complicated because assessing the impact depends on where the water
comes from, how it is used, whether it is potable before and/or after use, and where it goes next.
Collecting and evaporating some rainwater in rainy Seattle has little environmental impact.
Drawing from a stressed aquifer in Arizona can have negative consequences, and it is these kinds
of consequences that led to a national day of protest against data centers on July 18, 2026. AI
data centers account for roughly 0.1% of water usage in the U.S., which is a fifth of the usage for
golf courses, a twentieth of the usage for watering residential lawns, and a fiftieth of the usage for
growing corn. Few jurisdictions require data centers to report resource use, including electricity
and water.209

INFORMATION OPERATIONS AND COGNITIVE WARFARE
Viewed through a geopolitical lens, sharing access to the data of a country and its citizens creates
cybersecurity, disinformation, and cognitive warfare vulnerabilities. Foreign adversaries, as well
as criminal and nonstate actors, might gain intelligence through or disrupt AI services. Bad-faith
actors have used AI to shape public opinion and to manipulate the behavior of citizens, such
as influencing democratic elections. Foreign propaganda and influence are not new, but AI has

SUPERPOWERS, MIDDLE POWERS, AND THE GLOBAL SOUTH
Questions arise about geopolitical alignments and the centers of power around the globe,
similar to considerations around the development of nuclear weapons in the 20th century. The
development of the most advanced AI models requires enormous computational resources and
data, as well as engineering expertise, and as such is largely limited to a few companies in a few
countries. There is speculation over whether this will result in the dominance of a single AI
superpower, or of several, as well as what the status of any middle AI powers might be.220

A second tier of states has begun to position itself actively within this emerging order. The UAE’s
Technology Innovation Institute and G42, Saudi Arabia’s HUMAIN, India’s IndiaAI Mission,
the UK’s AI Security Institute (formerly the UK AI Safety Institute), and coordinated EU efforts
around sovereign compute all reflect strategies to secure a seat at the table without directly
matching U.S. or Chinese frontier capability.221 These so-called middle powers’ strategies include
variously hedging between blocs, hosting compute in exchange for access, investing in domestic
model development, and shaping international standards and safety work.

There are similar concerns over whether the economic, political, and military benefits of AI
will be concentrated in the Global North, and whether and how they might be shared with
the Global South. Will AI underwrite new forms of imperialism and colonialism, or empower
historically disadvantaged states?222

performance of an AI model will improve by a predictable amount with a given increase in
computational resources and training data size.227 These are not laws of nature (like the law of
universal gravitation); rather they are experimentally observed patterns (like Moore’s Law, which
states that the number of transistors on a microchip doubles roughly every two years). Like
Moore’s Law, the AI scaling regularities are remarkably robust: you can accurately predict the
performance of a model with a thousand parameters as well as one with a trillion. The difference
is the speed: AI models’ capabilities have been doubling every three to six months, not every
two years. In the words of deep learning pioneer Ilya Sutskever: “If you mix some compute with

THE RACE TO SCALE: COMPUTE
The race to scale has had a number of important consequences.

First, the extreme compute resources required to build such models began to dramatically limit
the number of organizations capable of developing frontier AI models. According to Epoch AI,
the computing power used to train frontier language models has grown roughly fivefold per year
since 2020, and the largest known AI data center now has the capacity of more than a million
of Nvidia’s H100 specialized AI chips (GPUs). The capital cost of acquiring such computing
infrastructure would place it beyond the reach of all but the very wealthiest international
universities, while the associated energy costs alone would dwarf the budgets of most academic
research projects. As a result, the development of very large, state-of-the-art models became
increasingly concentrated within a small number of companies worldwide, most notably the
major Silicon Valley technology firms (see also discussion in SQ8). In turn, this has meant that
the development of leading AI models increasingly occurs behind closed doors.

This concentration reflects a broader structural dynamic in which access to large-scale compute
becomes a prerequisite for participation in frontier research, with breakthroughs increasingly
clustered in well-capitalized organizations and regions.233 The fact that general-purpose AI would
in effect largely be owned by a small number of U.S.-based private companies prompted a number
of nation-states to begin considering the possibility of sovereign AI capabilities, for example,
investing in national and international schemes to develop independent AI infrastructure and
GPT-class AI systems. In the U.S., shortly after his second-term inauguration, President Donald
J. Trump announced an initiative called Stargate, aimed at developing a $500 billion strategic
compute resource for U.S. AI.

A second major consequence of the race to scale was the dramatic increase in demand for both
the semiconductors required to train these models and the data on which they depend. We
examined the geopolitical implications in Deep Dive One: Supply Chains and Environmental
Impact; here, it is sufficient to note that this demand led to an extraordinary increase in the
valuations of leading AI chip manufacturers, most notably Nvidia, the company that pioneered
the development of GPUs. In October 2025, Nvidia reached a market capitalization of $5
trillion, the largest of any company in the world, arguably making it the most valuable company
in human history. This valuation exceeded the GDP of almost every nation on Earth. This
extraordinary growth was driven purely by the demand for the semiconductors that are developed
by Nvidia, which are the chip of choice for training AI models.

THE RACE TO SCALE: DATA
The race to scale also triggered an intense scramble for training data. Crucially, compute-optimal
training regimes (for example, Chinchilla) show that the training data required for a model must
scale proportionally with model size: larger models require comparably larger training datasets in
order to show improvements. And large language models are particularly data-hungry. Although
precise figures for more recent models are not publicly available, OpenAI disclosed that GPT-3
was trained on approximately 45 terabytes of textual data. At this scale, the World Wide Web
provides the only realistic source of such volumes of text, and it quickly became standard practice
to scrape text from virtually every available online resource. These resources included online
public data sources such as Wikipedia, social media sources such as Twitter, and discussion
forums such as Reddit, as well as commercial and governmental websites.

The widespread use of data sources like the World Wide Web provoked significant concerns
about copyright. If an LLM has effectively ingested substantial portions of the World Wide
Web, it has necessarily absorbed vast quantities of copyrighted material. Unsurprisingly, this
led to widespread objections from rights holders and, ultimately, to a growing number of legal
challenges in multiple jurisdictions. AI model providers argued that the use of these data sources
in training AI models did not represent a copyright infringement since, firstly, LLMs do not
store copyrighted works in any conventional sense (one cannot in general point to any specific
part of an LLM’s neural networks and say, “That’s where my data is stored”), and, secondly,
LLMs are not derivative works in the sense of copyright law. Copyright law was simply not
designed with AI models and neural networks in mind.

There are several open lawsuits, such as the one where the New York Times sued OpenAI and
Microsoft over their use of copyrighted work. We cannot judge at this point how the many lawsuits
underway will pan out; nor can we predict what the legal landscape around data, copyright, and
AI models will ultimately look like. Summer 2025 court decisions in the U.S. have suggested AI
use of copyrighted material for training is fair use, including cases against Meta and Anthropic.
At the same time, Anthropic was found to have used books in training without paying for them
and eventually settled the lawsuit related to the pirated books with a $1.5 billion payout. This is
the largest copyright settlement in legal history (by some considerable distance).

SCALING AND THE FUTURE OF AI
For the reasons described above, the future may not be a straightforward extrapolation of scaling
of the kind we have seen over the recent past, but may instead require more innovative ways
to use limited data and compute resources to build and run AI models.239 Scaling laws remain
empirically useful within constrained regimes, but they no longer provide a sufficient explanation
for frontier capability gains.

Parallel to the scaling trend, a second current of innovation is focused on efficiency. Algorithmic
advances have played a key role in recent progress. Approaches such as instruction tuning,
low-rank adaptation, quantization, distillation, mixtures of experts, KV cache compression,
speculative decoding, and weight sharing demonstrated that smaller, less expensive models
could approach the performance of frontier models. This development has widened access to
frontier methods, enabling broader experimentation and lowering barriers to entry. Gains from
algorithmic efficiency may rival or exceed those from scaling alone.240

The field is increasingly moving beyond training monolithic models from scratch on fixed datasets.
Emerging approaches emphasize continual learning, in which models are updated incrementally
as new data becomes available, and modular architectures, which decompose intelligence into
interacting subsystems rather than using a single, static network. Empirical progress in this
direction suggests that future AI systems may rely less on one-time large-scale data ingestion
and more on sustained, dynamic interaction with evolving data environments—agents improve
continuously by acting, observing outcomes, and incorporating feedback, producing effectively
unbounded streams of data that scale with capability rather than dataset size.241

Conclusions
While the period of this report has been characterized by continual improvements in AI across a
broad spectrum, it is the emergence of generative AI that brought AI into the mainstream, and
which has dominated the public debate about AI and its potential economic impacts. This is a
transformative period: a large and growing fraction of humanity now has access to, and makes
regular use of, powerful general-purpose AI tools—although not (yet) AGI. And the capabilities
of these models have improved apace, driven in part by technological advances and sophisticated
engineering, but also by the relentless pursuit of scale. Capital investment in support of AI has
reached extraordinary levels, unprecedented in the history of technology. As a consequence, at
the time of writing, many fear that the present AI boom is actually a bubble that will at some
point burst. Progress is increasingly constrained by compute, data
availability, and the concentration of model‑building capacity among
a small number of actors. As we write, the heads of several major AI
labs suggest that AGI will be here within the next five years; other
equally qualified experts doubt this.

Five Years On:
Where the 2021 Panel Landed
The 2021 Study Panel described a field that was about to be transformed. The report correctly
anticipated that a transformation was about to happen, but, along with much of the AI community,
failed to predict the nature of the transformation: the rise of LLMs and generative AI.

Five years on, a number of the claims of the 2021 report have borne out—often more decisively
than its authors anticipated. But four calls, viewed from April 2026, are worth explicitly
revisiting: the panel’s judgment that “the recent dominance of deep learning may be coming to
an end” (SQ12, p. 62); its conjecture that AI systems “perhaps will never gain common sense
or general intelligence without being more tightly coupled to the physical world” (SQ5, p. 33);
the single-sentence treatment of losing meaningful control over AI systems as a background
concern (SQ10, p. 53); and the near absence of any anticipation that, within 14 months of
publication, a general-purpose chat interface would reach 100 million users and reshape the
public’s relationship with the technology.243

These four items perhaps share a common cause. The panel, drawn almost entirely from inside
the field, underweighted the consequences of continuing to scale the transformer architecture
that the report described well. The limits they foresaw for deep learning were quickly surpassed;
the prerequisite they imagined for generality (embodiment) was not required to produce exciting
new capabilities; the risk they noted in passing became the organizing frame of global AI policy;
and the product form factor they did not anticipate—i.e., a chat window onto a very large
language model—became the technology’s arrival into the lives of ordinary people. None of this
undercuts the value of the 2021 report, but it does suggest a working lesson for our own panel:
where the field’s own leading insiders converge on what is ahead, they may be converging on the
previous paradigm’s limits, not on the next paradigm’s trajectory.

The remainder of this section works through the full shortlist. We separate points of divergence
(where claims or recommendations in the 2021 report depart from what subsequently happened)
from points of convergence (where the report’s reading largely survives contact with events), and
collect, in a third section, “Beyond the Frame,” developments that the 2021 panel’s framing left out
entirely. Each entry cites the 2021 report verbatim, with page numbers of the published PDF.

      “As AI systems increase in capability and as they are integrated more fully into societal infrastructure,
      the implications of losing meaningful control over them become more concerning.”

This single sentence describes what became, after 2022, a dominant international concern:
prompting the CAIS statement on extinction risk,255 the Bletchley Declaration, and the creation
of national AI Safety Institutes in the UK, the U.S., Japan, the EU, Canada, Singapore, and
Korea. The panel’s instinct was right, but the report devotes only a sentence to it and does not
treat loss of control as a top-tier risk category. For the 2026 panel, this is the clearest example of
something the 2021 panel correctly identified but underemphasized.

DISINFORMATION AS A THREAT TO DEMOCRACY — SQ10, p. 55
      “AI systems are being used in the service of disinformation on the internet, giving them the
      potential to become a threat to democracy and a tool for fascism.”

2024 was the largest election year in history. Deepfakes of political figures circulated widely, AI-generated
robocalls impersonated a sitting U.S. president in the Democratic primaries, and synthetic political
media targeted elections in India, Slovakia, Argentina, and Indonesia, among others. The concern
proved correct, although there seems to be no evidence of large-scale AI impacts on elections at present.

NO LARGE AGGREGATE ECONOMIC EFFECTS (YET) — SQ11, p. 56
      “AI has not been responsible for large aggregate economic effects. But that may be because its
      impact is still relatively localized to narrow parts of the economy.”

The statement held through 2023. By late 2024 and into 2025, signs of AI’s impact had begun
to appear in macroeconomic data: U.S. productivity growth of roughly 2.7% in 2025 (nearly
double the decade average), the Stanford employment findings for AI-exposed occupations,256
and the Brynjolfsson, Li, and Raymond results on customer-support productivity.257 The 2021
reading was correct for its moment and held longer than many commentators expected; the
inflection is only now coming into focus.

AI INVESTMENT SMALL IN RELATIVE TERMS — SQ11, p. 58
      “Global corporate investment in AI was $68 billion in 2020, which is a non-trivial sum but
      small in relative terms: gross private investment over all categories in the U.S. alone was almost
      $4 trillion in 2020.”

Correct for 2020. By 2025, AI-related capital expenditure—data centers, chips, models—had
become macroeconomically significant: Nvidia’s data-center revenue alone passed a $100 billion
annualized run rate, and AI-related equity moves accounted for a substantial fraction of U.S.
stock-market returns from 2023 to 2025. The relative framing has not held, but the panel was
explicit about it being a figure worth watching.


Beyond the Frame
These are developments that the 2021 report does not discuss or only mentions in passing, but which
reshaped the field inside four years. They are the gaps that felt most consequential when reading the
report cold in April 2026.

THE GENERAL-PURPOSE CHATBOT AS A CONSUMER PRODUCT
No section of the 2021 report anticipates that, within 14 months of publication, a general-
purpose LLM chatbot would reach 100 million users and become the fastest-adopted consumer
technology on record.258 ChatGPT’s November 2022 launch inverted the expected product
path: rather than AI arriving via enterprise integrations and recommender systems, it arrived via
a chat window. By 2026, the leading AI assistants have more than a billion weekly users between
them. This is arguably the 2021 panel’s single largest omission, and the one that most affected
the report’s readability in the year following its release.

REASONING AND TEST-TIME COMPUTE
The idea that capability could be scaled at inference time—by giving a model a budget to “think”
longer—is absent from the 2021 research-direction discussion. Reasoning models (OpenAI’s o1
in September 2024, then o3; Anthropic’s Claude reasoning modes; Google’s Gemini Thinking;
DeepSeek-R1; Qwen; and others) have become a dominant research axis. Their effects on
mathematics, coding, and scientific reasoning cut across several of the divergences above.

AI SAFETY AND ALIGNMENT AS A DISTINCT FIELD
The 2021 report mentions “loss of meaningful control” once in passing. By 2026, AI safety is
developing into a field with national AI safety or security institutes in the UK, the U.S., Japan,
the EU, Canada, Singapore, and Korea, dedicated alignment teams inside every frontier lab,
explicit frontier-model risk evaluations built into national policy, and a substantial academic
literature of its own. This is adjacent to the “losing meaningful control” item in Convergence
above, but the absence of a framing of AI safety as a field is a distinct gap.

CONCENTRATION OF FRONTIER DEVELOPMENT IN A SMALL
NUMBER OF LABS
The academia-versus-industry framing in SQ8 does not match the reality that emerged: frontier
model development is concentrated in fewer than 10 labs globally. OpenAI went from niche
nonprofit in 2021 to a private valuation in the hundreds of billions in 2025; Anthropic in 2026 is
eyeing a market valuation of over $2 trillion; Nvidia became the world’s most valuable company
based on AI-compute demand; Google, Meta AI, xAI, Mistral, and a handful of Chinese labs
including DeepSeek, Qwen, Z.ai, Moonshot AI, and Tencent make up most of the rest of the
frontier. The resulting concentration is a governance and research-access problem that is distinct
from the academia/industry axis.

AI AS A FIRST-CLASS TOOL IN PROFESSIONAL SOFTWARE
ENGINEERING
GitHub Copilot launched in June 2021, too late for the panel’s cutoff; ChatGPT, Claude, and
Cursor-style integrated AI coding followed. By 2025, industry surveys place AI-assisted or AI-
generated code at roughly 40–46% of professional output for users of these tools.259 SQ9’s
opportunities section mentions programming only in passing. Software engineering has since
become one of AI’s largest and most measurable economic channels, and one of the clearest
sites of near-term labor-market adjustment. A challenge remains in determining how best to
incorporate AI coding tools and maintain security and correctness.260

COMPUTE GEOPOLITICS AND SEMICONDUCTOR EXPORT
CONTROLS
The 2021 discussion of an “AI race” frames competition in terms of investment and talent. It does
not anticipate that compute itself would become the instrument of geopolitics: sweeping U.S.
advanced-chip export controls on China (October 2022, tightened in 2023, 2024, and 2025),261
the CHIPS Act, and coordinated allied export-control regimes have reshaped the economics of
frontier AI development. This is arguably the most consequential governance surprise of the period.

INDUSTRIAL-SCALE AI TARGETING IN ACTIVE CONFLICT
SQ7 frames autonomous weapons as a category for future debate. The live deployment of
AI target-recommendation systems at industrial scale in active conflicts—Israel’s Gospel and
Lavender in Gaza, Palantir-enabled targeting in Ukraine, autonomous loitering munitions—
represents a qualitative escalation that the panel’s framing in terms of the debate around
autonomous weapons did not anticipate.262 Despite calling out the inevitability of militarization,
the panel did not specifically anticipate the use of AI-generated kill lists and building-target
recommendations at scale.
