                                               Abstract
          Binary Goppa codes form the algebraic foundation of the McEliece cryptosystem and the
     NIST post-quantum candidate Classic McEliece. To evaluate concrete key-recovery security
     margins across code parameters, the Technology Innovation Institute (TII) McEliece Chal-
     lenges provide 206 structural key-recovery instances (Track 2). Existing algebraic key-recovery
     attacks rely either on shortened-dual rank-2 and higher-order vanishing (HOV/HOVER) sys-
     tems, whose standard kernels collapse at moderate code rates, or on primal hold-out attacks,
     whose high-order derivative systems still span up to tens of millions of columns after shorten-
     ing.
          We present a primal-shortened key-recovery framework (Route A) built on a direct
     structural observation: although the public parity-check matrix scrambles the rows of the
     private Goppa matrix, it leaves the primal codeword space invariant, where every minimum-
     weight codeword obeys an exact quadratic identity over the secret support. Restricting
     the primal code to a compact column window before sampling compresses this degree-2
     interpolation system by up to 6.59× without penalizing multi-target Information-Set Decoding
     (ISD) yield. Pairing a cache-resident multi-target ISD engine with a systematic F2 -linear
     lifting solver reduces key recovery to lightweight codeword harvesting plus seconds of dense
     binary linear algebra, complemented by an accelerated HOVER solver (Route B) on one
     high-rate parameter family.
          Across the official TII catalog, our framework recovers 94 Track-2 private keys (89 via
     Route A and 5 via Route B), achieving 55 first-blood recoveries and accelerating 39 previously
     solved instances, with all 94 keys verified by TII’s official SageMath checker. Compared
     to the shortened-dual records on TII-166 and TII-125 (9.20 h and 9.47 h on 72 cores,
     CRYPTO 2026), Route A finishes in 5.36 s and 5.90 s on a single 64-core CPU server,
     achieving 6,968× and 6,493× core-hour speedups. On TII-254 and TII-253, it finishes in
     30.4 s and 1.80 s, achieving 1,896× and 43,400× wall-time speedups over the prior GPU-
     cluster record and the multi-core hold-out record by Anthropic. Finally, our codeword-supply
     analysis shows that Classic McEliece standard parameters lie far outside the feasible regime
     of this attack.


1    Introduction
Code-based public-key cryptography, initiated by McEliece [28] in 1978 and reformulated in dual
syndrome form by Niederreiter [29], is one of the oldest approaches to public-key encryption that
resists quantum attacks. Its security rests on two distinct assumptions: the average-case hardness
of bounded-distance syndrome decoding in random binary linear codes (whose worst-case general
decoding problem is NP-complete [4]), and the structural hardness of distinguishing a public
Goppa parity-check matrix Hpub ∈ Fmt×n2     from a uniform matrix or recovering its secret algebraic
representation. Whereas high-rate alternant instances and compact algebraic proposals such
as generalized Reed–Solomon subcodes succumbed to structural distinguishers and filtration
attacks [15, 10], binary Goppa codes [31, 25] have withstood nearly five decades of cryptanalysis
and form the basis of the NIST post-quantum key-encapsulation candidate Classic McEliece.

1.1    Prior Key-Recovery Paradigms and Bottlenecks
The 206 Track-2 challenges span extension degrees m ∈ {5, . . . , 10}, Goppa degrees t ∈
{2, . . . , 24}, and security ratings λTII ∈ [22, 255]. The 36 instances with λTII ≤ 82 were solved
during the initial competition without published keys, while 41 of the 170 instances with λTII ≥ 83
were subsequently solved across eight public papers and repositories [30, 2, 7, 18, 38, 33, 34, 35, 39].
Aside from Panny’s support-splitting search on small parameters [30] (such as 161 core-days on
TII-91), published algebraic key-recovery attacks fall into two paradigms.

Shortened-Dual Rank-2 and Higher-Order Vanishing Attacks. Because the rows of
Hraw (L, g) are F2 -bit slices of rational evaluations zji /g(zj ) over F2m , componentwise products of
dual codewords satisfy low-rank polynomial relations that do not hold for random linear codes.
Building on high-rate alternant distinguishers [15, 10], quadratic-form spaces [11], the syzygy
distinguisher [32], and the higher-order vanishing (HOV) distinguisher [19], recent attacks first
shorten the dual code Γ(L, g)⊥ at s coordinates to compress its dimension before extracting
algebraic structure. Bardet, Lemoine, and Tillich [2] (via Pfaffian rank-2 ideals) and Briaud et
al. [7] (via bilinear rank-2 modeling) isolate rank-2 forms in the shortened dual square, whereas
Hemmert [18] (via low-rank catalecticant MinRank search) and Saarinen [33] (HOVER, via the
public F2m -endomorphism algebra) exploit degree-3 and degree-4 forms that vanish together with
all first partial derivatives on the shortened dual code. Although these dual methods account
for 37 of the 41 prior Track-2 solutions, their non-trivial vanishing kernels exist only at high

Primal Hold-Out Attacks. To reach moderate code rates where shortened-dual kernels
vanish, Ghoshal, Ishai, Jain, and Sun [17] introduced the primal hold-out distinguisher, which
Vedenev [38], Weis [39], and Saarinen [34] extended to key recovery. Viewing the columns
of a shortened generator matrix (of dimension kℓ ∈ [35, 50]) as points on a hidden rational
curve, the attack holds out one or two anchor columns and searches for low-degree kℓ -variate
polynomials (typically of degree 6) whose values and low-order Hasse derivatives vanish at all
remaining columns. By univariate Hermite interpolation along the curve, any such polynomial
must also vanish at the held-out columns, exposing the secret support inside the kernel. However,
representingdegree-6 multilinear polynomials in kℓ variables still produces sparse matrices
of width k6ℓ ∼ 107 columns, turning kernel extraction into a large-scale sparse Krylov or
block-Lanczos computation. Recovering TII-253 (214, 8, 9) took 21.7 h on 32 Xeon threads by
Weis at Anthropic [39] or 14.0 h on an H200 GPU with 48 CPU threads [38] (which also solved
the shorter instances TII-173 and TII-249). Scaling further to TII-254 (223, 8, 12) required
two deflated 15,527,170-column operators taking 16.0 h wall time and 128 GiB RAM on up to
8× NVIDIA GH200 GPUs (27.2 GH200 GPU-h across the two Krylov stages) [34, 35].

1.2       Main Results and Technical Overview

4.4     Complementary Route B on High-Rate Instances
For the single high-rate parameter family m = 10, t = 11, mt = 110, n ∈ [1008, 1020] (k ∈
[898, 910], rate R ≈ 0.891 > 1 − 2/t), primal shortening in Route A would require a window n′ ≈
443 (w = 23). Instead, the dual code admits a clean degree-3 (pHOV = 3) higher-order vanishing
(HOV) kernel at dual shortening s∗ = 36 (κ = mt − s∗ = 74, ρ = mp2HOV = 90) [19, 18, 33].
For these five challenges (TII-172, 187, 240, 246, 252), we deploy an optimized HOVER
engine (Route B; Appendix A): combining Frobenius rank invariance [33] with the known span
dimension ρ = 90 replaces the 10-matrix catalecticant rank sum over F210 by a single rank
check rank Cat(h0 ) = 9, incremental commutator intersection stabilizes the algebra Eβ from
1–2 right-kernel blocks instead of all 650 blocks, and Segre–Veronese fiber extraction on 450
truncated coordinates is completed via closed-form support extension (25). Route B solves
all five (10, 11) instances in 89.10–90.10 s on 1 physical CPU core (0.025 ch), achieving two
first-blood recoveries (TII-172, 187) and a 4.17× wall-time / 50.0× core-hour speedup over
the 12-core reference HOVER solver on TII-252 [33].
