    Collision and Preimage Cryptanalysis of Blender
                                              MiniMax

                                           November 2026


                                               Abstract
         Blender is an addition-rotation-XOR cryptographic hash function family submitted by
     Bradbury to the first round of the NIST SHA-3 competition in 2008. Spanning the four stan-
     dard digest sizes from 224 to 512 bits, the algorithm pairs a carry-coupled working register
     and persistent carry flags with a write-only modular-sum accumulator. To resist local dif-
     ferentials and state-collision attacks, the design incorporates 13-byte cyclic prefix padding,
     dual modular message checksums, and a minimum 32-round block-alignment rule before
     discarding the working register to output the accumulator. Prior cryptanalysis required at
     least 2107.3 operations for preimages, lacked a practical standard-IV collision generator, and
     left the odd-word variant Blender-224 unbroken below the 2112 birthday bound.
         We present a unified collision and preimage cryptanalysis of the full Blender family by ex-
     ploiting the decoupling between its carry-driven working register and its write-only modular
     accumulator. First, we establish a global shifted subspace collapse that locks periodic reg-
     ister differences whenever the per-round mask count divides the state length. This reduces
     the 258-bit working state of Blender-256 to a 38-bit invariant graph and yields standard-IV
     cycle-swap collisions in under one second alongside zero-cost multicollisions. Second, we
     construct a local two-step differential that hides a message perturbation inside a symmetric
     carry transition and heals it in the next round via bitwise-complement inputs. Combined
     with carry-free polynomial prefix inversion and byte-lattice contraction, this trail produces
     constant-time semi-free-start collisions across all four NIST variants, breaks Blender-224
     in 269.56 evaluations, and yields minimal 24-byte standard-IV and 36-byte identical-prefix
     collisions on Blender-256. Third, we combine multi-shifted closed-walk steering with 2-adic
     lattice invariants and generalized birthday trees over the modular accumulator ring. This re-
     duces full first- and second-preimage complexities on Blender-256, Blender-384, and Blender-
     512 to 235.93 , 266.34 , and 267.64 evaluations, showing that write-only modular accumulators
     cannot compensate for low-rank mask broadcasting.


1    Introduction
Submitted by Colin Bradbury in October 2008 and selected by NIST among the 51 first-round
SHA-3 candidates [1], Blender was designed to achieve high software throughput across 16-, 32-,
and 64-bit processors without lookup tables or S-boxes. A Blender instance is parameterized
by a word width w ∈ {16, 32, 64} in bits and a working-state length m ∈ {6, 7, 8, 9, 10} in
words. These parameters define a digest length n = mw in bits and a per-round mask count
t ∈ {2, 3}. The four official NIST SHA-3 variants are Blender-224 (w = 32, m = 7), Blender-256
(w = 32, m = 8), Blender-384 (w = 64, m = 6), and Blender-512 (w = 64, m = 8). All four
official variants operate with t = 2 masks per round.
    Whereas earlier stream-oriented hashes such as PANAMA [3] continually feed an auxiliary
buffer back into the active state during absorption, Blender—like fellow first-round candidate
Waterfall [5]—separates its internal state into an active m-word working register (augmented
with t persistent 1-bit carry flags) and a strictly write-only m-word accumulator. However,
Blender’s concrete compression and finalization primitives depart substantially from LFSR and
S-box constructions:


                                                   1
 1. Carry-Coupled ARX Compression: At each message word, Blender evaluates t mod-
    ular integer additions that incorporate the persistent carry flags from the preceding step.
    The newly generated carry bits select a data-dependent rotation count, and the rotated
    sums are XORed alternately into the cyclically shifted working register.

 2. Modular-Sum Accumulation with Working-State Truncation: After every com-
    pression step, each updated working word is added modulo 2w into the corresponding word
    of the write-only accumulator. The accumulator never feeds back into the working register
    or carry flags. At the end of finalization, the working register and carry flags are discarded,
    and the accumulator words are serialized to form the n-bit digest.

 3. Cyclic Prefix Padding, Block Alignment, and Dual Checksums: To counter local
    differentials and length-extension attacks, Blender buffers the first 13 bytes of the message
    and replays them cyclically after the message body so that the message, padding, bit-length
    encoding, and two running modulo-2w message checksums (accumulating the absorbed
    words and their bitwise complements) execute at least 32 compression rounds and end on
    a 16-word block boundary before extracting the digest.

1.1   Prior Cryptanalysis
After NIST accepted Blender among the 51 first-round SHA-3 candidates in December 2008 [15],
six public cryptanalytic works examined the design in 2008–2009:

  • Newbold (2008 [13]): Newbold chained n+w             2 + 1 Joux working-state collisions [6] with a
    birthday search over the accumulator and primary checksum (n + w bits). This yielded full-
    state collisions in 2(n+w)/2 compression rounds per stage (2128 , 2144 , 2224 , and 2288 across
    Blender-224/256/384/512—above the 2n/2 birthday bound for a single collision, but yield-
    ing 2k -multicollisions in k · 2(n+w)/2 rounds with O(n)-word segments). Applying the
    Gauravaram–Kelsey additive-checksum framework [4] yielded preimages in n · 2(n+w)/2
    rounds (2135.8 , 2152.0 , 2232.6 , and 2297.0 , with O(n2 )-word messages) using cycle-finding.

  • Mendel (2008 [11]) and Lamberger–Mendel (ISC 2009 [10]): These works treated
    the compression round as a black box. By varying four message words (4w ≥ n/2 bits)
    and adjusting a fifth word modulo 2w to hold the five-word modular sum constant, each
    five-word Joux stage collides the working state and both checksums in 2n/2 evaluations (or
    2n/2+1 when accounting for the two carry flags omitted in [10]). Repeating n stages (5n + 1
    words including a padding-adjustment word) builds a 2n -multicollision in published time
    n · 2n/2 (2119.8 , 2136.0 , 2200.6 , and 2265.0 ; twice as high with carry matching). A Quisquater–
    Delescaille memoryless meet-in-the-middle search [14] on the accumulator then recovers
    preimages across all four variants with O(n) memory.

  • Xu and Li (IACR ePrint 2008/532 [19]): Xu and Li exhibited a 24-byte (6-word)
    colliding message pair (04 ∥0∥ffffffff versus 04 ∥ffffffff∥0) exclusively for the single all-
    zero initial working state and zero carries. In that state, all modular additions degenerate
    into carry-free XORs. Because reaching the all-zero state from the standard initialization
    vector (IV) costs 2256 operations, no semi-free-start (SFS) generator for non-zero states or
    standard-IV short collision was obtained.

  • Klı́ma (2008 [8] and IACR ePrint 2009/006 [9]): Klı́ma first reported a 927-bit versus
    926-bit near-collision matching 225 of 256 digest bits on Blender-256 [8]. In [9], Klı́ma

    repeat every n = mw rounds. This left n/2 + 2 unconstrained bits and located 10 working-
    state fixpoints in 10 · n · 2n/4+1 compression rounds (276.3 , 2108.9 , and 2141.3 on Blender-
    256/384/512). Auditing [9] against the Blender specification uncovers three fundamental

    Prior to our work, four major cryptanalytic gaps remained open across the Blender family.
First, once message-hashing costs are accounted for, no valid standard-IV collision construc-
tion faster than the 2n/2 generic birthday bound—let alone a practical sub-second collision
generator—was known. Second, no collision attack faster than the 2112 birthday bound was
known for odd-word Blender-224 (m = 7). Third, no short (< 100-byte) standard-IV collision,
identical-prefix collision for arbitrary prefixes, or arbitrary-state SFS generator was known for
any variant. Fourth, no preimage attack broke the 2107 barrier on Blender-256 or the 2171
barrier on Blender-384 and Blender-512.

Table 1 summarizes our results across the Blender family. Our analysis develops three core
techniques:
 1. Shifted Clean Subspace Collapse, Parity Obstruction, and Sub-Second Cycle-
    Swap Collisions (Section 3): Across all 15 variants in Bradbury’s specification, we
    prove a general divisibility criterion for subspace closure. Constraining the leading updated
    working word to a constant word or its bitwise complement makes all m − t stride-t register
    XOR differences message-independent if and only if t divides m. This criterion holds for
    9 of the 15 variants (including Blender-256, Blender-384, and Blender-512) and explains

    smaller). We also establish a Parity Obstruction Theorem proving that the unshifted zero-
    difference fixed point is unreachable from the standard Blender-256 IV for any message. In
    addition, we prove that the carry-coupled branching graph on the clean subspace has an
    exact mean out-degree of 2.000. Pairing two overlapping ancestor cycles on a single depth-
    first search (DFS) tree yieldsw standard-IV Blender-256 collisions in 0.045–0.73 s, zero-cost

      r multicollisions, and Θ(2 /ℓ) collisions on Blender-384 and Blender-512.

 2. Two-Step Silent Carry Healing, All-Variant O(1) SFS, and Solver-Free 24-Byte
    Collisions (Section 4): Bypassing the t | m divisibility barrier via a two-step local
    perturbation-and-correction trail [2], we introduce a carry-coupled ARX differential. The
    first step injects a silent carry perturbation that leaves the working register and accumula-
    tor unchanged while flipping both carry flags from (0, 0) to (1, 1). The second step applies
    bitwise-complement message words that lock the adder inputs to mid-point constants, si-
    multaneously healing the carry difference and preserving both modular message checksums.
    We establish an unordered state validity density of 2−69.56 across Blender-224 and 2−69.58
    across Blender-256, breaking Blender-224 in 269.56 ≪ 2112 state evaluations. We also de-
    rive deterministic zero-retry O(1) closed-form SFS collisions for all four NIST variants via
    circulant polynomial inversion, and prove that 24 bytes (6 words) is the global minimum
    standard-IV collision length on Blender-256. Finally, we build a solver-free 4-round lane-
    invariant and 2-round byte-lattice contraction solver (0.11–0.29 ms per target) that finds
    24-byte standard-IV and 40-byte identical-prefix collisions in pure C.
 3. Multi-Shifted Closed-Walk Steering and Preimage Cryptanalysis (Section 5):
    Building on multi-block internal-state collisions [6] and generalized birthday checksum can-
    cellation [17, 4, 12], we develop a multi-shifted closed-walk preimage attack over the modu-
    lar ring of Blender’s accumulator. Because Blender discards its working state immediately
    after the checksum rounds, equal-length, equal-checksum closed walks require no finaliza-
    tion inversion. Using 2-adic Smith Normal Form (SNF) analysis, we show that unshifted
    clean walks suffer a 12-bit subgroup rank defect that starves the leading accumulator lane.
    We prove that chaining closed walks across multiple shifted clean subspaces injects odd 2-
    adic units into the leading lane and saturates the maximal parity subgroup of index 2m−2 .
    Combining 8-anchor reverse-reachable DAG [7] slot generation with a joint checksum-and-
    accumulator generalized birthday tree [17] reduces full first- and second-preimage complex-
    ities to 235.93 on Blender-256, 266.34 on Blender-384, and 267.64 on Blender-512. We also
    verify the exact 2-adic SNF subgroup ranks and the closed-walk modular superposition
    identity empirically on Bradbury’s unmodified NIST C reference implementation.

2.2    State Layout and Initialization
We follow the official NIST SHA-3 Round-1 specification and C/C++ reference submission [1].
Blender defines 15 hash instances indexed by word width w ∈ {16, 32, 64} and state word count
m ∈ {6, 7, 8, 9, 10}, producing an n = mw-bit digest. The per-round mask count is t = 2 for
m ∈ {6, 7, 8} (the x2 family, which encompasses all four required NIST SHA-3 output sizes)
and t = 3 for m ∈ {9, 10} (the x3 family):

              Blender-224 : (w=32, m=7, t=2),         Blender-256 : (w=32, m=8, t=2),

              Blender-384 : (w=64, m=6, t=2),         Blender-512 : (w=64, m=8, t=2).
Each Blender instance maintains six state components. The active trunk consists of working
words W = (W0 , . . . , Wm−1 ) ∈ ({0, 1}w )m and persistent carry flags c1 , c2 ∈ {0, 1} (plus c3 ∈
{0, 1} when t = 3). The output and schedule state consists of modular-sum accumulators
Sum = (Sum[0], . . . , Sum[m − 1]) ∈ (Z/2w Z)m , running word checksums C1 , C2 ∈ Z/2w Z, a 13-
byte prefix buffer F [0 . . . 12], and a fragment-alignment counter enforcing the 32-round minimum
and 16-fragment block alignment.
    At initialization, Sum = (0, . . . , 0), c1 = c2 = 0, and C1 = C2 = 0. The initial working
words W IV are derived from fractional square roots of primes (matching the standard initial
words of SHA-224, SHA-256, and SHA-512 for Blender-224, Blender-256, and Blender-512).
In Bradbury’s official NIST C/C++ reference implementation [1], the Blender-384 (m = 6)
initialization loop loads five 64-bit words and leaves W5IV = 0 rather than loading a sixth table
entry (whereas Blender-224 copies eight entries from an 18-word table whose first seven entries
match SHA-224, leaving the eighth slot unused). All structural theorems, closed-form SFS
generators, out-degree laws, and preimage bounds in this paper hold identically under both the
written specification IVs and the official C reference IVs. All concrete digests and test witnesses
are verified directly against the unmodified NIST C reference.

2.4   Prefix Repetition, Checksums, and Finalization
Given an ℓ-byte message M with 16-fragment block byte-size Bblk = 16b (64 B for w = 32;
128 B for w = 64) and an ℓL -byte little-endian bit-length encoding L(8ℓ) (ℓL ≥ 1 minimal
byte width of 8ℓ), finalization proceeds in three deterministic stages before output extraction
(Figure 1):
 1. Cyclic 13-Byte Prefix Padding and Block Alignment: Let F [0 . . . flen − 1] store the
    first flen = min(ℓ, 13) bytes of M (or 13 zero bytes if ℓ = 0). Blender appends k ≥ 1 cyclic
    prefix bytes F [i mod flen ] (0 ≤ i < k), where k is the smallest positive integer such that
    ℓ + k + (ℓL + 1) + 2b is at least 2Bblk (32 compression rounds) and is an exact multiple of
    Bblk (16 fragments). In particular, k = (2Bblk − 2b − 1) − ℓ − ℓL when ℓ + ℓL + 2b + 1 < 2Bblk
    (replaying all of F ), and k = Bblk − ((ℓ + ℓL + 2b + 1) mod Bblk ) ∈ {1, . . . , Bblk } otherwise
    (replaying only the first min(k, flen ) bytes when k < 13).

 2. Bit-Length Encoding: Immediately after the k cyclic prefix-pad bytes, Blender appends
    the ℓL -byte little-endian bit-length 8ℓ followed by the 1-byte descriptor ℓL . This aligns the
    byte stream to an exact b-byte word boundary and updates C1 , C2 , W , and Sum on every
    absorbed fragment.

 3. Dual Checksum Absorption and Digest Extraction: Two final compression rounds
    are executed with message-word inputs f = ¬C1 and f = C2 (accumulating the updated W ′
    into Sum). Immediately thereafter, (W, c1 , c2 ) is discarded and Sum[0 . . . m − 1] is serialized

6     Architectural Diagnosis and Experimental Evaluation
6.1     Diagnosis of Structural Weaknesses
Our three attacks trace back to three interacting architectural flaws in Blender’s ARX-plus-
accumulator design, each pointing to a concrete design principle:

 1. Uniform Stride-t Mask Broadcasting Across m Lanes: Injecting only t = 2 masks
    alternately across m ∈ {6, 8} words creates m − t linear mask-free XOR chains Xi =
    Wi ⊕ Wi+t that close periodically whenever t | m and W0 ∈ {x, ¬x} (Theorem 1). Breaking
    this symmetry requires either distinct round-dependent rotations per lane or coprime tap
    schedules coupled with wide non-linear state diffusion.

 2. Rotation-Sum Compensation Across Discrete Carry-Selected Rotations: Cou-
    pling (c′1 , c′2 ) to r = 8 − (c′1 + c′2 ) ∈ {6, 7, 8} allows a 2-bit carry jump (0, 0) ↔ (1, 1)
    at step p to be canceled inside the pre-rotation sum (rol2 (u) and ror2 (v)) with probabil-
    ity (7/48)2 ≈ 2−5.56 . That carry difference is then healed deterministically at step p + 1
    via (2w−1 , 2w−1 − 1) complement inputs (Theorem 4). Mixing carries back into multiple
    non-aligned bit positions and applying at least 4 rounds of feed-forward state mixing per
    absorbed message word prevents 2-step local healing.

 3. Unidirectional Accumulator Without Working-State Feed-Forward at Output:
    Updating Sum without feeding Sum back into W , and discarding (W, c1 , c2 ) immediately
    after the checksum rounds, makes finalization an invariant affine translation (Proposition 7).
