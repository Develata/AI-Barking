 As mathematicians race to verify AI Riemann proof, a human proof emerges 

 [https://www.quantamagazine.org/update/169569/]

 November 20, 2026 [/] 

Today’s news of a claimed proof of the Riemann hypothesis [https://www.quantamagazine.org/updates/transformation/] came in an unusual way: a cryptic, short tweet [https://x.com/scottnarmstrong/status/2123773647919661288?s=20] by Scott Armstrong [https://www.scottnarmstrong.com/], a mathematical physicist at the Sorbonne in Paris. Armstrong’s message read, in its entirety: “@__alpoge__ ftw” together with a screenshot of an unpublished paper [https://alpo.ge/remark.pdf] that Levent Alpöge of Anthropic had shared on his personal website. As Armstrong explained to me in a phone call, he is close with Alpöge’s collaborator Tristan Buckmaster, a professor at New York University. “Tristan has I think decided it’s amusing to share a result by giving it to me and I post it on Twitter.” Armstrong said that after he received a text from Buckmaster earlier today, he called to ask if there was a Lean verification. Assured that there was, he then tweeted the paper out.

This heterodox publishing process only works because of Lean, a programming language designed, in large part, to be able to make sure that math is done without mistakes. In the past few hours, experts in Lean have been racing to verify Alpöge’s claim, even as nobody yet understands the many steps in the proof. (His Lean code has about 17 million lines.)

There are many subtleties involved in the verification. Lean, besides being a mathematical language, is also a computer programming language, and can itself contain bugs. It can also be used to play sneaky tricks by, for example, redefining symbols used in a proof to make something trivially true. The first step in verifying a proof in Lean is to make sure it “compiles.” This means that there are no obvious logical fallacies in it. Subsequent steps include compiling it using different independent versions of Lean, making it unlikely that the proof is exploiting a bug in Lean itself, as well as running something called a comparator, which double-checks that the proof hasn’t maliciously redefined any key concepts.

Several independent efforts have now successfully compiled Alpöge’s proof. Andrew Sutherland [https://math.mit.edu/~drew/] at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology was the first; he finished shortly before noon [https://leanprover.zulipchat.com/#narrow/channel/113488-general/topic/Discussion.3A.20Alp.C3.B6ge.20Proves.20the.20Riemann.20Hypothesis/near/642278022]. A few hours later, Simon DeDeo [https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/people/faculty/simon-dedeo.html] at Carnegie Mellon University also succeeded, and not long after that Sutherland compiled it using a variant of Lean. Comparator checks for nefarious redefinition of symbols, which are slower because they cannot run on multiple processors in parallel, are still ongoing, and should be complete in a matter of hours or days. “I’d be very surprised if there were any secret tricks,” DeDeo wrote in an email.

As this unfolded, Ravi Vakil [https://math.stanford.edu/~vakil/], the president of the American Mathematical Society and a professor at Stanford University, uploaded a 940-page manuscript [https://math216.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/the-music-of-the-primes.pdf] by Alessandro Masullo, whose LinkedIn page [https://www.linkedin.com/in/alessandro-m-masullo-459094113/] shows that he’s a postdoctoral scholar at Stanford.

Vakil wrote [https://math216.wordpress.com/2026/11/20/hurried-announcement-alessandro-masullo-grh/]:

Alessandro Masullo has a proposed proof of [the generalized Riemann hypothesis], and Brian Conrad has been looking closely at it for months and gave extensive feedback which is being incorporated, and it is so far looking good. It is all human, with no AI. The hope was to bring Alessandro to Stanford for a year to polish the writing and then announce it after running it by enough people. Now it seems best to put out the current draft. I hope people quickly check it, formalize, fill any small gaps if they occur.

The Lean community is already beginning to discuss [https://leanprover.zulipchat.com/#narrow/channel/113488-general/topic/Formalising.20Masullo.27s.20proof.20of.20GRH/with/642322280] what a formalization of Masullo’s paper would entail. (Since a proof of Riemann that he was working on several years ago did not, in the end, hold up, many number theorists are withholding judgement pending further scrutiny.)

Meanwhile, the AI-generated proof has generated criticism within the mathematical community. As Maksym Radziwill [https://maksym-radziwill.github.io/], also of NYU, put it in an email, proof by Lean code short-circuits understanding by focusing on manipulating symbols: “If the symbols are right and follow the rules, then the symbols are a valid proof.” But that’s not really the goal, he said. Alpoge’s four-page writeup contains “nonsensical statements,” he said, which do not fill mathematicians with confidence. “The object of mathematics as the rest of science is really the question ‘why?’ and this doesn’t answer it at all.”

 A claimed proof of the Riemann hypothesis 

 [https://www.quantamagazine.org/update/169564/]

 November 20, 2026 [/] 

The Riemann hypothesis has long stood as the most prominent open question in mathematics. On its surface it deals with when a particular function, called the zeta function, equals zero. More deeply, it concerns how prime numbers are distributed. It also has far-reaching consequences across numerous areas of mathematics.

On the morning of Friday, November 20th, Levent Alpöge, a mathematician at the artificial intelligence company Anthropic, uploaded a plaintive four-page paper [https://alpo.ge/remark.pdf] to his personal website in which he wrote, “I believe I have, working with Claude, discovered a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis.” Because his attempts to get Claude (Anthropic’s AI model) to produce human-readable text amounted to over 250,000 pages, he has not shared such a text. He has, however, made available 17 million lines of code [https://www.quantamagazine.org/updates/transformation/#169054] in the programming language Lean which he says contain a formal verification of the proof, which is of a stronger result called the generalized Riemann hypothesis.

Lean experts around the world are racing to independently verify that the code works as he claims. If they succeed in doing so, the mathematical community will likely accept the validity of the proof even before anybody has fully understood it. Lean experts estimate that they will have an answer, one way or another, in a matter of hours, but do not anticipate there to be obstacles.

In his announcement, Alpöge was almost apologetic. “The precise timing is horrible,” he wrote, presumably referring to Anthropic’s recent initial public offering. Alpöge says he had obtained the result on Monday November 16, and delayed sharing it for several days.

“I am presenting the world with a complete argument before having mastered a massive neighbourhood around its mathematics. It being obviously inappropriate to attempt to post some massive unreadable travesty of ‘slop’ I decided to only present Lean,” he wrote.

By clicking to watch this video, you agree to our privacy policy. [/privacy-policy]

Alpöge describes the hypothesis as a lifelong fascination for him. His narration of his interaction with Claude demonstrates a markedly different approach than OpenAI’s 377 results released on October 6 [https://www.quantamagazine.org/update/167703/], almost all of which were the result of a simple prompt to an AI model. (Those results included two different proofs [https://www.quantamagazine.org/ai-proved-the-quasi-riemann-hypothesis-what-happens-now-20261019/] of a weaker result called the quasi-Riemann hypothesis.) Instead, Alpöge’s mathematical expertise played a major role in repeated, iterative attempts with Claude to move closer to the result. Alpöge says that the AI left him behind: “Claude flew, and danced, and sparkled near that finish line … Claude began to leave me in awe.”

As Filippo A. E. Nuccio, a number theorist at the Jean Monnet University in France wrote in an online chat [https://leanprover.zulipchat.com/#narrow/channel/113488-general/topic/Discussion.3A.20Alp.C3.B6ge.20Proves.20the.20Riemann.20Hypothesis], “WOW, this is gorgeous: it basically contains all number theory produced in the last 50 years, in all branches of the discipline.” Alpöge thinks this won’t long be the case, writing in his announcement: “An astounding amount of mathematics is present in the argument, far more than I had imagined would be necessary as a student. My intuition insists it will be massively simplified, likely immediately.”

Together with New York University mathematician Tristan Buckmaster, Alpöge also claims to have resolved [https://github.com/tristanbuckmaster/navier-stokes/] an important variation of the Navier-Stokes problem. This variation shows that a “singularity” can arise even in fluids that are not being pushed by an external force.

As of this writing, Anthropic has not made an official announcement of either result.

This is a breaking news story, Quanta will publish further updates if and when independent mathematicians verify the Lean code.

 Race to find high-ranking curves shows AI’s current limits 

 [https://www.quantamagazine.org/update/169537/]

 November 19, 2026 [/] 

Ask any mathematician to give you an example of pure math being unexpectedly useful in the real world, and you are likely to hear about elliptic curves. They were studied for centuries as a mathematical curiosity, but they now enable bitcoin and secure web browsing, among many other applications.

To my eye, at least, they look more awkward than elegant:

 Merrill Sherman/Quanta Magazine

But compared to lots of mathematical abstractions, they are simple to write down. They’re any equation of the form y2 = x3 + ax + b. (Minor technical caveat, which you can ignore: The constants a and b cannot be related by 27b2 = –4a3.)

The images above illustrate a key feature of elliptic curves. Equations that look very similar might describe curves that have profoundly different properties. It’s as if adding a centimeter to the length of a car suddenly makes it go 10 times faster. This hidden complexity makes elliptic curves mathematically interesting and powerful.

Part of that complexity can be encapsulated in a number called the curve’s rank. Rank measures a way to count a curve’s so-called rational points [https://www.quantamagazine.org/without-a-proof-mathematicians-wonder-how-much-evidence-is-enough-20181031/] — solutions for x and y that can be written as fractions. Rank 0 curves have either no rational points, or at most finitely many. Other curves have an infinite number of rational points, all of which can be easily found given one particular starting point. Such curves have rank 1.

Most elliptic curves have either rank 0 or 1, but some have higher ranks. Curves with two distinct families of rational solutions — you need two independent starting points to find all the rational solutions — are rank 2 curves [https://www.lmfdb.org/EllipticCurve/Q/?rank=2]. Some have three such families: rank 3 [https://www.lmfdb.org/EllipticCurve/Q/?rank=3], and so forth.

The numbers of known curves diminish swiftly with rank. One well-established catalog includes hundreds of thousands [https://www.lmfdb.org/EllipticCurve/Q/?rank=2] of rank 2 curves, and just 19 curves with rank 5 [https://www.lmfdb.org/EllipticCurve/Q/?rank=5].

For decades, mathematicians have been searching for elusive curves with higher and higher ranks. In 2006, Harvard mathematician Noam Elkies [https://people.math.harvard.edu/~elkies/] found a curve with rank at least 28. That record stood for 18 years until Elkies and Zev Klagsbrun teamed up in 2024 [https://www.quantamagazine.org/new-elliptic-curve-breaks-18-year-old-record-20241111/] to find a curve whose rank is at least 29.

Since it’s easy to sift through trillions of curves, this search seems particularly ripe for AI. “It’s a juicy problem to throw computational resources at,” as David Renshaw [https://icarm.io/people/] of the Institute for Computer-Aided Reasoning in Mathematics at Carnegie Mellon University put it in June [https://icarm.zulipchat.com/#narrow/channel/519875-general/topic/Elliptic.20Curve.20Rank.20Leaderboard/near/603443505], when he set up a leaderboard [https://elliptic-rank.icarm.cloud/] tracking (largely AI-enabled) efforts to find high-ranking elliptic curves.

In August, an anonymous account [https://x.com/nasqret/status/2090429815941685491] (later revealed to be Anthropic mathematician Levent Alpöge [https://alpo.ge/] and Ava Howell [https://avahowell.me/pages/about/], a systems engineer) announced a rank 30 curve [https://elliptic-rank.icarm.cloud/curve/273], then a rank 31 [https://elliptic-rank.icarm.cloud/curve/273] curve. Thousands of curves with rank at least 30 and 31 have since been discovered with the aid of AI.

Samuel Li [https://samuelj.li/about/?utm_source=chatgpt.com], whose day job involves microchip engineering at Samsung, has a sideline as an amateur number theorist, and has been one of the protagonists of the AI search. He estimates that he and others have searched roughly 10 billion billion (1019) curves. So far, in that haystack, three needles with rank of at least 32 have been found. (Klagsbrun, Elkies, and Li have found two of these; the third was found by Lewis Combes [https://lewismcombes.github.io/], Blair Butler [https://www.maths.usyd.edu.au/ut/people?who=BJ_Butler] and Shir Levav Porat [https://www.maths.usyd.edu.au/ut/people?who=S_Levav+Porat&sms=y], at the University of Sydney.)

The new curves have all been found using a technique that Elkies pioneered in 2006. He discovered that if you take a particular surface and slice it the right way [https://www.quantamagazine.org/new-elliptic-curve-breaks-18-year-old-record-20241111/], you can find high-ranking curves. As Andrej Dujella [https://web.math.pmf.unizg.hr/~duje/] of the University of Zagreb put it, “AI is now a real participant in the search, but the geometric object that makes these records possible was constructed by a human two decades ago.”

For decades, the surface that Elkies had found was the only one useful for finding high ranking curves. But starting in August, Li found several other similarly fertile surfaces.

As of now, the rank-32 curves still hold the record, but nobody knows if there is an upper limit to the rank. “In order to say anything about ranks being bounded or unbounded, it seems we would need to approach this problem with some kind of genuine new idea,” Butler said in an email. But as his collaborator Combes noted, there is not sign of anyone — human or computer — having such an idea: “When the rank 30 and 31 curves were posted, I half-expected to see a new record every week, as an advertisement of some unpublished method for generating arbitrarily high ranks. But that isn’t what happened, and all the evidence points towards not even a hint of such a method being known to anyone.”

 AI’s ability to ‘autoformalize’ proofs is changing math 

 [https://www.quantamagazine.org/update/169054/]

 November 11, 2026 [/] 

It seems like every odd number greater than 5 can be written as the sum of three primes. (They don’t have to be distinct: 11 = 3 + 3 + 5.) Harald Helfgott, [https://webusers.imj-prg.fr/~harald.helfgott/anglais/] a senior researcher at France’s National Center for Scientific Research, claimed to have proved this result [https://arxiv.org/abs/1312.7748], known as the ternary Goldbach conjecture, in 2013. Yet there were some issues with the initial proof. Helfgott believed he had fixed them [https://math.stackexchange.com/questions/3164020/the-significance-and-acceptance-of-helfgott-s-proof-of-the-weak-goldbach-conject/3181521], but his argument would eventually stretch across more than 500 pages [https://webusers.imj-prg.fr/~harald.helfgott/anglais/book.html]. That’s a lot of math to check.

This type of dilemma is why formal proofs exist. Formal proofs reduce every line of mathematical argument to extremely basic steps — so basic that a computer can verify each small step in a mechanistic way. The idea is to remove expertise and persuasion from the picture, and in so doing, to eliminate the possibility of mistakes. “Formalizing” a proof like this has been a tedious and time-consuming process. It also required significant mathematical expertise to make sure it was done correctly. (The nuances here are chronicled by longtime Quanta Magazine contributor Kevin Harnett in his book “The Proof in the Code [https://www.quantabooks.org/books/the-proof-in-the-code/].”)

Over the past few years, a central goal of the AI-for-math community has been to figure out how to use AI systems to translate ordinary mathematical arguments into formally verifiable code. If this process of “autoformalization” succeeds, it gives AI models the ability to check their own work. But autoformalization had been suffering from the same accuracy problems that plagued other AI outputs. For instance, a May paper from Meta [https://ai.meta.com/research/publications/autoformbot-formalizing-mathematics-at-scale/] that claimed to formalize 26 math textbooks was riddled with errors [https://leanprover.zulipchat.com/#narrow/channel/583336-Autoformalization/topic/Atlas.20.28Meta.29].

Like so much else, AI autoformalization capabilities changed in the summer of 2026. In early September, Anthropic announced that they had formalized the proof of Fermat’s Last Theorem [https://www.anthropic.com/research/formalizing-fermats-last-theorem]. It took a swarm of agents 11 days to do so; they produced some 13 million lines of computer code. Unlike the Meta paper, experts concluded that the autoformalization worked. “If thousands of pages of the literature can be formalized end-to-end by some kind of AI swarm in an 11 day period now, then in the future we will start to see formalization of modern research being done on the fly,” wrote [https://xenaproject.wordpress.com/2026/09/04/flt-anthropic-has-beaten-me-to-it/] Kevin Buzzard, [https://profiles.imperial.ac.uk/k.buzzard] a formalization expert at Imperial College London. As if to prove his point, Anthropic researchers announced on November 9 that what had taken 11 days in September can now be done in 30 hours [https://claude.dev/blog/safely-scaling-multi-agent-teams-with-fable-5-2/].

About a third of OpenAI’s October 6 announcement of hundreds of results [https://www.quantamagazine.org/update/167703/] were accompanied by formal proofs. Since then, there have been increasing efforts to formalize not only particular results, but also entire bodies of knowledge. Beginning last week, an AI startup called Axiom Math [https://axiommath.ai/] announced four autoformalization results in rapid succession:

The formalization of the classification [https://axiommath.ai/research/the-enormous-theorem-formalized/] of finite simple groups [https://www.quantamagazine.org/groups-underpin-modern-math-heres-how-they-work-20240906/], an enormous effort spanning tens of thousands of pages over many years, that no human had fully understood.

A huge open-source textbook [https://axiommath.ai/research/the-stacks-project-formalized/] of modern algebraic geometry written by Aise Johan de Jong [https://www.math.columbia.edu/~dejong/] of Columbia University, called the Stacks Project [https://stacks.math.columbia.edu/].

The Weil conjectures [https://axiommath.ai/research/the-weil-conjectures-formalized-in-lean/], precursors to the Langlands program [https://www.quantamagazine.org/what-is-maths-mysterious-langlands-program-really-about-20260909/], which is central to modern math.

The first seven chapters [https://axiommath.ai/research/cluster-algebras-formalized-in-lean/] of a textbook on cluster algebras.

Independent experts say they are satisfied with Axiom’s formalizations. “They have done impressive things,” said Johan Commelin [https://math.commelin.net/], the director of the Mathlib Initiative, a key formalization effort. While formalizing the classification of finite simple groups, Axiom say they found 96 (minor and correctable [https://x.com/IlinVasily29521/status/2117337286421725688]) errors in 47 published papers.

And as for Helfgott’s proof of the ternary Goldbach conjecture, it too, has been formally verified [https://x.com/jdlichtman/status/2120301890504441905?a=cGpaPmKLEYwQTLKeo4U&s=20], as of November 10. As Jared Duker Lichtman [https://mathematics.stanford.edu/people/jared-duker-lichtman] of Stanford University put it in an email to me, “This is huge!!”

It now seems a question not of if, but when, all of human mathematical knowledge will be formalized by AI.

Aaronson expressed mixed feelings [https://scottaaronson.blog/?p=10295] about this on his blog.

The “shepherd’s group” of eminent physicists vetting OpenAI’s 1,147-page manuscript [https://www.quantamagazine.org/updates/transformation/#168483] in quantum field theory published [https://www.kitp.ucsb.edu/sites/default/files/3rd-Statement-About-Quantum-YangMills-Theory_Nov-9.pdf] formalizations [https://github.com/yang-mills-group] of the claimed results. This has followed weeks of closed-door scrutiny by the physicists amid calls from some mathematicians for their immediate release [https://agmai.org/#request-immediate-release].

Elliot Glazer, a logician at Epoch AI, announced the existence [https://x.com/ElliotGlazer/status/2118403066198376903] of a polynomial with 292,890,231,905 variables with integer coefficients with the following properties: If all but one of the variables are rational numbers (fractions) and the polynomial equals 0, the last remaining variable is guaranteed to be an integer. His announcement provoked a great deal of controversy [https://x.com/DanRomik/status/2119094176944206190?a=1uqIHwCh4k3gqUjz_1g&s=20]. Glazer and others subsequently found much smaller polynomials with the same properties.

 Mathematicians race to simplify AI proofs 

 [https://www.quantamagazine.org/update/168664/]

 November 5, 2026 [/] 

The OpenAI proof dump [https://www.quantamagazine.org/update/167703/] in early October included a landmark result in number theory: a claimed proof that so-called “Landau-Siegel zeros” do not exist [https://github.com/openai/math/blob/main/preprints/Uniform-exclusion-of-Landau-Siegel-zeros-October-1-2026/paper.pdf]. It was overshadowed by the company’s proof of the generalized quasi-Riemann hypothesis [https://www.quantamagazine.org/ai-proved-the-quasi-riemann-hypothesis-what-happens-now-20261019/], which automatically implied the nonexistence of these special zeros. But the Landau-Siegel zero result is significant in and of itself.

Mathematicians probe patterns in the prime numbers using the so-called zeta function. To then study patterns in how the primes interact with other numbers, they look at L-functions, which are generalizations of the zeta function. Both the Landau-Siegel and generalized quasi-Riemann problems are about particular L-functions that are important in number theory. In essence, Landau-Siegel is about how prime numbers behave. As Andrew Granville [https://dms.umontreal.ca/~andrew/expository.php] of the University of Montreal put it, “What Siegel zeros say is, something crazy happens.”

The inputs to L-functions are complex numbers, each of which has two parts: a real part, and an imaginary one (a multiple of the square root of –1, which mathematicians call i). The generalized Riemann hypothesis posits that, with some trivial exceptions, these L-functions equal zero only when their input has a real part of 1/2. (The “quasi” version is less strict.)

If the generalized Riemann hypothesis turns out to be false, it would mean some other zeros might exist. The farther a would-be zero is from having a real part of 1/2, the bigger a disruption it would cause to how prime numbers are expected to behave. That’s where Landau-Siegel zeros come in. These are putative zeros that have an imaginary part of 0 and a real part very close to 1 (which is, in the context of this problem, very far away from 1/2). This makes these zeros particularly important.

Mathematicians have long known that the zeta function itself does not have Landau-Siegel zeros. But even though numerical checks made it seem unlikely, no one could rule out the possibility that certain other L-functions might. If such zeros existed, that fact could be used to prove many other important results — including the twin primes conjecture [https://terrytao.wordpress.com/2015/08/26/heath-browns-theorem-on-prime-twins-and-siegel-zeroes/], which says that there are an infinite number of prime numbers that differ by 2 (such as 11 and 13, or 17 and 19). “If you have a Siegel zero, then other things fall into place in an unexpected way,” said Granville.

“It’s all moot now,” he added.

In the weeks since OpenAI released its proof that Landau-Siegel zeros do not exist, mathematicians have been [https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.15483v1] posting [https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21099] ever-simpler versions of the result.

One reformulation [https://hexagonmath.org/2610.00311?version=1], by Ben Green [https://people.maths.ox.ac.uk/greenbj/] of the University of Oxford, is, as Terence Tao wrote [https://terrytao.wordpress.com/2026/10/19/a-sketch-of-the-proof-of-non-existence-of-siegel-zeroes/], “particularly elegant.” Green obtained his proof through conversations with OpenAI’s Astra model, and demurs that “no human originality is claimed.” Jacob Pescador [https://jacobpescador.github.io/ai-math/papers/landau-siegel-stepanov/v3/], Kevin Barreto [https://x.com/acerfur/status/2112239827077026128], and Roelof van Dijk [https://hexagonmath.org/2610.00409?version=2] have used both Astra and Anthropic’s Opus 5.5 to further tweak Green’s reformulation.

“I generally won’t read AI papers directly,” said Bryce Kerr [https://www.unsw.edu.au/staff/bryce-kerr] of the University of New South Wales in Canberra, who assembled one of the other re-proofs of the Siegel result. “My process starts with asking AI to explain the new ideas or ‘big picture’ then I’ll try to fill in the details myself. If I get stuck I’ll go back to AI and ask it more questions. I guess it’s analogous to asking a human author to explain one of their papers.”

In place of the old races around priority, there appears to be an emerging competition to see who can devise the shortest, simplest proof. Barreto’s version weighs in at just three pages. “People are thinking I was trying to optimise for page count,” wrote [https://x.com/AcerFur/status/2112270215778033940] Barreto, who is an undergraduate at the University of Cambridge. “I was trying to optimise for more elementary classical machinery, which so happened to shorten the proof as a consequence.”

Granville, who is 64 years old, said that for many number theorists of his generation, the Riemann hypothesis always seemed too daunting a goal. But he and others had spent decades trying and failing to prove that Landau-Siegel zeros don’t exist. “For me,” he said, seeing the proof, “it’s quite emotional, more so in some ways than the quasi-Riemann hypothesis, because it’s what I really tried to do.”

 OpenAI releases three landmark results in math, physics, and computer science 

 [https://www.quantamagazine.org/update/168483/]

 October 29, 2026 [/] 

On October 26, OpenAI announced that an advanced AI model had solved three major problems [https://openai.com/index/building-scientific-understanding-with-academic-communities/]: one in math, one in theoretical computer science, and one in physics. On the advice of experts in the relevant areas, the company released the results in very different ways.

The math result, a proof of the “local smoothing conjecture,” was simply posted online [https://github.com/openai/math/blob/main/preprints/Local-smoothing-for-the-Euclidean-wave-equation-October-25-2026/paper.pdf], together with a Lean verification [https://github.com/openai/math/blob/main/lean/docs/079.md]. The idea is to give as many people as possible [https://agmai.org/#disadvantages-designating-mathematicians] an equal shot at making heads or tails of the AI’s output, which often appears unintelligible, even to experts.

The computer science result — which disproved a major conjecture, to the surprise of many — was also immediately released [https://github.com/openai/math/blob/main/preprints/The-Exponential-Time-Hypothesis-is-False-October-25-2026/paper.pdf], but this time with a promise that it would be “verified and shepherded” by three leading researchers in the field. Hours later, Anthropic independently released their own version of the result [https://hexagonmath.org/2610.00577].

On the advice of a group of physicists led by David Gross [https://www.kitp.ucsb.edu/about-us/people/permanent-members/gross], a Nobel Prize–winning physicist, OpenAI hasn’t yet released their third result, which claims to make a theory in particle physics mathematically rigorous. Instead, the physicists are now working to understand the 1,147-page AI proof [https://www.kitp.ucsb.edu/sites/default/files/YM_working_group.pdf] themselves. Meanwhile, Levent Alpöge [https://alpo.ge/] of Anthropic and Ethan Sussman [https://ethanwsussman.github.io/], a postdoctoral fellow at Northwestern University, have released work in progress [https://github.com/ethanwsussman/Yang--Mills-LLM-experiment] on related questions.

“In our view, these manuscripts are best understood as a form of raw research data,” OpenAI wrote. Each approach to converting that “raw data” into human understanding has pros and cons; it’s not clear if one of these approaches (or something new entirely) will emerge as a new norm for how to disseminate AI-generated claims in math and science.

For now, here’s a bit more detail about each of the three claims:

Proof of the Local Smoothing Conjecture

First described by Christopher Sogge [https://mathematics.jhu.edu/directory/christopher-sogge/] in a 1991 paper [https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF01245080], the local smoothing conjecture is about how waves like light and sound behave. It posits that as waves spread, they can’t concentrate too much energy in too small of a space for too long of a time.

To show this, mathematicians need to analyze solutions to the wave equation, a partial differential equation that looks simple but is hard to study. In 2019, Hong Wang [https://sites.google.com/view/hongwang/home] (who won a Fields medal [https://www.quantamagazine.org/hong-wang-wins-2026-fields-medal-the-third-woman-ever-20260723/] earlier this year) and two collaborators proved the local smoothing conjecture in two dimensions [https://arxiv.org/abs/1909.10693]. OpenAI claims to have proved it in all dimensions.

The conjecture stands at the top of a tower of important conjectures [https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-tower-of-conjectures-that-rests-upon-a-needle-20230912/] in harmonic analysis. The new proof automatically implies that these conjectures are also true. The tower includes the Kakeya conjecture [https://www.quantamagazine.org/once-in-a-century-proof-settles-maths-kakeya-conjecture-20250314/], a statement about how much maneuvering room is needed to get a needle to point in every possible direction. The new result would prove that the Kakeya conjecture is true in all dimensions.

Disproof of the Exponential Time Hypothesis

Logical formulas combine inputs, each of which can be either true or false, with NOT, OR, and AND statements. (If X is true and Y is false, then “X AND Y” is false, but “X OR Y” is true.) A formula is said to be “satisfiable” if there is a combination of inputs for which the formula as a whole is true. (The previous examples are satisfiable; “X AND Y” is true when both X and Y are true. However, “X AND NOT X” is unsatisfiable — there’s no input X that gives a true output.)

For small numbers of inputs, it’s simple enough to figure out if a formula is satisfiable or not. But if there are many different inputs, it can take a long time to do so. The exponential time hypothesis (ETH) concerns how much time algorithms need to figure this out for formulas that follow a particular format.

The ETH posited that such algorithms had to perform badly in a precise sense. Finding a faster algorithm would allow you to find faster algorithms for many other problems, but computer scientists largely expected that to be impossible.

Anthropic and OpenAI have now refuted the hypothesis, finding algorithms that perform faster than the hypothesized limit when there’s an extremely large number of variables [https://x.com/beenwrekt/status/2115445861006368787]. Researchers next hope to explore whether the result will lead to progress on the broader “P = NP [https://www.claymath.org/millennium/p-vs-np/]” problem, which is arguably the most important question in theoretical computer science.

Proof That Quantum Field Theories Are Consistent

For decades, physicists have built the so-called Standard Model of particle physics on math that is not fully rigorous [https://www.nature.com/articles/s42254-025-00909-2]. To firm up that foundation, one of the Millennium Prize Problems [https://www.claymath.org/millennium/yang-mills-the-maths-gap/] calls for a proof of two statements. First, that certain key mathematical elements of the Standard Model are rigorously consistent. And second, that the size of the lightest possible particle in the Standard Model with nonzero mass must have some fixed lower bound — its mass can’t be arbitrarily small. (This property is called the mass gap.) The unreleased OpenAI manuscript and work-in-progress from Sussman and Alpöge claim to prove the first statement, but not (as of yet) the second.

From the Quanta Archives

Out of the hundreds of results that OpenAI announced in its proof dump earlier this month, it shared “reasoning traces” — which explain how their AI model found what it found — for only ten of them. One of those ten [https://github.com/openai/math/blob/main/reasoning_traces/irrationality-exponent-of-pi.pdf] was a proof [https://github.com/openai/math/blob/main/preprints/The-irrationality-exponent-of-pi-is-2-September-24-2026/paper.pdf] that approximating π with fractions is as hard as it can possibly be.

In 2025, Erica Klarreich wrote about the difficulty of proving [https://www.quantamagazine.org/rational-or-not-this-basic-math-question-took-decades-to-answer-20250108/] that numbers are irrational. In 2019, Kevin Hartnett wrote about how to approximate π with fractions [https://www.quantamagazine.org/new-proof-settles-how-to-approximate-numbers-like-pi-20190814/]; Patrick Honner followed up with an exposition of how to approximate the square root of 2 [https://www.quantamagazine.org/how-rational-math-catches-slippery-irrational-numbers-20200310/]. In 2023, David Richeson wrote about Liouville numbers [https://www.quantamagazine.org/recounting-the-history-of-maths-transcendental-numbers-20230627/], irrational numbers that can be approximated very well by fractions.

 In Caltech ‘Mathathon’ saga, AI early adopters and critics find common ground 

 [https://www.quantamagazine.org/update/168181/]

 October 22, 2026 [/] 

One day in August, Caiman Moreno-Earle, a 19-year-old sophomore at the California Institute of Technology, had an idea. As Brian Zhao, a fellow student, remembers it, Moreno-Earle asked, “What if we give ourselves AI and lock ourselves in a basement for 40 hours and try and solve some open problems?” Almost on a lark, the pair, together with a few other students, asked Anthropic for $1 million in computing credits, expecting the tech company to come back with a counteroffer of something like $50,000. But Anthropic surprised them by agreeing to their original ask. Shortly afterward, OpenAI pledged a matching sum. A slew of other startups and tech companies also pledged support.

Later that month, the students launched a spare website [https://web.archive.org/web/20260831090246/https:/mathathonchallenge.com/] devoted to what they decided to call the “Caltech Mathathon.” They planned for 100 teams to compete to “solve open conjectures and build new mathematical theories” in “the first hackathon ever devoted to research level mathematics.” In their vision, students would use AI to speed up the process of coming up with new ideas and bringing them to maturity.

Almost immediately, they ran into opposition. An open letter criticizing the event [https://docs.google.com/document/d/1IL0b2oG2KvvSnxn_DuXsNxuHaCefDJ9hNY1k9BP3kKQ/edit?tab=t.0], drafted by Tasmin Chu [https://publish.obsidian.md/tasmin-chu/Tasmin+Chu], a Caltech graduate student, quickly attracted several thousand signatures. “This event is likely to have destructive impacts for the mathematical community,” the letter read.

The signatories worried that “AI companies will take credit for the effort of talented undergrads,” and that the Mathathon would lead to a slew of mathematical slop. They called on the organizers to suspend the event.

Then something surprising happened. Sergei Gukov [https://www.pma.caltech.edu/people/gukov], a professor at Caltech, and Terence Tao [https://www.math.ucla.edu/~tao/] of UCLA, who has become an éminence grise [https://www.nytimes.com/2026/10/16/us/math-ai-terence-tao.html] at the junction of AI and mathematics, got the organizers of the Mathathon (mostly undergraduates) and some of the signatories on the open letter (largely graduate students) to start talking to each other. “Everybody involved wants to move mathematics forward, so actually there was less disagreement between them than might initially have been reported,” Tao said in an email.

“We realized you didn’t have to solve an open problem to push mathematics forward,” said Zhao. And so, at the suggestion of Mayla Ward [https://mathematics.caltech.edu/people/mayla-ward], a Caltech graduate student who had signed the open letter, they began thinking about how to refocus the event on finding new proofs for old results.

As the two sides were beginning their discussions, OpenAI abruptly dropped its sponsorship [https://x.com/danintheory/status/2098125701782372640?s=20]. Shortly thereafter, Anthropic and the Mathathon organizers mutually decided to part ways. The Society for Artificial Intelligence Research [https://sair.foundation/], a nonprofit led by Tao, and XTX Markets, an algorithmic trading firm, stepped in to provide logistical support and funding. By late September, the two sides issued a joint statement [https://terrytao.wordpress.com/2026/09/28/joint-statement-about-mathathon/] on Tao’s blog announcing the new format. They plan to publicly release a list of problems, give participants 40 hours to work on them “hackathon style,” and then have some of the teams spend the next two months revising and writing up their results with mentors.

Zhao has begun compiling a list of some [https://link.springer.com/article/10.1023/A:1004824203467] existing [https://projecteuclid.org/journals/pacific-journal-of-mathematics/volume-13/issue-3] proofs that are long and hard to understand. (One, at more than 250 pages, takes up the entire Fall 1963 issue of the Pacific Journal of Mathematics.) “We want to heal the community through this event instead of dividing it,” he said. “It’s clear there is a big divide: Two thousand mathematicians signed the open letter against the Mathathon; on the other hand, 2,000 applied to be participants and judges.”

The revamped event [https://mathathonchallenge.com/] will be held in mid-November.
