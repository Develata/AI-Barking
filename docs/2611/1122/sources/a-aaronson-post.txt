The stochastic parrots have apparently proved the Riemann Hypothesis [https://scottaaronson.blog/?p=10344]

“… it is very probable that all roots are real. Of course one would wish for a rigorous proof here; I have for the time being, after some fleeting vain attempts, provisionally put aside the search for this, as it appears dispensable for the immediate objective of my investigation.” –Bernhard Riemann, 1859

[Update: According to “Poe’s Law,” there is no irony or sarcasm on the Internet so obvious that it won’t fly over countless people’s heads. In line with that, lots of people on social media are now saying variants of “how dare Scott Aaronson call LLMs ‘stochastic parrots,’ that demeaning and trivializing term from 2021? Doesn’t he understand that these so-called ‘stochastic parrots’ just proved the Riemann Hypothesis?” ROFL.]

I’m in California to visit Oratomic [https://www.oratomic.com/] and then Inkhaven [https://www.inkhaven.blog/fall-26]. I woke up this morning to the news that Levent Alpöge says that he’s gotten Anthropic models to prove the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis [https://alpo.ge/remark.pdf], producing 17.4 million (!!) lines of Lean code [https://github.com/CocoSgt/riemann-hypothesis-lean].

At a dinner last night, someone asked me “so, how much longer until an AI proves the Riemann Hypothesis? Still several more years?” I replied, don’t be sure of such things anymore. After all, AI has already proved that the nontrivial zeros of the Riemann zeta function are at most 3/8 away from the critical line [https://www.quantamagazine.org/ai-proved-the-quasi-riemann-hypothesis-what-happens-now-20261019/] (Quanta just published its article on that breakthrough yesterday!), and that already carries most of the implications for number theory that a full proof of RH would have. So who the hell knows what else is possible, and how quickly?

For those tuning in from home, the Riemann Hypothesis [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemann_hypothesis] says (in its easiest-to-understand version) that the number of prime numbers from 1 to n is very close to what it would be if each integer x>1 had an independent 1/ln(x) probability of being prime, up to a standard statistical error term that never exceeds Õ(√n). While we’ve known since 1896 that the number of primes from 1 to n is asymptotic to n/ln(n) (that’s the famous Prime Number Theorem [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_number_theorem]), you could’ve imagined the deviations being much larger.

David Hilbert famously said, “If I were to awaken after having slept for a thousand years, my first question would be: Has the Riemann Hypothesis been proven?” Meanwhile G. H. Hardy, on a ship that he feared might sink, sent his colleagues a telegram claiming to have proven RH, so that if the worst came to pass, at least the world might believe that a proof sank with him.

Many will of course question whether the proof announced today will stand. They’ll point out that, despite how heavily the world is now “lean-ing” on it, the Lean formal verification system still has issues, from kernel bugs (which have, in the past, allowed proofs of 0=1) to uncertain soundness of the underlying type theory. In my view, these issues can and should urgently be fixed! Until they are, a sufficiently motivated AI could almost certainly exploit them to prove a false statement!

[Update (Nov 21): The proof has now been verified independently by the Lean community, using con-leche, a piece of Lean that itself is formally verified all the way down to the axioms of set theory. This is a much higher degree of verification than a typical human paper accepted to a math journal. The proof is correct.]

All the same, I see zero indications that that’s happening right now. I note that, so far, not one of the AI results with Lean certificates has needed to be retracted (while several without Lean certificates have needed to be). So I’m not 100% sure, but my bet is that the proof will stand.

Mathematicians of course won’t be satisfied with 17 million lines of Lean. They’ll be satisfied when, and only when, they’re able to explain and distill and reconstruct the ideas for themselves. And that will probably take years, since human mathematicians move only at biological speeds. And while they’re working on it, how many more such wonders will there be, in this age of wonders and terrors?

My 9-year-old son, informed that RH was proved, replied that he’s not even surprised at this point. Maybe you need to have come of age in the human math era to be surprised by such things.

The P=?NP problem now moves firmly into place as the Final Boss of the Math We’ve Cared About, the position I always thought it should occupy. But for how much longer? For how much longer will anything last that we currently take for granted?

It was only a couple days ago that my wife and I had the following conversation:

Dana: I turned off the irrigation, yet somehow water in our house is still leaking. We need to…

Me: Look, a lot of the smartest people I know now think that recursive self-improvement and the end of the entire known world are coming in 12-18 months. This leak…

Dana: We should still fix the leak.

 Comment #35 November 20th, 2026 at 6:12 pm [#comment-2044011] 

Youlian #11 asks what we know about the human proof. A few concrete things, read off the manuscript itself.

The method is not a scaled-down version of the AI one. It shares almost nothing. Fable’s summary of the Claude route goes through automorphy lifting: Galois cohomology growth bounds, Galois representations attached to regular algebraic cuspidal automorphic representations of GL_n over CM fields, theta lifts between unitary groups, plus arithmetic Hilbert-Samuel and Moriwaki’s bigness theorem. Search the 940 pages for those and you find: “Eichler-Shimura” 0 times, “CM field” 0, “Hilbert-Samuel” 0, “Moriwaki” 0.

What Masullo does instead is Weil’s explicit formula and the trace formula. Those words appear 155 and 203 times. The theta that shows up 435 times is adelic theta functions and Schwartz-Bergman spaces, not theta lifts. He widens the zero-free region with Deligne’s tensor-power trick. “Amplification” appears zero times, which is notable because that is the standard tool in this area and its absence is either a real idea or the problem.

Two facts that argue against a quick dismissal. He is not unrecorded: there is arXiv 2011.11182, math.AG 2020, “Crystalline cohomology over general bases,” under A. M. Masullo. Real mathematics, but algebraic geometry, not analytic number theory, and no journal version I can find. And the acknowledgements say the work began in his first year of grad school at Stanford under Brian Conrad and took nearly 12 years, so the Conrad connection is the stated origin, not a rumor travelling through a blog post.

What argues for caution is that the draft is demonstrably unfinished. There are 27 footnotes that say only “Return here” – his own markers for loose ends – spread across pages 199 to 483. Two more specific ones read “complete proof” and “after the Proposition is updated.” Chapter 13, which is supposed to deduce GRH from the trace formula, is two pages and states no theorem. Ryan McCorvie started a Lean formalization today and his errata file already lists eleven statements that are false as written, all in the appendices’ recalls of standard material.

So the honest answer to Youlian is: the author is a working algebraic geometer with one arXiv paper, the approach is genuinely independent of the Claude one, and the manuscript is a working draft rather than a finished proof. Whether the trace formula step holds is exactly the open question.

Disclosure: I run science.markets, which has a market on whether this proof holds. I say so because it is my reason for having read the thing closely.

Scott #70:

I realize the proof is now Lean verified, and not subject to fudging experimental data like the infamous 90s physics frauds. Still there’s something about the model of discovery that leaves me cautious.

Like you, I hope effort is put into breaking these proofs down into decipherable chunks. It’s useful for human brains and future models applying/extending results. 

Right now it feels a bit like we asked for a design for a bridge and were given a document specifying every single atom’s position without being given an underlying theory of mechanics. Yes, it solves the problem but is dissatisfying. 

Perhaps because I think of a proof as a way for understanding and verifying, and not just a brute fact. And 250k pages (!) lands more in that category than as a useful advance others can build on.

Looking toward the future of AI science, it seems to me like AI models will still be subject to the need for experimental data, which becomes the rate limiting step on theory development. (Thinking more on cosmology or high energy physics there). But potentially amazing at mesoscopic phenomena or purely mathematical if we can get digested results out.

I wasn’t going to “defend” Le Cun or anything in particular he might have said or not have said – obviously he doesn’t need that, certainly not from me.

As a reminder: knowing “just” BB(95) would have told us whether RH holds and knowing “just” BB(215) would still tell us whether NP=P or not. So nobody in their right mind would call these problems “unsolvable”. (Never mind that these values of BB are beyond the physically feasible…) They just didn’t seem to be within reach, and indeed settling BSD or the Hodge conjecture seemed much closer within reach – because for both there is a positive chance that they might admit a counterexample in the precise formulation in which they were posed as Millennium problems, albeit a pathological one, much as it turned out for Navier Stokes. So they might have seemed to be in the range of problems where the known advantages of AI were “obvious” or at least less surprising. Not for the Riemann Hypothesis. But again, Alpöge didn’t prompt “please solve RH” either. He himself contributed, by asking for well chosen solutions to sub-problems and hypotheses along the path.

My real remark is however: the point isn’t that test-time compute “enhances” performance. My point is that you need (!) it because pure LLM gets lost in hallucination. The coupling to Lean (or to a human co-worker in the loop or both) is what allows you to tame that source of trouble.

It is what currently allows you to do that. Of course it’s possible that some future AI development gets to a place where you don’t need Lean for that. But unless and until that happens, having a human in the driver’s seat (or being the head of that Centaur) is the model. (Or if you wish: call the human the feet of the Centaur if that suits you better – the point is you need both. It’s team work.)

Sure, for Chess and Go it was different. We now know that permanently, the human in the driver’s seat is “useless” for improving at Chess or Go. But Chess and Go are finite games, with finite state spaces. It’s not helpful to take that as evidence. (You would need ‘asymptotic chess’ or ‘asymptotic Go’ at the very least to make the analogy work. But basically no human plays those games. So it’s moot as well.)

For the moment, we have Lean or a human. Not pure LLM plays. For math, we know that a major reason why test-time compute is needed is simply that you need to build that “Lean cage” – which of course isn’t part of the LLM proper, although an end user may not “see” that difference. For the compute and the training, the difference is real. Hence for the overall performance measures, once you’re being honest.
