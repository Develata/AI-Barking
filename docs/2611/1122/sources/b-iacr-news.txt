 LARK: Smaller and Faster Lattice-Based PRG

 [https://eprint.iacr.org/2026/3000]

 Yu Zhang, Jingnan He, Yijian Liu, Xianhui Lu, Dongshu Cai, Kunpeng Wang

 ePrint Report

 A key challenge in designing practical lattice-based pseudorandom number generators (PRGs) is that such generators typically produce byte-oriented output, whereas lattice parameters do not necessarily align with byte boundaries, creating a tradeoff between fully utilizing each computed value and maintaining efficient byte-oriented generation. To avoid additional packing overhead, LEAP (FSE 2025), a state-of-the-art lattice-based PRG built on Ring Learning with Rounding (RLWR), retains only the lowest eight bits of each RLWR value for byte-oriented generation and discards the remaining high-order bits. Consequently, this high-order information contributes neither to LEAP's output nor its security. We present LARK, a lattice-based pseudorandom generator that directly exposes the low-order byte as pseudorandom output and uses the discarded high-order information as security-relevant hidden uncertainty. This additional uncertainty allows LARK to meet its target security levels with smaller parameters, yielding compact keys and efficient implementations.

To justify using the discarded high-order information as hidden uncertainty, LARK replaces LEAP’s RLWR-based output-generation component with one based on Ring Learning with Alternating Moduli (RLAM). For efficient polynomial arithmetic, we formulate RLAM as a ring analogue of the LAM problem introduced by Chen et al. (CRYPTO 2026), in which LWR rounding is replaced by direct reduction between coprime moduli. This modulus reduction introduces statistical bias, which LARK removes with lightweight masked rejection. We prove that LARK is pseudorandom under the decisional NTRU and RLAM assumptions. Compared with LEAP, LARK reduces key sizes by $42.85\%$ and $68.00\%$ at the $128$-bit and $256$-bit security levels, respectively, while consistently improving performance. LARK improves performance by up to $37.16\%$ in plain C, $10.55\%$ with AVX2, and $21.05\%$ with AVX512. In particular, the AVX512 implementations achieve $0.90$ and $0.91$ cycles per byte at the two security levels, attaining sub-1 cycle/byte performance at both levels.

 Evaluating CNF/SMT Encodings for SAT-Based Differential Cryptanalysis of Lightweight Block Ciphers

 [https://eprint.iacr.org/2026/2999]

 Marzieh Vahid Dastjerdi, Majid Rahimi, Iman Mirzaali Mazandarani, Sadegh Sadeghi

 ePrint Report

 This study evaluates three encoding methods for automated differential cryptanalysis: (1) SMT formulations (using CVC), (2) standard CNF, and (3) size-optimized CNF (via Logic Friday). We assess these using four SAT/SMT solver types: single-core (CryptoMiniSat-v5, CaDiCaL), multi-core (Treengeling), and massively parallel Mallob—novel to cryptanalysis. Encoding-solver combinations are tested on seven lightweight block ciphers representing distinct design philosophies: SPECK-32 and
CHAM-64 (ARX structure), SIMON-32 (AND-RX structure), PRESENT, GIFT-128, and MIDORI-64 (4-bit S-box in SPN structure), and LBLOCK (Feistel structure). For each cipher, SAT/SMT instances targeting specific rounds/differential weights were generated, with wall-clock solving time, parallel efficiency, and modeling effort measured.
Our results establish criteria for optimal encoding-solver pairings that strike a balance between modeling simplicity and computational performance. Crucially, Mallob emerges as the state-of-the-art framework for large-scale automated differential cryptanalysis.

 Differential Collision Resistance of Feed-Forward Sponge Variants

 [https://eprint.iacr.org/2026/2998]

 Ruixi Lv, Guozhen Liu, Shun Li, Siwei Sun

 ePrint Report

 Feed-forward sponge variants have recently been proposed in independent lines of work to overcome the capacity bound limitation of the sponge construction and to strengthen provable (second-)preimage security. Representative examples include Sponge-DM, Sponge-DM$^{c}$, Sponge-EDM$^{c}$, and Sponge-F$^{P,pd}$.
While these designs improve generic security guarantees, their behavior under dedicated cryptanalysis remains largely unexplored.
Focusing on differential collision attacks, we review the inner and outer collision frameworks for sponge hashes and analyze how the feed-forward operation, the rate-capacity ratio, and the squeezing phase affect collision cryptanalysis. Our analysis shows that feed-forward sponge variants do not necessarily offer stronger collision resistance than the standard sponge, and that the modified collision conditions and the enlarged rate can even facilitate attacks. We substantiate this analysis on feed-forward sponge instantiations with Keccak-p that are configured to match the generic collision security levels of SHA3-224, SHA3-256, SHA3-384, and SHA3-512. For these instantiations, we develop classical and quantum collision attacks. Compared with the best known attacks on the corresponding SHA-3 instances, our attacks reach more rounds or lower complexity in several cases. These results demonstrate that, for the reduced-round instantiations considered in this work, the structural changes introduced by feed-forward sponges do not carry over to collision resistance against dedicated classical and quantum attacks. They also indicate that strong collision resistance still relies on a careful choice of the feed-forward sponge configuration and on the differential propagation of the underlying permutation.

 One Type Fits All: A General Analysis of Representation Trees for Subset Sum, LWE and Decoding

 [https://eprint.iacr.org/2026/2997]

 Andre Esser

 ePrint Report

 The representation technique is the basis of the best known algorithms for random subset sum, of combinatorial attacks on small LWE keys and of information set decoding. It builds the solution in a search tree, the representation tree, whose analysis has so far required a combinatorial derivation by hand for every variant.

We introduce a general analysis technique for any representation tree, which replaces this derivation by a few short entropy formulas and turns the whole analysis into one explicit optimization problem, so that a new variant needs no new analysis. This technique alone improves the asymptotic exponents for almost all applications we consider. We then extend this analysis by adding filters, known from nearest-neighbor search, to every level of the best known trees and optimize them jointly with all other parameters.

Finally, we improve the filtering technique by partitioning the enumerated vectors into a sparse and a dense part, which lets the filters act in a more concentrated way, with significant gains especially for subset sum and LWE. Further, we reduce the number of optimization variables for the filters from quadratic to linear, which makes optimizations feasible that were previously out of reach.

Heuristically, random subset sum is solved in time $T=2^{0.2620n}$ and memory $M=2^{0.2120n}$ instead of $T=M=2^{0.2830n}$, and LWE keys following the distribution used in ML-DSA are recovered in time $2^{0.2954n}$ instead of $2^{0.378n}$. We also obtain the fastest combinatorial algorithms for LWE keys as in Kyber and Dilithium and the fastest algorithms for decoding over $\mathbb{F}_q$ for small $q\geq3$, ternary decoding with large weight, decoding with restricted errors and Lee metric decoding. Further, we improve on the best known algorithms for nearest-neighbor search over $\mathbb{F}_q$ and on the best known time-memory trade-offs for subset sum.

 Real Brakedown Performance: A Portable Scalar Baseline for Spielman-Code Polynomial Commitments

 [https://eprint.iacr.org/2026/2996]

 Jipeng Zhang

 ePrint Report

 Brakedown-style polynomial commitment schemes encode the rows of a coefficient matrix with a linear-time Spielman code and Merkle-commit the columns. New schemes are typically compared against the unoptimized LCPC and PolyCommit libraries. Conversely, the reported speedups of the AVX-512 encoder SIMD-Spielman over LCPC combine vector arithmetic with portable algorithmic and memory optimizations. We present RBP-LCPC, an optimized implementation of LCPC in portable stable Rust that integrates column-major batched encoding, right-to-left graph evaluation, lazy modular reduction and cache-aware slicing. Its incremental structure isolates the contribution of each optimization. On both x86-64 and AArch64, its compiled hot kernels contain no vector arithmetic. With LCPC's code and prime, its encodings, Merkle roots and proofs match those of LCPC bit-for-bit. On an AMD Zen 5 server with one thread and N = 2^16 to 2^28 coefficients, RBP-LCPC encodes 8.14-9.06x faster than LCPC under its default build configuration. SIMD-Spielman, measured on the same server, remains 1.59-4.44x faster up to N = 2^26. Commit achieves a smaller speedup of 5.21-5.95x over the default LCPC configuration (4.48-5.95x across both default and tuned configurations), because column hashing then accounts for over a third of single-threaded commit time. Prove and verify are 1.83-7.61x and 2.65-5.36x faster than under the default LCPC configuration across thread counts and sizes, and single-threaded PCS setup is 1.31-1.44x faster. The accompanying artifact reproduces all baselines and measurements from fixed source revisions.

 Improved GGM Witness-Encryption Bypassing Rank Gap

 [https://eprint.iacr.org/2026/2995]

 Charanjit S. Jutla

 ePrint Report

 Hair and Sahai construct witness encryption in the classical
prime-order generic-group model using a designed matrix space with a growing rank gap. We retain their encryption mechanism while replacing the minimum-rank requirement with a lower bound on the min-entropy of $Mr$ for nonzero matrices $M$ that do not permit witness extraction, where $r$ is the short random vector used in encryption. A matrix with substantial concentration has a column factorization from a finite family of short rational patterns. One list of linear forms evaluated at powers of a public random seed preserves all these patterns simultaneously, except with exponentially small probability, and therefore suffices in place of their multiple folded lists.

For a quadratic verifier encoding with $N$ Boolean variables, using $t=\lceil\log N\rceil$ forms gives one-bit ciphertexts of
$\widetilde O(N^5)$ bits and optimized decryption using $\widetilde O(N^4)$ group queries. For sufficiently large $N$, distinguishing advantage on false instances is at most $N^{-\log N}$ against classical generic adversaries making at most $N^{\log N}$ group queries. Correctness error is $2^{-\Omega(N)}$, simultaneously for all valid witnesses of a ciphertext. At these same security and correctness guarantees, a beneficial reparameterization of Hair--Sahai's deterministic construction gives ciphertexts of $\widetilde O(N^6)$ bits and, with the same decryption optimizations, $\widetilde O(N^5)$ group queries.

 Decoding Cost Lens: Cost Estimation for Code-Based Cryptography

 [https://eprint.iacr.org/2026/2994]

 Tianrui Wang, Anyu Wang, Yuxuan Li, Qicheng Teng, Bo Pang

 ePrint Report

 We present Decoding Cost Lens, a platform for evaluating the concrete decoding costs of code-based cryptographic schemes. It maps key- and message-recovery objectives to explicit attack instances, integrates classical Hamming- and rank-metric estimators, and provides quantum estimates for binary Hamming instances. The evaluation accounts for known block structure and applicable multiple-target effects under stated cost conventions. We apply the platform to 64 parameter sets from 15 first-round candidate schemes in the NGCC call for next-generation commercial cryptographic algorithms, comparing estimated work factors with the specifications' claims and security targets. Four schemes have classical decoding estimates below both the corresponding declared cost and the target for at least one parameter set. Zero-direction maximal minors (ZD-MM) replaces one guessed zero with a search over directions in a single block. For BAG-Loong, the lowest ZD-MM costs are $113.1$, $212.3$, $299.1$ and $386.7$ bits in increasing parameter order. ZD-MM also puts key recovery below target for BAG-Piglet-128
and -256 ($114.6$ and $254.0$ bits), and for all three BRA sets ($112.1$, $161.8$ and $316.8$ bits) after block transposition. For NSS-HQC, key and baseline message decoding costs fall below the $256$-, $384$-, and $512$-bit classical targets; the corresponding baseline message costs are $226.0$, $332.5$, and $440.9$ bits. NSS-HQC-128 also falls below its declared key-recovery cost while remaining above its target. The estimated quantum baseline message cost for NSS-HQC-512 is $251.6$ bits against a $256$-bit target.

 PCC: Proof-Carrying Credentials for Enhanced Anonymity and Post-Quantum Delegation

 [https://eprint.iacr.org/2026/2992]

 Zixuan Wang, ZHENG Tianyu, Yuan Lu, Bin Xiao

 ePrint Report

 Delegatable Anonymous Credentials (DACs) let an authority be delegated through a chain of intermediaries and later exercised anonymously, as in DeFi credit delegation.
Because presentation is the operation a holder repeats at every use, its cost is what matters in practice; yet existing DACs build this presentation from the delegation chain itself.
We call this limitation prover awareness: to present, a holder must possess and process delegation-lineage material whose shape mirrors the chain.
It has two faces: its size makes the presentation cost grow with the delegation depth, and its content exposes the holder to delegation-topology metadata that, in incentive-driven and compliance-aware settings, enables lineage-based policy vetoes, strategic disintermediation, and de facto censorship in otherwise permissionless networks.

We formalize the privacy face as Enhanced Delegation Anonymity (EDA), a leakage-parametrized two-observer notion combining verifier-side anonymity with holder-side topology obliviousness, the latter given both as a simulation definition and as an indistinguishability game over paths with identical allowed holder leakage.
Because both faces share one cause, the chain-structured credential, a single change of representation removes them together: our framework Proof-Carrying Credentials (PCC) replaces the explicit chain with a single recursively updated proof-carrying state via Non-Uniform Incrementally Verifiable Computation (NIVC), making the presentation independent of the delegation depth and hiding the topology at once, while preserving restricted delegation and expressive presentation.
Unlike prior constant-size DACs tied to bilinear pairings, PCC is hardness-agnostic and admits an explicit post-quantum-compatible instantiation in the QROM from transparent FRI-based recursion and hash-based signatures.

On consumer-grade hardware, the Halo2 (non-post-quantum) instantiation keeps the verifier-facing proof constant at 7.344KB with 35-36ms verification across all tested depths, and a 128-bit Plonky2 (post-quantum) instantiation keeps a constant 157.9KB proof and 3.8ms verification across depths $L\in\{1,2,4,8,16\}$, at 1.03s per delegation step.
Holder-side topology hiding and depth-independent presentation are thus achievable at practical recursive-proving cost.

 A Practical Collision for 36-Step SHA-512

 [https://eprint.iacr.org/2026/2991]

 Junhong Huang

 ePrint Report

 We give a colliding message pair for SHA-512 reduced to 36 steps, starting from the standard initial value. To our knowledge it is the first practical collision for 36-step SHA-512; the previous practical record was 35 steps. The attack is not new.  High Throughput Meets Low Latency: CKKS Functional Bootstrapping on GPUs

 [https://eprint.iacr.org/2026/2989]

 Carlos Agulló-Domingo, Carlo Pascoe, Óscar Vera-López, Andreea Alexandru, Ahmad Al Badawi, José L. Abellán, Yuriy Polyakov

 ePrint Report

 Functional (programmable) bootstrapping extends the bootstrapping operation of Fully Homomorphic Encryption (FHE) to evaluate an arbitrary function while refreshing a ciphertext. This capability was a unique feature of scalar CGGI/DM cryptosystems for many years until it was recently added to the toolset of vectorized FHE schemes, with CKKS being the most promising option both in terms of throughput (amortized time) and functionality. Previous work showed that CKKS functional bootstrapping outperforms CGGI/DM functional bootstrapping in terms of throughput by two to three orders of magnitude, but still loses in latency on CPU by two to three orders of magnitude. We present an optimized GPU implementation of CKKS functional bootstrapping that closes the latency gap. Our implementation supports evaluating functions over both small integers (as in CGGI/DM) and real numbers (unique to CKKS), which we call discrete-CKKS and continuous-CKKS functional bootstrapping, respectively.

Our GPU implementation evaluates discrete-CKKS functional bootstrapping over a ciphertext with 64 encrypted Boolean values in 2.30 ms and with 16,384 encrypted Boolean values in 5.24 ms on RTX 4090, a consumer-grade GPU, whereas the CGGI programmable bootstrapping of one encrypted bit on the same GPU takes 2.16 ms. For 6-bit lookup tables, our discrete-CKKS functional bootstrapping takes 31.4 ms for 32,768 integers vs. 15.7 ms for one integer using CGGI. On RTX PRO 6000, our continuous-CKKS functional bootstrapping is 3.5x to 7.4x faster than the best prior GPU implementation on the same GPU, while bootstrapping four to eight times as many values. Our continuous-CKKS functional bootstrapping of the identity function, which corresponds to regular CKKS bootstrapping, takes 4.82 ms for 16,384 slots on RTX PRO 6000, the first reported bootstrapping latency below 5 ms for a CKKS ciphertext with this many slots. We were able to achieve such low latencies via a combination of algorithmic, architectural, and implementation optimizations, many of which were applied to both FIDESlib and OpenFHE. Our continuous-CKKS functional bootstrapping also scales to larger ring dimensions, reaching 28.5 bits of precision with 23 levels remaining at a ring dimension of $2^{17}$.
