Unforced Euler singularity, unforced Navier-Stokes singularity, and the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis [https://terrytao.wordpress.com/2026/11/20/unforced-euler-singularity-unforced-navier-stokes-singularity-and-the-generalized-riemann-hypothesis/]

20 November, 2026 in math.AP [https://terrytao.wordpress.com/category/mathematics/mathap/], math.NT [https://terrytao.wordpress.com/category/mathematics/mathnt/] | Tags: AI [https://terrytao.wordpress.com/tag/ai/], incompressible Euler equations [https://terrytao.wordpress.com/tag/incompressible-euler-equations/], Levent Alpöge [https://terrytao.wordpress.com/tag/levent-alpoge/], Matei Coiculescu [https://terrytao.wordpress.com/tag/matei-coiculescu/], Navier-Stokes equations [https://terrytao.wordpress.com/tag/navier-stokes-equations/], Riemann hypothesis [https://terrytao.wordpress.com/tag/riemann-hypothesis/], Tristan Buckmaster [https://terrytao.wordpress.com/tag/tristan-buckmaster/] | by Terence Tao [https://terrytao.wordpress.com/author/teorth/] | 127 comments [https://terrytao.wordpress.com/2026/11/20/unforced-euler-singularity-unforced-navier-stokes-singularity-and-the-generalized-riemann-hypothesis/#comments] 

Obviously these results will be reported widely in other outlets, but I will note them here, in decreasing order of how “digested” the results currently are:

Levent Alpöge, Tristan Buckmaster, and Matei Coiculescu, with heavy AI assistance, have written a human-readable proof of the finite time blowup for the unforced incompressible Euler equations in three dimensions [https://cims.nyu.edu/~tristanb/cc.pdf], which is formalized in Lean here [https://github.com/mcoiculescu/EulerBlowupPublic].

Levent Alpöge and Tristan Buckmaster, again with heavy AI usage, have generated a proof of the finite time blowup for the unforced incompressible Navier Stokes equations in three dimensions, again with a Lean formalization [https://github.com/tristanbuckmaster/navier-stokes/]. Here, the paper is technically human-readable and the general strategy of proof is broadly clear (following one of the expected paths to this result, namely to numerically build an approximate discretely self-similar solution and then use rigorous perturbation theory to convert this to an exact solution); but more work needs to be done to create a proper, professionally written proof.

Levent Alpöge, using an internal AI model, has generated an (enormous) Lean-only proof of the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis. This Lean Zulip thread [https://leanprover.zulipchat.com/#narrow/channel/113488-general/topic/Discussion.3A.20Alp.C3.B6ge.20Proves.20the.20Riemann.20Hypothesis] is probably the best place to follow the efforts to verify the proof and extract the strategy of proof (which, from very initial analysis, appears to use the full power of modern developments on the Langlands program and arithmetic geometry). (UPDATE: the moderators of the Lean thread are now focusing the discussion on the Lean-related aspects of the proof only.) I imagine the digestion process will take some time, though in this case at least we have a human author to communicate with to assist with this task.

See also Buckmaster’s Mastodon post [https://mastodon.social/@tristanbuckmaster/117649035783718539] regarding the first two results.

[This is a guest post by the MathHelm scientific committee, whose membership is listed at the bottom of this post. -T.]

Mathematics depends on our regular conversations with colleagues and with the wider public. They help us share ideas, deepen our collective understanding, see where the field stands, and decide what to explore next. They also keep us connected. As technology changes rapidly, talking through uncertainty and supporting one another matter for our well-being and our shared effort to understand the universe.

On October 6, 2026, OpenAI released 722 mathematical manuscripts generated by an internal AI model, claiming significant results across many research areas. This release will undoubtedly bring significant changes to the mathematical community, even though most of these papers were not verified by us humans and among them, many results did not have a companion lean-verification.

Like many others, I was interested in several of these papers. I also found the whole collection intimidating. Their presentations often differ from the scholarly conventions I knew, and I could not quickly assess most of the arguments on my own. It was natural that I wanted to discuss with colleagues, but often felt hesitant to speak before I felt confident in my judgment. Sometimes I wasn’t even sure whom to discuss with. A few colleagues felt the same way. Together, we created MathHelm [https://mathhelm.org/]: a place for everyone to share comments, ask questions, and think together on massive AI-generated papers, including these 722 papers and any other similar paper.

Each of us only has the expertise to assess a small part of such a broad collection. Useful discussions were already appearing online: explanations of an idea, questions about a proof, or missed connections to earlier or ongoing work and related concerns. People’s comments are scattered and easy to miss. Bringing them together saves time for everyone and gives the community a clearer picture.

MathHelm is open to researchers worldwide, with discussions primarily in English. We invite mathematical researchers to register under their real names and discuss AI-generated papers and related mathematical questions. Comments can address correctness, readability, originality, main results, or key ideas. We hope everyone could be as objective and specific as possible, and include references where appropriate. You can also upload your own PDF notes.

The forum was founded by the scientific committee listed below and operates on a nonprofit basis to serve the mathematical community. It focuses on mathematical content and takes no predetermined position for or against AI. Our policy prohibits any organization from using forum content to train AI models.

Our guiding principle is simple:

Mathematicians have no obligation to check AI-generated papers. But if you happen to read part of one, your observations may be valuable to the whole community.

Even small comments such as a question about the proof method, an explanation of a single step, or a useful reference can help. You do not need a verdict on an entire paper to join the conversation.

Whatever tools we use, mathematics should remain a shared human pursuit: people should be able to understand its ideas, question its claims, and help shape its future. We hope MathHelm can offer a little steadiness in this age of rapid change, and a place to keep learning from one another.

Lean Grimoire [https://terrytao.wordpress.com/2026/11/11/lean-grimoire/]

11 November, 2026 in diversions [https://terrytao.wordpress.com/category/non-technical/diversions-non-technical/], math.LO [https://terrytao.wordpress.com/category/mathematics/mathlo/] | Tags: applets [https://terrytao.wordpress.com/tag/applets/], Lean [https://terrytao.wordpress.com/tag/lean/] | by Terence Tao [https://terrytao.wordpress.com/author/teorth/] | 83 comments [https://terrytao.wordpress.com/2026/11/11/lean-grimoire/#comments] 

Back in July, I started experimenting [https://terrytao.wordpress.com/tag/applets/] with using modern coding agents to build a number of mathematical visualization applets. With the extremely hectic events of the last few months, I had to set those projects aside; but I recently found a bit of time to work on one such project that I had started back then, which was to modernize a Javascript interactive textbook I had made back in 2018 [https://teorth.github.io/QED/], entitled “QED”, and announced previously on this blog [https://terrytao.wordpress.com/2018/08/18/qed-version-2-0-an-interactive-text-in-first-order-logic/]. The purpose of that textbook was to teach propositional and first-order logic in the form of a “crafting game”, where one applied various rules of logic to reach a desired conclusion from a given set of hypotheses.

With a coding agent, I rebuilt the game [https://teorth.github.io/tao-web/apps/grimoire.html] (which I have given the working title of “Lean Grimoire”) with a number of new features, for instance giving the option to show an in-progress Lean proof of one’s game state. Just for fun, I also added an option to play in a “fantasy mode” in which various game elements are depicted using fantasy-themed flavor text (for instance, mathematical statements become “potions”, mathematical expressions become “scrolls”, and so forth). Here is a typical screenshot, in which one has nearly completed the proof of the assertion given :

 [https://terrytao.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/image.png]

I would be interested in having some playtesting of this app to make it more useful as a means to teach logical proofs and/or Lean.
