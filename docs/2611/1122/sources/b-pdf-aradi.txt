                    Integral Cryptanalysis of ARADI

                                                 Abstract
          ARADI is a 128-bit block, 256-bit key, 16-round substitution–permutation network pub-
      lished by the U.S. National Security Agency (Greene, Motley, and Weeks, IACR ePrint
      2024/1240) for low-latency memory encryption alongside LLAMA. The ARADI specification
      gives no round-by-round security bounds, relying on strict per-key data limits from frequent
      rekeying. In ToSC 2026 (Issue 3), Beyne, Neyt, and Sajjad proved via quasidifferential
      trails that high-round differential trails of ARADI collapse to weak-key subsets (2−38 at 12
      rounds and 2−39 at 14 rounds, invalidating prior 11–14-round single-key differential attacks).
      Meanwhile, prior all-key integral attacks (Dunkelman and Ghosh, ePrint 2025/1227) need
      2118 –2126.8 chosen plaintexts on 10–12 rounds or reach only 6 rounds (1,536 chosen plaintexts).
          We present OPTIC (Orthogonal Pullback and Ternary Integral Cryptanalysis), a three-
      module framework achieving all-key (100% of 2256 keys) master-key recovery on ARADI across
      5 to 12 rounds. Forward, pulling masks back onto the quadratic rows of ARADI’s Toffoli S-box
      across the four-column islands of its orthogonal fourth linear layer collapses two nonlinear
      rounds into one cubic layer. Backward, each weight-three involution turns subkey recovery into
      evaluating a ternary inverse-S-box tree, closing to the 256-bit master key in constant time from
      any even-starting subkey pair (Dunkelman and Ghosh). Instantiating OPTIC yields: (i) single-
      core recovery on 5 rounds (6 chosen plaintexts for the last subkey in 11 ms; 4 chosen plaintexts
      for the master key in 16.0 s) and 6 rounds (48 chosen plaintexts in 2.4 s via 198 certified equa-
      tions); (ii) a 216 -chosen-plaintext 6-round distinguisher with a 42-dimensional null space (261 ×
      below ePrint 2024/1772 and 266 × below ToSC 2026(3)), enabling single-core master-key recov-
      ery on 7 rounds (3,072 chosen plaintexts, 3.5 s) and 8 rounds (90,112 chosen plaintexts, 6.0 s,
      < 20 MB RAM), plus the first dedicated 9-round master-key attack (222.32 chosen plaintexts,
      254.77 Walsh–Hadamard butterflies, 4.7 MB); and (iii) 10–12-round all-key attacks improving
      data and time over ePrint 2025/1227, reaching (2126.81 , 2149.88 packed ops, 2123.49 bits) and
      (2126.58 , 2148.62 packed ops ≈ 2141 encryptions, 2153.81 bits) at 12 rounds. This trades higher
      data and memory than ToSC 2026(3) to cover all 2256 keys and run > 248 × faster than its
      non-heuristic 2197 attack.


1     Introduction
1.1    Memory Encryption and Threat Model
Protecting random-access memory (RAM) against physical probing, interposer snooping, and
cold-boot extraction [24] requires encrypting cache lines on the critical memory-bus path.
Because every additional nanosecond of encryption latency directly stalls processor load/store
pipelines, general-purpose ciphers such as AES are frequently replaced in memory controllers by
dedicated low-latency primitives, including PRINCE [12], PRINCEv2 [14], MANTIS [4], QARMA [3],
QARMAv2 [1], SCARF [15], and ChiLow [5].
    In August 2024 (revised September 2024), Patricia Greene, Mark Motley, and Bryan Weeks
of the U.S. National Security Agency published ARADI alongside its authenticated memory-
encryption mode LLAMA [23]. ARADI is an unrolled 16-round substitution–permutation network
(SPN) operating on a 128-bit state (four 32-bit words w, x, y, z) under a 256-bit master key.
It combines a bit-sliced 4-Toffoli-gate S-box π with four alternating involution linear layers


                                                      1
Λ0 , Λ1 , Λ2 , Λ3 of row weight 3 and an F2 -affine key schedule. Unlike open academic designs, the
19-page NSA specification [23] provides no differential, linear, algebraic, or integral security
bounds justifying its 16-round count. It notes only that the rotation offsets (aj , bj , cj ) “were
chosen by a limited search over the possible values” [23, Section 3.1, p. 5]. Instead, Section 2
of [23, p. 2] states the operational threat model explicitly.
      “we can reasonably enforce frequent re-keying (say once per day), and so we have a
      relatively small upper bound on the amount of data available to the attacker on a
      given key.”
This operational constraint creates a clear split in the cryptanalysis of ARADI. Theoretical
attacks requiring 2108 –2128 chosen plaintexts per key exceed physically realizable memory-bus
traffic under daily rekeying by more than twenty orders of magnitude. By contrast, ultra-low-data,
practical-time all-key recovery attacks directly probe the true security margin of ARADI under
its intended deployment assumptions.

1.2    Prior Cryptanalysis of ARADI
Since August 2024, ARADI and LLAMA have attracted sustained scrutiny across fifteen active
cryptanalytic works on IACR ePrint and in peer-reviewed venues. One early power side-channel
preprint, ePrint 2024/1987, was withdrawn in July 2025 and is excluded below.1 Table 1
summarizes all single-key cryptanalytic results on ARADI:

  • Differential Cryptanalysis and the Quasidifferential Weak-Key Barrier (ToSC
    2026(3) [10]): Automated trail searches by Bellini et al. [7] (INDOCRYPT 2024 ) proposed
    8–10-round differential/linear distinguishers and an 11-round differential attack. Mandal
    et al. [31] reported 11- and 12-round differential attacks (D = 2127 , T = 2234 and 2254 ).
    Ghosh, Michel, and Naya-Plasencia [22] proposed a 14-round differential-MITM attack
    (D = 2126 , T = 2248.57 ). However, in ToSC 2026(3), Beyne, Neyt, and Sajjad [10] applied
    quasidifferential trail analysis [11] to ARADI. They prove that all high-probability differential
    characteristics impose 26–39 independent linear constraints on the master key, outside which
    the fixed-key differential probability is identically zero:

    (a) Invalidation of Prior 11–14-Round Differential Attacks: Beyne et al. [10, Table 1, §3.2,
        §4.1] proved that the 9-round differential underlying [22] holds only for a weak-key class
        of size 2256−26 = 2230 , so its T = 2248.57 complexity exceeds exhaustive search (2230 )
        over the valid key space (ineffective). Meanwhile, both [7] and [31] omitted 4 required
        outer-round subkey columns (16 bits) during 2-round partial decryption, causing the
        right key to be discarded (invalid ).
    (b) Weak-Key 12- and 14-Round Differential Attacks in [10]: Constructing two new 9-round
        differentials (with fixed-key probabilities 2−107.678 for a 2−38 fraction of keys and 2−119
        for a 2−39 fraction of keys), Beyne et al. [10, §5.2–5.3, §6.1–6.2] presented a 12-round
        attack valid only for 2218 weak keys (2−38 of the key space) with primary complexity
        D = 2108.68 chosen plaintexts (CP), T = 2196.68 ≈ 2197 , M = 236 . A high-memory variant
        runs at D = 2111.38 CP, T = 2117 , M = 282.38 , relying on a binomial key-randomization
        heuristic with 63% success within the 2−38 weak-key class. Their 14-round attack is valid
        for 2217 weak keys (2−39 fraction) with D = 2120 CP, T = 2208 , M = 236 .

    Because algebraic degree bounds hold deterministically for all 2256 keys, integral/ZeroSum
    cryptanalysis [27] bypasses the weak-key barrier entirely. Bellini et al. [8] (ACNS 2025 ;
    reproduced by CLAASP-MP [9]) derived 4–8-round Toffoli degree distinguishers (D =
    24 , 216 , 284 , 2113 , 2124 CP) and a 10-round attack (D = 2124 CP, T = 2192 after correcting

    (D = 213 , 277 , 2112 , 2124 CP, where 7R uses a shifted starting round). Combining ZeroSum
    distinguishers with partial-sum Fast Walsh–Hadamard Transforms (FHT/WHT) [21, 16,
    33, 20], Dunkelman and Ghosh [19] achieved all-key 10–12-round master-key recovery

    impossible-differential attack (D = 2121.47 CP, full-key T ≈ 2255 [22]).

  • Low- and Medium-Data Key Recovery (5–7 Rounds): Vaziri [34] proposed a 5-round
    known-plaintext (KP) MITM attack (28 KP, T = 2234.8 ). At LATINCRYPT 2025, Bellini
    et al. [6] studied neural-assisted key recovery (5 rounds with ≤ 1,040 CP [≤ 520 pairs] and
    10 · 240 neural evaluations assuming 30 pre-known key bits; 7 rounds with D = 232 CP,
    T = 270 –272 evaluations). They also report a classical 5-round truncated-differential attack
    with 20 CP (40 CP after blind-column correction) and T = 242 operations for the last round
    key. Finally, Dunkelman and Ghosh [19, Footnote 4, p. 16] released a 6-round FHT prototype
    requiring 6 × 28 = 1,536 CP.


1.3     Overview of the OPTIC Framework
Spanning 5 to 12 rounds across twelve orders of magnitude in data (4 CP to 2126.58 CP) appears
to require a patchwork of disparate techniques. These range from dead-column island MITM,
one-hot XOR-SAT, and super-S-box collapse to colliding-pair cubes, 1-round lifts, additive-split
Bloom-MITM, streaming joint-histogram WHTs, and progressive list joins. Building on the
ZeroSum and partial-sum Walsh–Hadamard key-recovery paradigm [28, 21, 16, 33, 20, 8, 19],
we show that all of these attacks are parametric instantiations of a single framework, OPTIC
(Orthogonal Pullback and Ternary Integral Cryptanalysis).

 1. Forward Pullback Distinguishers (Module I, Section 3): Over F2 , a plaintext pair
    is a 1D cube (d = 1) whose cube sum is its output difference. Thus, 5-round truncated
    differentials (d = 1, 2), our 6-round 48-point prefix distinguisher (d = 6), our 6-round 42–80D

    unique orthogonal fourth linear layer cancels all cross-column quadratic terms and collapses
    two consecutive rounds into one degree-3 super-S-box (6 rounds at 48 CP).

5.2    Comparison with Quasidifferential Cryptanalysis
Comparing OPTIC against the quasidifferential analysis of Beyne, Neyt, and Sajjad [10] (ToSC
2026(3)) highlights both the complementary tradeoffs across 5–14 rounds and deep algebraic
connections between the two paradigms:

 1. All-Key Determinism (100% of 2256 Keys) vs. Weak-Key Differential Collapse
    (5–12 Rounds): In [10], every differential characteristic with R ≥ 5 rounds imposes linear
    constraints on the round keys via quasidifferential trails. Their 5-round characteristic [10,
    Example 1, pp. 7–8] holds with probability 2−42 only on a 2−8 weak-key fraction (and zero
    otherwise), while their corrected 6-round characteristic [10, Remark 1 and Appendix A]
    has key-averaged probability pavg = 2−82 (and Bellini et al. [7, Table 4], reproduced in [10,
    Table 3], report key-averaged characteristic bounds pavg = 2−106 , 2−122 , and 2−137 for 7,
    8, and 9 rounds without fixed-key guarantees). Above 5 rounds, Beyne et al. evaluate
    fixed-key differential probabilities only on 9 rounds: for the Round-0 differential of [7], they
    obtain 2−109 on 3 · 2256−28 keys and 2−106.678 on 2256−28 keys (2−26 of the key space in
    total) [10, §3.3], and for two shifted-round 9-round differentials they obtain 2−107.678 for
    a 2−38 fraction of keys and 2−119 for a 2−39 fraction of keys [10, §5.2–5.3]. Likewise, their
    12-round key-recovery attack [10, §6.1, Table 1] succeeds only for 2218 weak keys (2−38
    fraction) in time T = 2196.68 ≈ 2197 (D = 2108.68 CP, M = 236 ; or T = 2117 , D = 2111.38 CP,
    M = 282.38 under a binomial randomization heuristic with 63% success on the 2−38 weak-key
    subset). Within its 2−38 weak-key class, [10, §6.1] uses 217.9 × less data (2108.68 vs. 2126.58 CP)
    and lower memory (236 states vs. 2123.49 or 2153.81 bits) than our 12-round integral attacks,
    and extends to 14 rounds on 2−39 weak keys. Conversely, every Module I identity in OPTIC
    holds deterministically with probability 1 for all 2256 master keys. On 5 and 6 rounds we
    recover the full 256-bit master key from 4 CP (16.0 s) and 48 CP (2.4 s). On 6 rounds our
    216 -CP distinguisher requires 266 × less data than [10, Appendix A], and on 7–9 rounds
    we achieve full all-key recovery where [10] gives no key recovery. On 12 rounds we trade
    higher data and memory to expand the valid key space by 238 × while running > 248 × faster
    (T = 2148.62 packed ops ≈ 2141 enc.) than the non-heuristic T = 2197 attack of [10, Table 1].

 2. Unified Algebraic Origin of Half-Word Cone Asymmetry (Law II.3 vs. [10,
    Table 5]): In [10, §5.1, Table 5], Beyne et al. observed empirically via code search that
    extending a 1-column distinguisher-output difference across 2 or 3 outer key-recovery rounds
    requires guessing fewer subkey columns when the active column lies in the “left” 16-bit half
    (our upper half u, MSB columns 0 . . . 15) than in the “right” 16-bit half (our lower half
    l, MSB columns 16 . . . 31). In particular, |Kout | = 16 vs. 18 columns at 2 rounds and 39
    vs. 43 columns at 3 rounds (16-bit difference). Law II.3 explains both their observation
    and our 12-round |T12 (c)| = 22 vs. 23 leaf-cone split from a single matrix identity: for
    j ∈ {0, 1, 2}, bj + cj ̸≡ 0 (mod 16), so L⊤j ̸= Lj . In [10], differences propagate through
