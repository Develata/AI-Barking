                   FINITE-TIME SINGULARITY FORMATION
               FOR THE THREE-DIMENSIONAL EULER EQUATIONS

               LEVENT ALPÖGE, TRISTAN BUCKMASTER, AND MATEI P. COICULESCU


        Abstract. We construct smooth periodic initial data for the three-dimensional incompressible
        Euler equations whose solution blows up in finite time. The velocity remains bounded, while the
        vorticity magnitude at the origin tends to infinity. Away from the origin, the velocity and its
        time derivative have smooth spatial limits at the singular time. The construction uses localized
        Craik–Criminale waves as building blocks of a multi-scale construction.

                                             1. Introduction
   The incompressible Euler equations are among the earliest partial differential equations derived
in mathematical physics. Euler presented them in 1755, less than a decade after d’Alembert derived
the wave equation for the vibrating string [41, 42].
   The goal of this work is to construct smooth initial data for the incompressible Euler equations
on the three-dimensional torus whose solution blows up in finite time. We represent T3 by the cube
[−1/2, 1/2]3 with periodic boundary conditions (opposite faces identified). We write these equations
as
(1.1)                  ∂t u + (u · ∇)u + ∇p = 0,           div u = 0   on T3 × [0, T∗ ).
Local existence and uniqueness for sufficiently regular data go back to Lichtenstein and Günther in
the 1920s [43, 44]. Classical theory on T3 supplies a unique maximal smooth solution from smooth
divergence-free data [13, 10]. By the Beale–Kato–Majda criterion [9, 11], a smooth solution on [0, T )

continues smoothly through T < ∞ if 0 k curl u(t)kL∞ dt < ∞.

solutions exist for all time [45]. Yudovich [46] proved global existence and uniqueness for bounded,

possibly nonsmooth initial vorticity. In three dimensions, Fefferman’s description of the Navier–
Stokes Millennium Problem stated that the global regularity question was also open for the Euler
equations on R3 and on T3 [47].
   Numerical searches for blow-up include the Taylor–Green vortex study of Morf, Orszag and
Frisch [48] and Kerr’s computations for antiparallel vortex tubes [49]. In a later computation, Hou
and Li [50] observed at most double-exponential growth of the maximum vorticity past Kerr’s
predicted singular time. Luo and Hou [51] found numerical evidence for axisymmetric blow-up at
the wall of an axially periodic cylinder. Childress, Gilbert and Valiant [52] studied, analytically and
numerically, eroding vortex dipoles in axisymmetric flow without swirl, whose maximal vorticity
grows like t4/3 , and Childress and Gilbert [53] proposed, by formal asymptotic analysis, the hairpin
geometry as a model for finite-time blow-up.
   On R3 , Elgindi [15] constructed finite-time blow-up from C 1,α0 initial data for some α0 > 0.
Elgindi, Ghoul and Masmoudi [16] obtained stable, locally self-similar blow-up for finite-energy
data in this regularity class. Chen and Hou [28, 29] prove nearly self-similar blow-up from smooth
axisymmetric data in a periodic cylinder with boundary, using computer-assisted estimates. The
theorem below concerns smooth periodic data on T3 , with bounded velocity and vorticity growth
concentrated at the origin.

   We construct a sequence of smooth approximate solutions of the Euler equations whose residuals
tend to zero. The sequence converges in every spatial C m norm, uniformly on compact time intervals
before T∗ , to a solution of the Euler equations.
   At each step in the sequence we add an oscillation at a higher frequency, localized near the
origin and initially small in velocity. The velocity gradient of the preceding scales amplifies the new
oscillation, whose gradient in turn drives the growth of the next one. The growth intervals have
finite total length. This multiscale construction follows the approach of Córdoba and Martı́nez-Zoroa
for the Euler equations with forcing and for forced IPM [18, 19]. Córdoba, Martı́nez-Zoroa and
Zheng [17] used a cascade of vorticity layers to obtain blow-up for the unforced Euler equations on
R3 from C 1,α data smooth away from the origin. Related constructions give blow-up with forcing
for the inviscid Boussinesq equations, the variable-density Euler equations and the hypodissipative
Navier–Stokes equations [20, 21, 22]. Our companion papers proving singularity formation for forced
IPM, the forced Boussinesq equations, and the forced Euler equations [3, 1, 2] use forces smooth
in space and time up to the singular time. In the present construction we use Craik–Criminale
waves as building blocks and choose the initial velocity at each step so that the limiting solution is
unforced.
   Convex integration constructions of nonunique weak solutions of the Euler equations also use
oscillations at successive frequency scales. Below the Onsager exponent, this method yields solutions
with nonconstant kinetic energy [23, 6]. For the unforced Euler equations in the plane, Coiculescu [24,

25] uses nested vortex rings at different scales. An instability propagates from the inner rings
outward, yielding distinct renormalized weak solutions with the same initial vorticity. See also
Choi [26] and Hou, Qin, Song and Zhang [27]. For the three-dimensional Navier–Stokes equations,
Jia and Šverák [54] gave a conditional construction of nonunique Leray–Hopf solutions based on
spectral assumptions about forward self-similar flows. Nonuniqueness was shown for finite-energy
weak solutions by Buckmaster and Vicol [7], and in the Leray–Hopf class with a common force by
Albritton, Brué and Colombo [4]. In a recent preprint, Buckmaster, Coiculescu and Li [8] obtain
nonuniqueness of unforced Leray–Hopf solutions.

  The periodic construction starts from the stationary shear flow
                                     U0 (x) = sin(2πx2 )(e1 + ρe3 ),
where ei are the coordinate vectors and ρ > 0 is small. It solves the Euler equations with zero
pressure. In the initial coordinate frame its central gradient has ν = 2π and η = 2πρ, so that ε = ρ.
Its explicit flow supplies the first wave; subsequent waves use the successive gradients they create.
   Craik–Criminale waves on R3 provide a local model for amplification. For the periodic construction,
we localize each wave by a smooth cutoff supported in the interior of a fundamental cell in Lagrangian
coordinates. We extend the localized field periodically and transport its phase and cutoff by the
preceding periodic velocity, as in geometric optics [37, 38, 39, 40]. Since the cutoff vanishes near
the cell boundary, the wave vector need not lie in Z3 . Localization and the spatial variation of the
background introduce errors, which the corrections below make small at each step and remove in
the limit.

1.4. Comparison with blow-up constructions with forcing. The construction of blow-up for
the forced Boussinesq equations by Alpöge and Buckmaster [1, Section 1.2] also uses coupled wave
amplitudes to amplify a gradient and prepare the next wave. There the temperature gradient grows
together with a vorticity amplitude. The accompanying shear would obstruct the next growth stage,
so an external force rotates the temperature gradient and wave direction together. This preserves
the coupling that drives the temperature amplitude while changing the horizontal component of
the wave vector, and hence the sign of vorticity production. A scalar shooting parameter selects a
rotation pulse that returns the vorticity amplitude and that horizontal component to zero, retaining
the temperature gain. Keeping the wave vector vertical then holds both amplitudes fixed while the
next wave grows.
   The construction of blow-up for the forced Euler equations by Alpöge and Buckmaster [2,
Section 1.2] builds on this mechanism for axisymmetric flow with swirl. Circulation replaces
temperature and couples to azimuthal vorticity. The angular control returns the principal azimuthal-
vorticity amplitude at the material center to zero while retaining the circulation-gradient gain. The
radial dependence of the elliptic operator and of this coupling requires amplitudes that vary across
the transported support, with transport by the preceding velocity, including its corrections.
   In the present unforced construction, the amplified shear itself drives the successor. Its orientation
is obtained from the Craik–Criminale wave evolution; the pressure coefficient is determined by

incompressibility and is not a rotation control. The next wave is activated at the start of its
amplification interval, with its amplitude in the growing direction (Subsection 6.2).
   Localization presents a further difference. In the construction for the Boussinesq equations the
profiles are linear near zero, so each layer’s velocity and temperature are exactly linear on a region
containing the later layers. The streamfunction corrections vanish there, preserving this region.
In both forced constructions, the remaining errors are absorbed by a force smooth through the
singular time. To solve the unforced Euler equations, we must remove those errors. The pressure
and velocity corrections need not preserve a neighborhood on which the velocity is linear; the next
wave uses the central gradient and estimates on its spatial variation.
   The organization of this paper is as follows: Section 2 proves amplification and its stability,
Sections 3–5 localize the waves and construct the linear corrections, Sections 6 and 7 start and
iterate the construction, and Section 8 passes to the limit.

formalization of the proof and the latest version of this manuscript.

1.6. Provenance. Before OpenAI released its paper on the unforced Euler equations, the first
and second authors had completed a second proof of blow-up for the Euler equations with smooth

reduced equations for the amplitude of a wave, the system (2.5) below. In our unpublished work
on the hypodissipative Navier–Stokes equations, much of the argument for removing the force was
developed, with human prompts guiding that work. This is consistent with the second author’s
statement of September 7 that the hypodissipative work pointed toward a blow-up construction for
the unforced Euler equations [30].

in [30].

we replaced parts of the argument by ideas of our own, which the models implemented. Together

2.2. Amplification on a prescribed interval. Of the two entries ν = E1 · BE2 and η = E3 · BE2
of the older gradient introduced in Lemma 2.2, the coupling ν helps the preceding shear amplify a
new wave, while η changes its direction and eventually ends the growth. A small ratio η/ν therefore
allows a large gain before decay. In the model case of Subsection 1.1 and the limit of an infinitely
strong preceding shear, the amplitude of a new wave solves a linear system of two equations with
explicit coefficients (Lemma 2.3). Up to errors measured by one number δ, the amplitude of a
general new wave solves the same system (Lemma 2.4). Its solution with growing initial data peaks
after a large gain (Lemma 2.5), and the wave follows this solution with relative errors that grow
only polynomially in the length of the interval (Lemma 2.6). The couplings of the new wave are
estimated in Lemma 2.7, and Proposition 2.8 locates the ratio prescribed for its successor after the
amplitude peaks in the strong-shear limit.

                   5. From a localized wave to an approximate solution
   We correct the localized wave of Section 3 to obtain the parent velocity for the next wave. The
corrections must preserve the leading wave’s rank-one contribution to the velocity gradient at the
origin, where the next wave is amplified. The new parent must also have controlled flow deformation
and satisfy the pressure and residual bound (5.2) for the next endpoint problem.
   At each level of the expansion we first remove the phase average of the error using Theorem 4.2.
The first mean correction also cancels the residual of the parent. Transport of the leading wave
by the new mean then enters the equation for the oscillatory correction. The residual of the new
parent has three sources. The depth controls the remainder of the expansion. The wave is small
compared with its peak while it is switched on, so its gain controls the error from the time cutoff.
The final Fourier cutoff contributes the error estimated in (5.56). Section 7 chooses the parameters
to make all three errors small on the scale of the next wave.
