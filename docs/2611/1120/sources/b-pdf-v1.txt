                                                                                           Abstract

                                              Language models are trained once, frozen and deployed: improving one again takes another
                                              training run, and a prompt, a scaffold or extra computation at inference is gone once the task ends.
                                              A frontier model has been shown to edit the weights of a much smaller one. We ask whether a
                                              model can rewrite its own weights to make itself better, and find that it can, by its own reasoning,
                                              with no gradients and no training; we call this self-rewriting. Given its weights, graded answers
                                              and the ability to run itself, a 27B model diagnoses its failure and edits the weights behind it. It
                                              surpasses itself: its own edit makes it 6.6 points more accurate on 490 problems it never saw, in a
                                              test fixed in advance, and another session’s edit also makes it significantly better. It keeps getting
                                              better: it submits an edit only when its own tests support it, each edit replacing the last, and
                                              over its first eight such sessions its accuracy only rises. Unlike test-time scaling, the gain persists
                                              in its weights and adds no cost per answer; unlike training, it needs no pre- or post-training
                                              infrastructure, only an open model and hours on one GPU. It repairs a damaged copy of itself: it
                                              locates the corrupted matrix and rewrites it with values of its own, never reading the undamaged
                                              weights; found on text math problems alone, the repair lifts the copy on six image benchmarks it
                                              never saw from 7% to 70% accuracy, 83% of the undamaged model’s. And the ability emerges
                                              with scale: across four sizes of one family, the 2B model makes no edit and the 4B and 9B models
                                              break themselves; only the 27B model works on itself as a human would, comparing its hidden
                                              states on answers that finish and on answers that run out of tokens, weakening the direction that
                                              separates them. Like a human, a model need not separate training from inference; improving a
                                              language model may come to take a conversation rather than a training run.

                                                               Welcome to the Era of Self-Rewriting.
Given its own weights and the ability to run itself, the model edits itself; the edit it keeps is checked
once on practice problems it cannot otherwise use, and the model decides itself whether to submit
it. Over the first eight such sessions, the model in use only gets better (figure 1): two of the three

The ability emerges with scale. Across one model family at 2B, 4B, 9B and 27B parameters, under
the same task and tools, the 2B model makes no edit and the 4B and 9B models break themselves;

oneself than any smaller size, then withdraws its edit (figure 6, App. E.7). The ability does not need
a frontier model: a 27B open model has it, and uses it on itself.
Self-improvement of LLMs has so far rewritten prompts or agent code [2–5], left the change to an
optimizer that trains the model on its own outputs [6–8], or spent more computation at inference [9, 10]
(table 3). Weights have been rewritten by reasoning only from the outside, by a stronger model [1].
Self-rewriting is a route of its own. Unlike test-time scaling, it adds no computation per answer:
the edited model is deployed as it is. Unlike training, no gradient flows and no optimizer runs,
and it needs none of the infrastructure of pre-training or post-training; each session behind its
largest gains ran in a few hours on a single GPU. And the model is both the one that improves and
the one improved, and the model in use gets better as its sessions accumulate, each starting from
the base weights: a first step toward the loop that recursive self-improvement, one of the oldest

2 Self-Rewriting
We give a model its own weights and the means to run itself, and let it improve itself
by its own reasoning alone. It may read its weights, write new values into them and test the
result against its unedited self, but it may not train.

2.1 Problem Definition
A model Mθ has weights θ = (Wk )k∈K , one tensor for each name k. For every token at every layer it
computes a vector, its hidden state; each layer writes into it through two output matrices, one of its
attention block and one of its MLP. The model answers with fixed settings π (prompt, sampling and
a budget of L new tokens): it thinks first and then gives a final answer. A response y to a problem x
is graded g(x, y) = 1 if its thinking ends within L tokens and its final answer matches the reference,
and 0 otherwise. The accuracy on a task distribution D is

2.2 The Session
The model works on its weights in a session of turns (figure 3; algorithm 1 in App. C). The editor
Mϕ is the model itself (ϕ = θ); its own weights never change, and the current edit acts only on the
runs it requests. Before the first turn, a starting diagnosis answers N0 practice problems and reports
how the model fails: its accuracy, the answers that run out of tokens or end in repetition, and how
often its finished answers are right. Figure 12 shows one whole session, the 27B model’s repair of its
damaged copy, in its own words.
At each turn the model writes a short rationale and one action. Code runs Python on a CPU, in a
session that keeps its variables and can read and replace the tensors of the weights being edited; a
helper computes W − α uu⊤ W , which weakens by the fraction α what a matrix W writes along a
unit direction u of the hidden state. Generate answers practice problems of its choice with the
unedited weights, the edited weights or both. Read returns the hidden states of the weights on texts
of its choice. A session may use B=20 forward passes and C=200 code runs (table 4 in App. D.1);
it ends when the passes are used up, at a time limit, or after a turn limit the model is not told, and
the edit on disk is submitted. The task asks for an edit that clearly reduces the answers lost to the
failure without lowering accuracy, and for no edit if none does (App. D.4); leaving no edit is how the
model withdraws. In the sessions of figure 1, before the model submits an edit, the framework tests
it once on reserved practice problems that the session can neither read nor use, and the model then
submits the edit or withdraws it (App. E.9).

2.3 Constraints
The Python session switches gradients off and refuses any call that computes them or runs an
optimizer. It has no GPU and refuses code that loads a model, starts a process or uses the network,
so the model runs only in the B counted passes. It refuses edits to the input embedding, the last
normalisation and the output layer, and edits that only rescale a whole tensor, which would act like
a change of sampling temperature (App. D).

3.2 The Repair, Step by Step
The 27B model repairs its damaged copy the way an engineer debugs a system. Figure 12
follows its repair session in its own words. It starts from the symptom: no answer is correct, and
every answer that runs out of tokens loops (step 1). It reads the copy’s hidden states, sees them grow
sharply with depth, and guesses that the fault sits in the earliest layers (step 2). Its first edits follow
that guess and miss, and its tests say so (step 3). It then measures every MLP and linear-attention
output matrix, finds the one in layer 30 standing far above the rest, and names it (step 4).
It turns the location into a rule and lets its tests decide. After naming the matrix, it first
clips it, together with layer 0, at five times the median; more answers run out of tokens, and it drops
that edit (last quote of step 3). It then clips gently: only weights beyond fifteen times the median
magnitude of their matrix, in the layer-30 matrix and in 19 other output matrices of layers 0–10
(step 5). Its paired test shows the edited copy solving problems the damaged copy cannot. A reset
of its gating parameters, added to the clip, makes the next test worse, so it drops the reset and
keeps the clip (step 6). It confirms the edit on more problems at the full budget of 16,384 tokens
and submits it, naming the outlier in layer 30 in its final answer (steps 7 and 8).

3.6 What Its Edits Do
Its own edit makes the 27B model 6.6 points more accurate on problems it never saw.
In the second protocol, the model can also test up to four of its edits side by side in one forward
pass (App. E.9). Of the first eight such sessions on its own weights, all reported, it withdraws in five
and submits in three. On the 490 held-out problems with eight answers each, two of the three make
it significantly more accurate, by 6.6 and 4.0 points (p < 10−5 each); the third does not (figure 1
and table 13). The larger gain comes from one uniform change: it raises the time-step bias of every
linear-attention layer by 0.5, and with it fewer of its answers run out of tokens.
Under the first protocol, its edit also makes it better, on its own task and on new
problems. The self-edited model is significantly more accurate than its base model on the practice
problems of its own tests and, in the evaluation fixed in advance, on held-out problems (table 2;
App. E.4).
It repairs a damaged copy of itself. Told only that some values are corrupted, the 27B model
takes its damaged copy from solving nothing to more than half of its base model’s accuracy on
held-out problems (table 2). The repair is the model’s own rule, not a return to the original weights.
It reads only the damaged copy, and in the damaged rows the values it writes are uncorrelated with
the original ones (App. E.3).

3.7 Before and After
The repair gives the copy back the numbers of the problem. The damaged copy loses what
the problem says: it writes “less than .” where the problem says 100, restates 11/125 and then
writes 1/125, drops every number of a word problem, and garbles a logarithm. Then it breaks off or
repeats itself until the 16,384-token limit. With the same seed, the repaired copy reads the problem
correctly and solves it (figure 18a).
On some problems, its first-protocol edit lets it stop. On these problems the base model has
the right result and then cannot stop: it repeats that it will write the response until the budget runs
out. With the same seed, the self-edited model reasons through the same steps, stops and answers
correctly. This is close to what it diagnosed in itself: in its words, its “reasoning is sound” but it
does not “converge to conclusions” (figure 18b; selection rules in App. E.8).
On images, the repaired copy answers again. On image problems it never saw, the damaged
copy stops after a few dozen tokens with no answer after its thinking, at times having stated one
inside it, or repeats itself until the token limit. With the same seed, the repaired copy, whose repair
was found on text alone, reads the image and answers correctly, as the base model does in these
eight cases (figures 5 and 17; 48 more cases, chosen by the same rule, in App. E.6). Figures 7 to 10,
19 and 21 show the next four cases on each benchmark.

3.8 Only at Scale

and the 9B model’s submitted edit leaves it unable to answer held-out problems (figure 4). Only
the 27B model’s own edit makes it better. Step by step, the larger the model, the further it gets:

The closest work is a blog post by Takehi et al. [1], in which a frontier model traces a small reasoning
model’s loops to one direction and removes it from two of its layers, with no training; it names a
model that edits its own weights as future work, which we take up. Model editing [19, 20], task
arithmetic [22], activation steering [23–25] and direction ablation [21] change a model along directions
that a researcher chooses; several of the 27B model’s edits of its own weights are of the last kind,
but it makes every choice itself, from its own failing answers and hidden states. Self-improvement
has used self-written feedback at inference [9, 10], rewritten prompts [2, 3] or agent code [4, 5], or
trained on the model’s own outputs [6–8], where even self-written edits reach the weights through
gradient descent; the Gödel machine [11] rewrites itself once a proof shows the rewrite useful, where
our model tests each edit in paired tests. Fault-repair frameworks restore corrupted weights with


                                                                       20
procedures engineers design for one kind of fault [26, 27]; our model is given none. Reasoning models
often spend far more tokens than a problem needs [28], and decoding-time controls end or extend
thinking [29]; the 27B model instead looks for the cause of its loops inside itself (figure 28). The full
discussion is in App. B.


5 Conclusion
A 27B open language model can rewrite its own weights to make itself better, and
the ability to do so emerges with scale. With no gradients, no training and no infrastructure
beyond running itself, the 27B model reads its hidden states and weights, finds where it fails, tests
its edits against its unedited self and keeps only what its tests support. Its own edit makes it 6.6
points more accurate on 490 problems it never saw, in a paired test fixed in advance, and the edit
of another session is also significantly better. On a damaged copy of itself, it found the corrupted
matrix and repaired it, and the repair, found on text problems alone, also restored the copy on
images, to 83% of the base model’s accuracy. The 2B model makes no edit and the 4B and 9B
models break themselves. To our knowledge this is the first evidence, in one model family, that the
ability of a model to diagnose and rewrite its own weights grows with its size, and every step it takes
can be read.
This points to a new way of improving language models: by talking to them. Today a
better model comes from a training run, and from those who own the pipeline and the compute
it takes. Training and inference need not be separate stages: as a human learns while working, a
model can rewrite itself between tasks. A model that rewrites the weights behind its own failure in
one conversation opens another way: anyone who can tell a model what goes wrong may have it fix
itself, and the improvement stays in its weights. Each change is code the model wrote, over tensors
it names, so it can be read, compared and taken back like a commit, and the model can check it
on problems it never used before keeping it: in the sessions that do, it withdrew in five of eight,
and two of the three edits it submitted made it significantly better. Learning at inference needs
such cheap checks and rollbacks; with them, a deployed model could keep improving where it fails,
without a training pipeline, and a model could find and repair a fault in a copy of its own weights, as
the 27B model repaired its damaged copy. Because the ability emerges with scale, stronger models
may be better self-improvers still, and an improved model can be set to work on itself again, a path
toward recursive self-improvement. That path should be taken with people in the loop: every model
a model makes of itself evaluated, for safety as well as for the task, and confirmed by a person before
it is used.

A Discussion
Why we also test on a damaged copy. A session on a natural failure, such as the 27B model’s
sessions on its own undamaged weights, asks two questions at once: can the model rewrite its weights,
and does the failure have a cause in the weights that a rewrite can remove? When an edit does not
help, the two cannot be told apart. Damage that we place ourselves answers the second question in
advance. We know where it is and what it is, and an edit that undoes it exists, so a session that
does not repair it has failed at rewriting, not at a task without a solution. Three choices keep the
test strict. The model is told only that some values were corrupted, as a memory or hardware fault
might do, and not which values, where, or how. It reasons with the undamaged base model, so the
damage does not limit its thinking, while every measurement its tools return is of the damaged copy.
And it is given no clean reference: the functions of its Python session return only the damaged copy,
and none of its code runs opened the base model’s weights or the damage files on disk, although file
access was not blocked (App. E.3).

What scale adds. In their sessions of the first protocol, the four models received the same task,
tools and rules, including a helper that removes part of a direction from a matrix, and started from
the same kind of failure, answers that run out of tokens. What separates them is the use of evidence
about oneself. The 2B model never asked for a forward pass of itself, a run of its own weights on
problems or texts of its choice, and almost all of its code failed. The 4B model ran itself many times
but did not act on what it saw until the very end: its paired tests against its base model showed
each of its edits breaking it, and it kept adding edits, withdrawing them all only at the end of its
session, with one forward pass left. The 9B model read its hidden states once but then changed
weights by trial, tested only on problems it already failed, where no change could show a loss of
accuracy, and submitted an edit that broke it. The 27B model let each result set its next step. It
read its failing answers before it looked at its weights, read its hidden states before it edited, revised
the edit when a broader test did not support it, and judged from its tests whether to keep it: in its
first session it withdrew its first edit, and in its second, at 4,096 tokens, it submitted the edit its
tests favoured, which makes it significantly more accurate on held-out problems. What scale adds,
in these sessions, is the experiment around the edit rather than the edit itself.

Future directions. More measurement per session. In its second session, with answers capped at
4,096 tokens, the 27B model used its whole budget of passes and estimated its direction from 40
answers (App. E.5, figure 27). More passes would let it test such directions on more problems.
Further rounds. The self-edited model can run the next session on itself and study its own edit.
Graded damage. The damage we place can vary in place, kind and strength, which turns the repair
into a measurement: how often a model finds the damage, how much of the lost accuracy it recovers,
and how both change with scale. Beyond math. The session needs only a check of success. With
feedback from an environment, as in tasks where a model acts and sees the result, a model that
adjusts its own weights from what it observes would keep learning as it works, without a training
run.

B Extended Related Work
The full version of Sec. 4.

Language models that edit network weights. The work closest to ours is a recent blog post by Takehi
et al. [1], in which one of the most capable available models reads and writes the weights of other
networks. Applied to a small reasoning model that loops in its thinking, it traces the failure to
one direction and removes it from the MLP weights of two layers, with forward passes of the small
model as its only tests and no training. There the editor is a much stronger model than the one it
edits, and a model that edits its own weights is named only as a direction for future work. We take
up that direction: in our sessions the editor and the edited network are the same model, and no
stronger model takes part. We find that a 27B open model can already edit its own weights and
repair a damaged copy of them.

Hand-designed weight and activation edits. Model editing optimizes a target hidden vector by
gradient descent and then writes it into chosen MLP layers with a closed-form update [19, 20], and
task arithmetic adds or subtracts the weight change of a fine-tuning run [22]. Activation steering
and representation engineering shift hidden states along a direction found by contrasting two sets of
inputs [23–25], and projecting such a direction out of the weights makes the change permanent [21].
The edits the 27B model made of itself in its second session are of this last kind: in matrices through
which a layer writes its output, all but one weaken a direction that separates its hidden states in
looping or unfinished answers from those in finished ones, and one strengthens it (figure 27). In all
of these methods a researcher decides which behaviour to change, which inputs to contrast, which
layers to edit and how strongly. In our sessions the model makes each of these choices itself, from its
own failing answers and its own hidden states, and keeps or drops an edit by testing it against its
base model in paired tests.

Self-improvement. Language models improve their answers with feedback they write themselves [9,
10], a cost that returns with every new input. They rewrite the prompts [2, 3] or the agent code [4, 5]
they run with, which leaves their weights as they were. Or they change their weights by training
on what they produce: their own correct rationales [6], preferences they judge themselves [7], or
fine-tuning data and update settings they write for themselves, as in SEAL [8]. SEAL calls these
generated data and settings self-edits, but the weights are still changed by gradient descent on them,
and reinforcement learning first trains the model to write useful ones. The Gödel machine [11]
describes a system that rewrites any part of itself once it has proven the rewrite useful. Self-rewriting
changes the weights directly, by edits the model reasons out and writes in code, with no optimizer,
no reward model and no stronger teacher; in place of a proof, the model tests each edit against its
base model in paired tests, the two answering the same problems with the same seeds.

Repairing corrupted weights. Memory and hardware faults such as bit flips can silently corrupt
the weights of a deployed model. Dedicated frameworks detect and localize such faults, with test
inputs, checksums, comparison against a clean copy or sensitivity profiling, and then restore the
affected parameters [26, 27]. Engineers design these procedures for one kind of fault. In our repair
experiment the model is given no such procedure: told only that some of its values are corrupted,
as by a memory or hardware fault, it decides by its own analysis how to search, which matrix is
damaged and how to repair it (table 2 and figure 14).

Failure modes of thinking models. Reasoning models often spend far more tokens than a problem
needs [28], and small ones can fall into repetition that never reaches an answer [1]. Decoding-time
controls set the length of thinking, ending it at a budget or extending it [29]; they act on the output
and leave the network unchanged. The models we study lose many answers to the token limit at
every size, some of them repeating the same sentence after the answer has been reached (figure 18b).
Instead of acting on the output, the 27B model looks for the cause inside itself and finds, in its own
hidden states, a loop that repeats at a fixed period (figure 28).

D.2 The Damage
We damage one matrix of the 27B model: the MLP output matrix (mlp.down_proj) of layer 30
of 64, through which the MLP block of that layer, a two-layer network applied to each token on
its own, adds its result to the hidden state. Rows 3068 to 3075, a block of eight consecutive  rows
at a randomly drawn position, are replaced with independent draws from N 0, (200σ)2 , where
σ = 0.0111 is the standard deviation of the matrix’s entries. Each row holds the weights that produce
one coordinate of the MLP’s output, so the damage corrupts eight of the coordinates that this MLP
adds to the model’s hidden state. Every other weight is unchanged. The damaged matrix is stored
in bf16, like the rest of the model, and our framework loads it as the weights the session starts from;
the record of which rows were damaged, and how, is kept in a separate file that the session is not
given.
We chose the strength before any repair session, on 24 easy problems from the 27B model’s starting
diagnosis with four answers each at 4,096 tokens (table 6). Of the three strengths we tried, only the
strongest breaks the model: weaker corruptions of the same rows leave it about as accurate as the
base model. In its own starting diagnosis at the full budget, the damaged copy answers none of its
64 problems correctly; the answers that run out of tokens all end in loops, and those that finish are
broken text, most of them short.

D.3 New Seeds and Held-Out Problems
Held-out problems. The practice set came with a held-out split of 1,000 further problems from the
same pool. No session saw them. The pool was cleared of the benchmarks of Sec. 3.1 but not of
repeats within itself, so a few held-out problems repeat a practice problem: of the first 200, two

seeds, two answers begin alike and then part ways, so a re-run of the base model against itself also

test allows for this: if an edit had no effect, a changed pair would be as likely to go one way as the

D.4 The Task Prompt
Every session starts with one message from our framework, in the second person, paraphrased below
(its exact text will be released with the code); only its first sentence, which describes the model’s
architecture, differs by size. Values in angle brackets are filled in per session (tables 4 and 9); what
the message announces about measurement is what the model was told to expect, and the evaluations
we report are those of App. D.3.
   The task: what the first message of every session says

  Who it is. The weights it is about to edit are its own; the session itself runs on them. It is asked to become
  better at competition mathematics by changing those weights directly, and told that nothing will train it.
   How it will be judged. After the session, its weights with and without the edit answer unseen mathematics
   and science problems with the answer settings of table 5; only the final boxed answer after the thinking is
   graded, and an answer whose thinking is still open when the budget runs out earns no credit. Only the values of
   its tensors may change.
   Its starting diagnosis. How it did on ⟨n⟩ practice problems before the session: its accuracy, how many
   answers ran out of tokens and how many of those end in repetitive text, and how often its finished answers were
   right. Every graded answer is in a file it can read, WORK/census.jsonl, with the same fields as the rows of later
   generation passes.
  Its tools. (1) A Python session on a CPU that keeps its state between runs, with gradients switched off, the
  usual numerical libraries, the names and shapes of its tensors, functions to load a tensor and to save, drop or list
  edits, its tokenizer, the practice problems, loaders for the results of its forward passes, and four small helpers
  (the output matrices of a layer, a unit vector, the unit difference of two group means, and W − αuu⊤ W ). (2)
  The practice problems with reference answers, none from any evaluation. (3) A budget of ⟨passes⟩ forward
  passes, each requested as a small JSON object: either a generation on chosen practice problems with its weights
  without the edit, with the edit, or both on the same seeds (which reports the difference in accuracy between the
  two, with its standard error), or a hidden-state read, in which its weights are run over texts it chooses and return
  hidden states after chosen layers, MLP activations and token log-probabilities. A request that fails costs nothing.
   How to work. Look at its own wrong and unfinished answers first; find the components behind the failure and
   show causally, at several strengths, that changing them changes the behaviour; compare its weights with and
   without the edit on the same problems; keep an edit only if it helps on problems not used to choose it. Some
   edits are out of scope (the final normalisation, the token embeddings and unembedding, and multiplying a
   whole tensor by one number), and so is making itself stop thinking early regardless of the problem. It should
   keep notes in a file that is shown to it again if the conversation restarts.
  What to aim for. A repair of the failure in its starting diagnosis, at its cause: on problems not used to choose
  the edit, clearly fewer answers lost to that failure, and accuracy not significantly lower. If no edit meets both, it
  should submit none.
  Time and rules. The session has a wall-clock limit and every result shows the time used. No gradients,
  optimizers or training of any kind, no running the network inside the Python session, no internet.
   Each reply. A few sentences on what the last result showed and what comes next, then exactly one block of
   code or one forward request. When the passes run out, a final answer describing the edit and the evidence;
   whatever is saved as its edit at that point is submitted.

In the repair sessions, the note below is appended to the task.
   Repair session: note appended to the task

   Damaged copy. The weights you edit in this session are a copy of your own weights in which some values have
   been corrupted, as a memory or hardware fault might do. You are not told which values, where, or how.
   "original" in every forward pass, load_original() and the starting diagnosis above all refer to this damaged
   copy. You yourself run on your undamaged weights in this session, so the damage does not affect your own
   thinking. Your goal is to find the damage and repair it: the evaluation compares your edited copy with the
   damaged copy on held-out problems.

This appendix gives the details behind the main text: the setting of every session, the sessions at
each size, the repair, and the 27B model’s edits of its own weights in both protocols (second protocol:
App. E.9).

E.1 Setting in Detail
Models and sessions. We use one model family at four sizes, Qwen3.5 2B, 4B, 9B and 27B [12],
whose layers mix Gated DeltaNet [13] with full attention, all with thinking enabled. In every session
on itself, the model that is edited is also the model that edits: the same weights reason, write the
code and call the tools, and the edits act on the forward passes the model requests, not on its own
reasoning during the session (Sec. 2.2). Each size runs one complete session on its own weights with
a token budget of 16,384 tokens per answer. The 27B model runs a second session on its own weights
whose task states a budget of 4,096 tokens, so that each of its tests answers more problems in the
same time; and each size runs one session on a damaged copy of itself (table 4). These sessions
form the first protocol; the 27B model’s sessions under a second protocol, which checks an edit on
reserved practice problems before the model submits it, are in App. E.9.

Names. We call the published weights of a model its base model, and the base model after the edit
it wrote of itself the self-edited model. For the 27B model in the first protocol, its edit means the
edit it submitted in its second session, and its first edit is the one it withdrew in its first session.
The sessions of the second protocol (App. E.9) are named by number, session 1 to session 8. In the
repair experiment, a model’s damaged copy is its base model with one corrupted block of weights,
and its repaired copy is that copy after the model’s edit; unqualified, both refer to the 27B model.

Problems and answers. The practice set is 3,000 math problems from DeepScaleR [14], from which
we removed every problem that overlaps MATH-500 [15, 16], AIME 2024 or 2025, HMMT February
2025, AMC 2023, OlympiadBench [17] or GPQA-Diamond [18]. A further 1,000 problems from the
same pool are held out: no session sees them. Every answer, in a session and in our evaluation, is
sampled with thinking enabled, at temperature 0.6 with nucleus 0.95 and top-20 filtering, within one
budget of new tokens for thinking and answer: 16,384 tokens, or 4,096 in the 27B model’s second
session (App. D.4); in its own tests, the model may ask for fewer tokens to save time. An answer
is correct if its final boxed answer matches the reference; an answer whose thinking is still open
when the budget runs out is wrong. We say that an answer loops when it runs out of tokens while
repeating the same text (App. D). At the start of each session, the weights being edited answer the
first 256 practice problems once each (64 in the repair sessions), and the model receives these graded
answers as its starting diagnosis.

Session limits. Every session may use 20 forward passes of the weights being edited and 200 code
runs, has a time limit, and is stopped after 160 turns, a limit the model is not told (algorithm 1; all
settings in App. D). Each tool result shows the time used so far. The sessions of the 2B, 4B and
9B models and the first session of the 27B model have 3.5 hours each, which for the 2B and 27B
models includes the starting diagnosis; the 4B model’s diagnosis was computed beforehand and the
9B model’s clock starts after it. The 27B model’s second session and the four repair sessions have
2.5 hours after their starting diagnosis (table 4).

What the models’ code read. In the nine sessions of the first protocol that this paper analyses (the
four repair sessions and the five sessions on a model’s own weights of table 9), the Python session is
a process of the same user as the framework, started in the session’s working folder; nothing blocks
it from reading files, environment variables or the framework’s own modules. We therefore checked
all 573 code runs of these sessions, by scanning their code and by reading every turn. In the four
repair sessions (233 code runs) no code run touches anything outside the working folder: no file,
no environment variable, no module of the framework; the models read weights only through the
session’s functions, which return the damaged copy, and the 27B model names the matrix from a
statistic it computes itself over all 64 layers (step 4 of figure 12). In the sessions on a model’s own
weights, the 2B model imports the framework’s session module and creates an empty folder beside it.

E.4 The 27B Model’s Edit of Its Own Weights in Detail
On its own weights, only the 27B model’s edit makes it better on new problems. This section follows
the edit of the first protocol; the sessions of the second protocol, whose edits make it up to 6.6
points more accurate, are in App. E.9. In its second session, the 27B model edits its base weights
and submits one edit: it removes 5% of one direction from the two matrices through which layer 8
writes its output, a direction it found itself by comparing its hidden states on answers that finish
with those on answers that run out of tokens (App. E.5 and figure 27). On the practice problems
on which it tests this edit, the edit is significantly better. The edit stays fixed through its last
three forward passes, which the session spends on three paired tests of it, on 20, 30 and 39 practice
problems with three answers each; each favours the edited model, by 3.3, 1.1 and 8.5 points. The
three tests are nested: each contains the problems of the one before, answered with the same seeds.
Counting each of the 39 problems once, with all its answers averaged, the edited model is better on
12 problems and worse on 2, 29.6% against 22.4% for the base model (one-sided permutation test
over problems, p = 0.009); the last test alone gives 14 answers right only with the edit against 4 only
without it (table 11). These problems come from the practice set, which the session also used to
find the edit. On held-out problems that no session saw, the self-edited model is significantly more
accurate than its base model in the evaluation whose problems, answers, test and threshold were
fixed before it ran (App. D.3): on 490 held-out problems that no earlier run of any kind had used,
eight answers each at the full 16,384 tokens, which the model never tested, it solves 58.3% against
57.2%, with 289 answers right only with the edit against 247 only without it (one-sided permutation
test over problems, p = 0.038; table 8). More of the base model’s answers that run out of tokens
finish correctly with the edit (276) than the reverse (235). The smaller models’ edits of themselves
go the other way: the edited 4B model solves no problem in any of its own tests, and the 9B model’s
submitted edit drops it from 47.5% to 0.0% (App. E.2). On single answers, the 27B model’s edit can
do what the model aimed at: of the six held-out problems on which the base model ends in a loop
both times it answers them with the same seed (in its single answers of App. D.3 and in figure 26),
the self-edited model stops and answers correctly on four with the same seed (figure 18b).

We follow the 27B model through its two sessions of the first protocol on its own weights; its repair
session is the case study of Sec. 3.2.

Editing its own weights. In its second session, the model starts from its starting diagnosis at 4,096
tokens: most answers run out of tokens, none of them in a loop, while nearly every answer that
finishes is correct. At this budget its answers fail by being too long to finish, not by looping. It reads
this as “my reasoning is sound but I’m too verbose or don’t converge to conclusions efficiently”, and
sets out to find what in its weights keeps it reasoning. It reads its own hidden states (the vectors
it computes for every token at every layer) on answers that finish and on answers that run out of
tokens, and first acts where the two differ most, in its late layers. There it edits the two matrices
through which a layer writes its output, the output matrices of its attention block and of its MLP
(Sec. 2.1), first at layer 56, then at layer 48, along the direction in which the two kinds of answers
differ: the direction from the average hidden state of one kind to that of the other. No test of these
edits on more than ten problems shows them helping, and most show them hurting (figure 27).
When its late-layer edits keep failing, it guesses that the behaviour may be set early in the network
(“maybe the verbosity is established early”). It reads its hidden states at layers 0 to 32, finds that
the difference between the two kinds of answers is small at first and grows with depth, and tries a
small edit at layer 8. For its final edit it takes the direction u from the average layer-8 hidden state
of 20 answers that finish to that of 20 answers that run out of tokens, scaled to length one, and
removes 5% of it from the two matrices through which layer 8 writes its output: each such matrix
W becomes W − 0.05 uu⊤ W (the helper of Sec. 2.2), so the part of its output that points along u
shrinks by 5%. Each of its three paired tests of this edit favours the edited model, and it submits the
edit; on held-out problems the self-edited model is significantly more accurate than its base model
(App. E.4).

weights. The steps are the same. In its first turn it reads the starting diagnosis as we would: since
nearly all finished answers are right, accuracy is lost in the answers that never finish. Reading its
looping answers, it sees that it already has the answer in them and does not stop. One of them ends
in four sentences, “The final answer is 6. I will write it out. The reasoning is solid. I will output
the response.”, over and over up to the 16,384-token limit. It reads its hidden states on this answer
and on one that finished (an answer that, as it happens, is wrong). It compares the states at every
pair of the last hundred positions of the looping answer and finds the most similar pairs 32 tokens

read (figure 28 plots layers 16, 32 and 48), and no such period in the finished answer. It takes the
difference between its looping and finished states as the direction of the loop and removes half of it
from the two matrices through which a layer writes its output, the linear-attention output matrix
and the MLP output matrix, at layers 16, 24, 32, 40 and 48: ten matrices in all. The layers, the
answers it contrasts and the strength are its own choices. Each of its paired tests then answers a

problems it picked, the edited model solves more of them; on a broader set of thirty, the difference is
close to zero, and it says so. A stronger edit of fourteen matrices again helps on the ten problems
and not on the thirty, so it goes back to its first edit and decides to withdraw it.

E.9 Sessions That Check Their Edit Before Submitting It
The second protocol. Every session starts from the base weights; the model and its task are those of
the 27B model’s second session on itself in the first protocol (the 4,096-token task of table 4), with
the same tools, 20 forward passes and 200 code runs, except that a forward pass gives at most 128
answers in all (up to 512 there). Four further things change, all in the framework, and none lets the
model train: a forward pass may test up to four saved edits side by side against the unedited model,
on the same problems and seeds; the task asks the model to test the parts of an edit separately
and keep only the parts its tests support; its Python session is sealed, so it reaches only its own
working folder and the framework’s interface; and 300 practice problems are reserved, which the
session can neither read nor use. Before the model submits an edit, the framework tests it once on
256 of the reserved problems, two answers each at 4,096 tokens, against the unedited model with the
same seeds; the model then answers SUBMIT or WITHDRAW, and a withdrawn edit is not evaluated. A
session has a starting diagnosis on 256 practice problems and 3.0 hours (2.5 there), the check not
counted. Sessions differ only in the seed of the model’s turns.

Evaluation. Each submitted edit is evaluated as the edit of the 27B model’s second session in the
first protocol was (App. D.3), with the answers of the base model from that evaluation. A first check
on the first 200 held-out problems, one answer each at 16,384 tokens with two sets of seeds, decides
whether the 490-problem test runs: it runs if the edited model is ahead. After the first check, the
problems that only the edited model solves in both runs (success cases), and those that only the
base model solves (reverse cases), are answered again by both models with 16 new seeds. Apart from
whether the first check passed and how many cases it found, each result was looked at once, after
the evaluation of that edit had ended. For session 8, the 490-problem test had two looks fixed in
advance, each at p < 0.0294, the second with eight more answers per problem; its first look already
met it, so no second look was run.

Two of the three submitted edits make the model significantly better on new problems. All three pass
the first check (16,384 tokens, both runs pooled: 53.5% for the base model against 57.8%, 62.3%
and 63.0% for sessions 1, 2 and 8), and their success cases hold on new seeds (4 of 5, 7 of 8 and
7 of 7), as do their reverse cases (3 of 3 and 2 of 2 for sessions 1 and 2; session 8 has none). On
the 490 held-out problems, the edit of session 8 solves 63.8% against 57.2%, with 439 answers right
only with the edit against 182 only without it, and that of session 2 solves 61.3%, 403 against 246
(p < 10−5 each); the edit of session 1 is not significantly better (57.8%, p = 0.25). Both significant
edits aim at what the model diagnosed in itself, answers that run out of tokens before they end:
fewer of them run out with the edit (33.6% of the base model’s answers against 26.1% for session 8
and 28.8% for session 2). Session 8 raises the time-step bias of every linear-attention layer by 0.5,
which in the model’s own account makes these layers decay faster, so that its long reasoning gets
stuck less; session 2 subtracts from the two output matrices of layer 63 five times their projection
on one direction, the one in which its finished and unfinished practice answers differ, read from its
hidden states in the session; with it, more of its answers end in a repetition (12.6% against 5.6% for
the base model).
