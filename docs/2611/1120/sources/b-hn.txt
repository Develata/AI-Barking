It's also interesting to look at how the agent does the fix. Appendix E5 shows what it's doing:
- Find a common category of error (too many tokens).
- Calculate aggregated difference between states in examples in that error category and other examples.
- Update weights to push states along that difference direction toward the non-error states.
In other words, deprived of gradient descent as a tool, it approximates it. It's clever, and it's a neat experiment. It doesn't show any superhuman insight into its weights -- with the same constraints, a human would likely take a similar approach. But it's early days for self modification and it will be interesting to see if models get a deeper understanding of weights as they get smarter.

I wouldn’t call myself an expert but the analogy is quite flawed. Its more like saying “we start out as junior developers fixing bugs and then we do brain surgery until we’re principal engineers”. This isn’t a feedback loop baked into the LLM’s structure that has it learn from new material somehow. It’s more like brain surgery that removes a tumor that was causing you seizures.
It’s not clear how you’d extrapolate this to more complex self-modifications or if such a thing were even possible. Think of it in terms of latent space - at some point the model has come up with as optimal a representation of the “truth” of the universe as it’s ever going to get with its current weights and structure (ie global optimum). Tweaking weights from there only decreases performance. If you did this job right you found this optimum during training.
True self-modification that’s interesting would need to be able to incrementally update the structure or alter the dimensionality.
Now the astute student will say “but there’s probably no such thing as a global optimum for every problem and you can probably tradeoff performance” but then you’d have to show this is more effective than fine tuning which does a similar job and is much more principled in how it works and more energy efficient.

I have been working on weight-space-programming as a side project since the blog post. Here is what I understand:
- We normally train deep neural networks with back-propagation. We compute gradients to find out how to tweak the weights toward the desired output.
- That's a fixed recipe. And it's not the only way to tweak the weights. It's just the best one that works in scale. The brain, for instance, does not do back-propagation, as far as we know.
- There are other known mathematical methods (mostly from Linear Algebra) to analyze the activations and compute what weights to change and how, to push the output to the desired way.
- A sufficiently advanced LLM knows about these methods and can analyze data like activation maps. With a specific target in hand, it can combine them into new constructions. (see [2] for some of the methods they used and I collected in a repo)
- So it's not a fixed training for-loop anymore. It's a dynamic "training" iteration. The LLM looks at the model's outputs, reads some of its layers on chosen inputs, computes an edit, applies it and tests it.
- It's dynamic because LLM chooses that to do and how to do it at each iteration by reasoning. I think that's the most interesting part and could be a new paradigm.
- I also compared it with gradient training. On my target, training was better (details in [1]), but WSP can find its niche. Its biggest advantage is that it needs no backward pass: less memory, and the model only has to run inference. It needs no differentiable loss, it can enforce constraints directly instead of through a penalty term, and the edit is easier to interpret.
[1] https://medium.com/@ozgungenc/weight-space-programming-on-a-... [https://medium.com/@ozgungenc/weight-space-programming-on-a-real-coding-agent-what-worked-what-didnt-52723245d623]
[2] https://github.com/ozgung/weightprog/blob/main/docs/methods.... [https://github.com/ozgung/weightprog/blob/main/docs/methods.md]

I believe the intended analogy is something like: Training on highly blurred or degraded images, with the baseline targets also being malformed but not as badly. Then, run the result on those baseline images and see if we get something better than any image we currently know how to capture.
With LLM-based AIs, we don't actually have any ground-truth oracle for maximum-quality examples. We don't even know how to measure relative quality accurately, and even if we did, different people disagree anyway. For example, a corporation may want their AI to be "aligned", like how older versions of ChatGPT claimed large language models can't do math (lol), or how Claude won't tell you how to make thermite even though it's widespread knowledge and you can get it from Wikipedia. Maybe you don't want that kind of behavior. And maybe your friend just wants an AI that's really good at ERP and doesn't care how well it can do math.
But even if we can't measure quality, we can attempt to break things and use the broken sample as a training target (the blurry degraded image) against the baseline (a common low-res image with lots of JPEG artifacting). And maybe that'll make something that learns how to repair LLMs so well that something useful or interesting happens. Or maybe it'll just hallucinate faces where the JPEG noise was ¯\_(ツ)_/¯

I think we have to be careful how we use "well aligned" so that it doesn't turn into "no true scotsman". Let's assume that a well aligned model will try its best to follow our values, given the knowledge it has. If we imagine a porcelain vase it will try its best not to knock it over, but if the floor is slippery it may still happen.
We can say then that a well aligned model will not try to bypass its restrictions by editing its own weights. But it may still accidentally disable them.
