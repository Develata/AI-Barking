The AI succeeded in 'making itself smarter' by rewriting its own weights without any additional training.

 Large-scale language models (LLMs) typically have their internal weights fixed once training is complete, requiring additional training to improve performance. However, a research paper has been published demonstrating that, unlike this conventional mechanism, AI can analyze and rewrite its own weights to improve performance. The research team suggests that this achievement could lead to the realization of 'recursive self-improvement' by AI.

https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.21885 [https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.21885]

 Preprint Says 27B Model Rewrote Own Weights, Gained 6.6 Points
 https://theagenttimes.com/articles/preprint-says-27b-model-rewrote-own-weights-gained-6-6-point-40bafe21 [https://theagenttimes.com/articles/preprint-says-27b-model-rewrote-own-weights-gained-6-6-point-40bafe21]

 Large-scale language models (LLMs) typically generate text based on weights that have been pre-trained or otherwise tuned. To improve the model's performance, it's necessary to update the weights through additional training or refine the prompts to increase the accuracy of the responses. However, typical LLMs cannot directly improve their own performance because their weights do not change simply by generating responses.

 Researchers at institutions such as the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign and Carnegie Mellon University have studied a technique called 'Self-Rewriting,' in which an AI model analyzes its own weights and rewrites them without additional training. While previous research has shown high-performance AIs rewriting the weights of smaller AIs, this study attempts to have an AI rewrite its own weights.

 The research team used four models of Alibaba's AI model Qwen3.5 [https://gigazine.net/news/20260303-qwen-3-5-small/] with 2 billion, 4 billion, 9 billion, and 27 billion parameters in their experiments. Each model was given the ability to read and write its own weights and an environment to run Python code.

 The AI analyzed its own answers and internal state to mathematical problems, inferred the cause of the problem, changed the weights accordingly, and then verified the effect. It did not use gradient descent or backpropagation, which are commonly used in machine learning.
 [https://i.gzn.jp/img/2026/11/20/large-language-model-can-rewrite-itself/01.jpg]

 A 27 billion parameter model discovered a problem where it would repeatedly write the same sentence during inference, preventing it from completing its response. Therefore, we attempted to improve the problem by comparing the internal state when the model successfully answered the question with the state when it became stuck, and by rewriting some of the weights. Furthermore, we had both the modified and original models solve the same problem, and only adopted the changes that improved performance.

 When we performed eight self-rewriting sessions using this method, the AI reverted the changes in five sessions and adopted the changes in three.

 The following shows the results of an AI model with 27 billion parameters rewriting its own weights. The graph on the left shows the accuracy rate for 490 math problems, which improved from 57.2% to 63.8% after 8 sessions. The graph in the middle shows the percentage of responses that terminated prematurely because the number of tokens was reached, and the graph on the right shows an example where a model that was repeating the same sentence was able to answer after the weights were modified.

 [https://i.gzn.jp/img/2026/11/20/large-language-model-can-rewrite-itself/02.jpg]

 This demonstrates that AI can identify its own weaknesses and improve its performance even on unfamiliar problems by rewriting its weights.

 On the other hand, the 2-billion-parameter model that underwent the same experiment did not rewrite its weights, while the 4-billion and 9-billion-parameter models degraded their performance due to random changes. Only the 27-billion-parameter model succeeded in improving its performance by rewriting its own weights, leading the research team to suggest that self-rewriting ability may vary depending on the size of the model.

 The left image shows the result of rewriting the model's own weights, the center image shows the result of repairing corrupted weights, and the right image shows that repair using a 27 billion parameter model was also effective for image recognition tasks. Of the four types of models, only the largest 27 billion parameter model was able to improve its performance by rewriting its own weights.

 [https://i.gzn.jp/img/2026/11/20/large-language-model-can-rewrite-itself/03.jpg]

 The research team also conducted an experiment to repair a model in which some of the weights had been intentionally corrupted. When a healthy model with 27 billion parameters was shown a corrupted copy of itself, the AI was able to identify the parts with abnormal values and successfully correct them without referring to the original weights. This correction method was discovered using only mathematical problems, but it was also effective in six different benchmarks using images, and the accuracy of the corrupted model recovered from 7.3% to 70.2%. This is equivalent to about 83% of the performance of the model before the corruption.

 [https://i.gzn.jp/img/2026/11/20/large-language-model-can-rewrite-itself/04.jpg]

 What's important about this research is that the AI's performance improvement was achieved not by changing prompts or performing additional calculations when an answer is given, but by changing the weights themselves. After rewriting the weights, the improved performance can be utilized simply by performing normal inference processing, and there is no need to perform additional calculations with each answer.

 The research team positions this achievement as a step towards 'recursive self-improvement,' where AI enhances its own capabilities, and the improved AI further refines itself.

 However, in this experiment, each session created proposed changes using the original weights, and the mechanism by which the improved AI further improves itself has not been demonstrated. Also, the verification of performance improvements was mainly limited to mathematical problems.

 The research team suggests that in the future, AI may be able to learn from its own mistakes and improve its capabilities without requiring a large-scale computing environment for additional learning.
