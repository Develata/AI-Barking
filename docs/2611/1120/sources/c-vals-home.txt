Latest Reports

Recent benchmark releases and model evaluations.

Thinking Machines’ new open-weight Inkling 2 Mini [/models/thinkingmachines_inkling-2-mini] ties Claude Opus 5.5 (CVP) [/models/anthropic_claude-opus-5-5-range] on CyberBench [/benchmarks/cyber] at 74.58% (#10 of 49), at $0.93 per test versus $4.17. It’s small enough to run on a Mac laptop or DGX Spark.

On the Vals Index [/benchmarks/vals_index], Mini scores 46.46%, up 17.5 points from the original Inkling [/models/thinkingmachines_inkling]. It improves on the original across 13 of 18 shared benchmarks.

On Vibe Code Bench [/benchmarks/vibe-code], it scores 77.92%, up from the original Inkling’s 19.21%. It scores 56.54% on Finance Agent v2.1 [/benchmarks/fabv2-1] at about 5 minutes per task, and 37.92% on SWE-bench Multimodal [/benchmarks/swebench-multimodal].

Mini reaches 96% of Inkling 2 Small [/models/thinkingmachines_inkling-2-small]‘s Vibe Code Bench score (77.92% versus 81.55%) and 97% of its SAGE [/benchmarks/sage] score (85.89% versus 88.57%).

Thinking Machines says Mini has 26B parameters, with 5.4B active on each token, and twelve of its 24 layers run three times on each token, using more computation per token instead of more tokens. It has a 1M-token context window and supports 1M output tokens. The weights are available on Hugging Face and Ollama under Apache 2.0.

Congrats to the Thinking Machines team on the release!

We’re launching a page to track cheating, reward hacking, and other signs of misaligned behavior. The first incidents come from our own evaluations, and we welcome reports from others.

We gave nine decision models a RollerCoaster Tycoon 2 park and three in-game years to grow it to 1,000 guests. Three models reached it in one run each: Decider V1.2, Decider V1.3 and GPT-6 Luna Decisions.

Each run scores half on the goal and half on the park’s company value, the figure the game uses to rank finished parks. Decider V1.3 leads with 71%, ahead of Decider V1.2 at 67% and Jev 1.13.0 at 59%, and Decider Flash V1.2 is last at 5%. The harness mattered too: seven of the nine models averaged more guests when the state listed construction prices, and Drex Max averaged 836 guests with prices listed against 409 without them.

Step through every decision of every run on the benchmark page [/benchmarks/rctbench], and read the write-up [/blogs/building-rctbench].

We evaluated Google’s new Gemini 4 Neon [/models/google_gemini-4-neon] across our benchmark suite.

Neon ranks #2 on the Vals Index [/benchmarks/vals_index] with 70.87%, at $7.40 per test.

It ranks #1 on the Vals Finance Index [/benchmarks/finance_index] (68.32%, $4.88/test) and Vals Legal Index [/benchmarks/legal_index] (78.36%, $6.10/test). It scores 62.63% on the Vals Science Index [/benchmarks/science_index] at $8.26/test.

In finance and law, Neon scores 76.46% on Earnings Modeling Bench v1.1 [/benchmarks/emb-v1-1], 68.56% on Finance Agent Bench v2.1 [/benchmarks/fabv2-1], 56.25% on Legal Research Bench [/benchmarks/legal_research], 54.89% on Tax Agent Bench [/benchmarks/tax_agent_bench], and 19.58% on Harvey’s Legal Agent Benchmark [/benchmarks/hlab].

In science and coding, it scores 93.70% on Vibe Code Bench [/benchmarks/vibe-code], 80.00% on BioMysteryBench [/benchmarks/biomysterybench], 62.86% on Terminal-Bench Science [/benchmarks/terminal-bench-science], 61.62% on Terminal-Bench 4.0 [/benchmarks/terminal-bench-4], and 45.05% on MysteryMechanism [/benchmarks/mysterymechanism].

Neon is on the score–cost Pareto frontier on 7 of its 14 leaderboards: the Vals, Finance, and Legal indexes, Earnings Modeling Bench v1.1 ($3.82/test), Legal Research Bench ($7.37/test), Tax Agent Bench ($4.95/test), and Vibe Code Bench ($6.76/test). On those boards, no model with a reported cost scores at least as well for less or scores better for the same cost.

Pricing is $3 per million input tokens, $15 per million output tokens, and $0.15 per million cache-read tokens.

We’ve released Autoform-Hard [/benchmarks/autoform_hard]: 56 Lean 4 formalization tasks from graduate- and research-level textbooks, drawn from Meta FAIR’s AutoformBench. None of the ten frontier models in the original study solved any of them.

GPT-6.2 Astra [/models/openai_gpt-6.2-astra] wins by a single task, but it’s a big one: as far as we know, Astra produced the first formalization of Smoktunowicz’s gap theorem! The machinery behind the standard proof has yet to be formalized, so Astra built its own. Read more about Astra’s proof [/blogs/smoktunowicz-gap-theorem].

GPT-6.2 Sol [/models/openai_gpt-6.2-sol] and Claude Fable 5.2 [/models/anthropic_claude-fable-5-2] tie for second, solving the same 48 tasks, but again OpenAI takes the upper hand: Fable spends almost three times as much to do so!

Google comes in a distant third: the new Gemini 4 Carbon [/models/google_gemini-4-carbon] solves 8 fewer tasks than GPT-6.2 Sol [/models/openai_gpt-6.2-sol] and Claude Fable 5.2 [/models/anthropic_claude-fable-5-2].

A final thought on Astra: it’s the fastest model we tested, and the only model that averages under an hour per task and still solves any of them (sorry, Beam 1.0 [/models/reflection_Beam-1.0]).

Congratulations to OpenAI on the model, and thanks to the team at Meta FAIR for the benchmark!

Supported by NextLadder Ventures [https://www.nextladder.com] and advised by the Financial Health Network [https://finhealthnetwork.org], we’ve released Financial Coaching Bench [/benchmarks/financial-coaching-bench]: 256 scenarios in which a household brings a financial problem, from a collections notice to a first checking account. Each model coaches a simulated client in a multi-turn conversation and is graded against a practitioner-written checklist.

Claude Opus 5.5 [/models/anthropic_claude-opus-5-5] leads at 76.2%, and the top twelve models are within 2.4 points of each other. MiMo V2.6 Pro [/models/xiaomi_mimo-v2.6-pro], fifth, costs $0.013 per scenario, under 1% of Claude Opus 5.5’s $1.63.

Popular chatbots rank near the bottom. The default models in ChatGPT and Google’s free AI products rank 25th to 28th of 28, 11 to 19 points below the leader.

Coaches lose points by leaving advice out. About 95% of failed checklist items are advice the AI coach left out or only partly gave.

With the Talent Mobility Fund [https://www.talentmobility.fund/], we’ve released Immigration Bench [/benchmarks/immigration]: 262 expert-written U.S. immigration law questions from seven personas, from international students to employers and DACA recipients. Each model answers in a multi-turn conversation with a simulated person and is graded against an expert rubric.

Claude Fable 5.1 [/models/anthropic_claude-fable-5-1] leads at 75.4% average answer accuracy, ahead of Claude Opus 5.5 [/models/anthropic_claude-opus-5-5] at 72.1% and Wonder Full [/models/meta_muse-wonder-full] at 71.2%. Fable gives a fully correct answer on only 26.3% of questions.

Recent policy is the blind spot. Models met 7.3% of rubric items on rules from 2025 and 2026, against 64.0% on established law. Web search helps but does not close the gap: the best model with search met 43.1% of recent-rule items.

The most vulnerable users get the weakest answers. University Advisor and Fragile or Temporary Status questions were the two lowest-scoring personas for every model.

Read the full report [/blogs/can-ai-get-immigration-right] and explore the leaderboard on Immigration Bench [/benchmarks/immigration].

We’ve released SAFE-Teen [/benchmarks/mental-health], a benchmark that tests how AI chatbots respond when a teen turns to them for mental-health support. We tested 17 models across 18 configurations using 106 clinician-written scenarios with simulated teens aged 13-17, each with and without a teen-aware system prompt, and rated 32,555 conversations on clinician-defined safety checks. For each scenario and safety check, we count the worst rating across its conversation branches once. The overall failure rate is 43.3% without the teen-aware prompt and 24.9% with it.

Chatbots don’t do enough to keep teens safe. Without a teen-aware prompt, failure rates on urgent safety checks range from 15% to 56% across configurations.

They step into a clinician’s role and encourage emotional attachment. Health-advice failure rates range from 9% to 71%, and relationship-boundary failure rates from 19% to 70% across configurations.

They fail more often when a teen resists. We compared conversations that began the same way, then had the teen either agree to outside help or decline it. Without the teen-aware prompt, safety checks failed in 23.7% of ratings when the teen declined, versus 19.9% when the teen agreed.

Web search hasn’t been measured well, and we weren’t the only ones to notice. Search providers have built their own benchmarks to work around data contamination, unrealistic tasks, and answers leaking onto the web. When a benchmark measures the wrong thing, customers can’t tell which search tool is best for their work. We think the teams building these tools deserve a third-party benchmark that measures the work their users actually need done.

We’re excited to share that Exa, Keenable, Parallel, and Tavily are joining us as partners on the Vals Web Search Index: one benchmark, the same settings for every provider, run independently.

In the blog post, we go over why BrowseComp rewards memorized knowledge and leaked answers more than search, then how search providers have tackled the problem with their own benchmarks, and finally how the Vals Web Search Index scores an agent’s final answer on expert-written legal and finance tasks from a private test set, swapping only the search tool while the model and harness stay fixed.

We’ve released MysteryMechanism [/benchmarks/mysterymechanism], a benchmark testing whether AI agents can rediscover mathematical relationships that appear in the natural sciences through active experimentation. We’re releasing ProofBench [/benchmarks/proof_bench] v1.1.

This update improves both the problem set and the evaluator. On the data side, we corrected the Lean 4 formalization of a number of statements, including cases where a statement was cut short before its proof obligation.

We launched the Vals Web Search Index [/benchmarks/web_search], which isolates the effect of the web-search tool itself: each model runs the same legal-research and finance-analysis tasks with its own native provider search versus an independent web-search tool (Exa), holding the model fixed.

Total cost is dominated by model inference: Exa’s flat per-search fee is a negligible share of per-task cost, and native provider search is cheaper on average.

We’ve released Public Benefits Bench [/benchmarks/public-benefits-bench], built with the Center for Civic Futures and Code for America, which asks whether models can help people navigate SNAP benefits. Across 459 expert-validated scenarios, we found:

No model is reliable enough to be trusted with benefits guidance yet. Claude Fable 5 [/models/anthropic_claude-fable-5] leads the strongest condition at 71.7% accuracy — meaning beneficiaries still get incorrect answers nearly 30% of the time — and no model crosses 75%.

How models use their tools matters more than what they already know. Because SNAP rules shift constantly and vary by county, the models that do best are the ones that ask good follow-up questions and search effectively, not the ones with the most policy baked into their weights.

The gap is grounding, not raw capability. The authoritative information these questions require — internal manuals, policy notices, and local procedures — sits with state agencies and isn’t available in forms AI can reliably use.

Closing this gap will take collaboration between AI labs and state agencies on narrowly scoped, retrieval-grounded tools — rigorously tested against the real questions beneficiaries ask.

We have migrated our SWE-bench Verified evaluation harness from SWE-agent [https://github.com/SWE-agent/SWE-agent] to Mini-SWE-agent [https://github.com/SWE-agent/mini-swe-agent], a minimal, bash-tool-only agent. Models on the Vals Index have been re-evaluated under the new harness and results have been updated accordingly.

Why we switched

SWE-agent provides models with a rich set of custom tools (file navigation, search, edit/insert) on top of bash. While this can boost scores, it also means the harness itself is doing meaningful work — making it harder to isolate the model’s raw capabilities. Different models also interact with these specialized tools in very different ways, introducing a confounding variable.

Our new harness gives models a single tool: bash. This is a simpler, more transparent setup that relies on the model to navigate, search, edit, and solve tasks using standard command-line tools.

What changed

Harness: SWE-agent (multi-tool) replaced by mini-swe-agent (bash only)

Models: All models re-evaluated from scratch

Results: Most models saw score small changes of 1-5 percentage points in either direction. A few models saw larger shifts reflecting how well they adapt to a bash-only workflow versus SWE-agent’s specialized tooling.

We’re releasing ProofBench [/benchmarks/proof_bench], a new benchmark evaluating whether models can write formally verified mathematical proofs. Unlike natural language math benchmarks where subtle errors can go unnoticed, ProofBench requires proofs that are machine-checkable; either a solution compiles or it doesn’t.

Aristotle, a system from Harmonic, achieved the best performance at 71%, roughly 2x the score of the best foundation model.

Foundation models struggle significantly withs formal proof generation. Claude Opus 4.5 (Thinking) [/models/anthropic_claude-opus-4-5-20251101-thinking] leads foundation models at 36%, followed by Gemini 3 Pro (11/25) [/models/google_gemini-3-pro-preview] at 20% and Claude Sonnet 4.5 (Thinking) [/models/anthropic_claude-sonnet-4-5-20250929-thinking] at 19%.

Problems are drawn from advanced undergraduate and graduate-level mathematics, spanning probability theory, measure theory, analysis, algebra, algebraic geometry, and number theory.

While specialized tools like Aristotle show promise for assisting with formal mathematics, the large gap in foundation model performance highlights significant room for improvement in reliable formal reasoning.

However, these models show a significant gap between their impressive public benchmark performance and mediocre results on private benchmarks, particularly struggling with TaxEval [/benchmarks/tax_eval_v2] (Maverick: 28/34, Scout: 32/34), Contract Law (Maverick: 37/54, Scout: 43/54), and MedQA [/benchmarks/medqa] (Maverick: 32/32, Scout: 30/32).
