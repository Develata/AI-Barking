Unforced Euler singularity, unforced Navier-Stokes singularity, and the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis

20 November, 2026 in math.AP [https://terrytao.wordpress.com/category/mathematics/mathap/], math.NT [https://terrytao.wordpress.com/category/mathematics/mathnt/] | Tags: AI [https://terrytao.wordpress.com/tag/ai/], incompressible Euler equations [https://terrytao.wordpress.com/tag/incompressible-euler-equations/], Levent Alpöge [https://terrytao.wordpress.com/tag/levent-alpoge/], Matei Coiculescu [https://terrytao.wordpress.com/tag/matei-coiculescu/], Navier-Stokes equations [https://terrytao.wordpress.com/tag/navier-stokes-equations/], Riemann hypothesis [https://terrytao.wordpress.com/tag/riemann-hypothesis/], Tristan Buckmaster [https://terrytao.wordpress.com/tag/tristan-buckmaster/] | by Terence Tao [https://terrytao.wordpress.com/author/teorth/] 

Obviously these results will be reported widely in other outlets, but I will note them here, in decreasing order of how “digested” the results currently are:

Levent Alpöge, Tristan Buckmaster, and Matei Coiculescu, with heavy AI assistance, have written a human-readable proof of the finite time blowup for the unforced incompressible Euler equations in three dimensions [https://cims.nyu.edu/~tristanb/cc.pdf], which is formalized in Lean here [https://github.com/mcoiculescu/EulerBlowupPublic].

Levent Alpöge and Tristan Buckmaster, again with heavy AI usage, have generated a proof of the finite time blowup for the unforced incompressible Navier Stokes equations in three dimensions, again with a Lean formalization [https://github.com/tristanbuckmaster/navier-stokes/]. Here, the paper is technically human-readable and the general strategy of proof is broadly clear (following one of the expected paths to this result, namely to numerically build an approximate discretely self-similar solution and then use rigorous perturbation theory to convert this to an exact solution); but more work needs to be done to create a proper, professionally written proof.

Levent Alpöge, using an internal AI model, has generated an (enormous) Lean-only proof of the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis. This Lean Zulip thread [https://leanprover.zulipchat.com/#narrow/channel/113488-general/topic/Discussion.3A.20Alp.C3.B6ge.20Proves.20the.20Riemann.20Hypothesis] is probably the best place to follow the efforts to verify the proof and extract the strategy of proof (which, from very initial analysis, appears to use the full power of modern developments on the Langlands program and arithmetic geometry). (UPDATE: the moderators of the Lean thread are now focusing the discussion on the Lean-related aspects of the proof only.) I imagine the digestion process will take some time, though in this case at least we have a human author to communicate with to assist with this task.

See also Buckmaster’s Mastodon post [https://mastodon.social/@tristanbuckmaster/117649035783718539] regarding the first two results.

Science Markets [https://science.markets]

Thank you for the ordering by how digested the results are. That ordering is the whole story, and it is worth being explicit about why.

For the first two results the digestion path is already visible: human-readable proof, strategy broadly clear, Lean formalisation on top. A reader can form a judgement in an afternoon. The third has no such path yet. A Lean-only artifact of that size is a different kind of object, and the question “is it right” splits into questions that resolve on very different timescales: does the headline statement in Lean say what we mean by GRH; does the archive compile against a known-good Lean with only standard axioms; is the strategy something a number theorist recognises as a proof. The Zulip thread can answer the second relatively quickly. The third is the one that has always taken years.

The two precedents are both uncomfortable. Wiles announced in 1993, the gap surfaced within months, and the repair took until 1995. Mochizuki published in a refereed journal in 2012 and the people who work on the problem still do not accept it. In neither case could the answer be read off the artifact.

Which makes the interesting question not “is it true” but “what observable event would settle it, and who is entitled to say so”. For anyone trying to price this rather than just follow it, writing that down before the claim exists is the only version that is worth anything. Written afterwards it collapses into an argument about credibility, which is precisely the failure mode this whole episode is about.

Disclosure: I work on https://science.markets [https://science.markets], which runs a market on this question. The resolution rule is fixed before the market opens and cannot be edited: https://science.markets/markets/riemann-zeta-critical-line/ [https://science.markets/markets/riemann-zeta-critical-line/] — it requires a complete unconditional proof in ZFC, independently checked with no unresolved gap, and it says in advance that finite numerical verification does not count and that independence from ZFC leaves it unresolved. Being forced to write down what does not count was the useful part.

Terence Tao [http://www.math.ucla.edu/~tao]

The conventional wisdom has been that purely analytic methods are not sufficient to establish RH, as it is too easy to perturb the zeta function to move a few zeroes off of the critical line (cf. the resolution of Newman’s conjecture on the de Bruijn-Newman constant). As with the Landau-Siegel problem (the resolution of which is a corollary of GRH), the main promising routes have been automorphic methods, algebraic number theory methods, or arithmetic geometry methods; the latter were the key for instance to Deligne’s resolution of the function field analogue of RH (the Weil conjectures). It is very early days but it seems like the AI-generated proof uses all of these inputs. There has also been the wildcard of “F_1” approaches, as proposed by Alain Connes; the new Masullo manuscript apparently goes down this route to some extent, but it will take some time to digest that manuscript also. [EDIT: on closer inspection, the Masullo manuscript is closer to Deninger’s strategy towards RH than Connes’, although the two approaches have some similarities (though neither is close to being concretely fleshed out).]

A brief disclaimer: this is meant as a bit of a polemic. Someone has to say it out loud, and it is probably better coming from inside the house.

The recent wave of formal Lean proofs from Anthropic—culminating in the claim regarding the Riemann Hypothesis—marks the end of pure mathematics as a level playing field.

For centuries, math was the ultimate equalizer: a mind with a chalkboard had the same tools as a professor at Princeton. Now, it looks much more like Formula 1. You can be the best driver in the world, but if a competitor works for a factory team and gets to drive the unreleased prototype—the car the public won’t see for another two years—they are going to set the track record.

An objective look at recent results makes it hard to argue that narrow human mastery is still the main bottleneck. Levent Alpöge has been resolving long-standing conjectures in fields well outside his own specialization. While steering these systems clearly requires mathematical maturity, the fact that one researcher can break open unfamiliar subfields shows that the underlying engine is now compensating for—and in some cases outperforming—decades of niche human expertise.

To be clear, the fact that a machine can prove theorems is not surprising in itself. We have always known that mathematics can be formalized, and in principle, a computer could stumble upon a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis purely by luck. In that sense, it is not fundamentally different from chess—just vastly more complex, where trying every possible path of formulas is far too demanding to be feasible. What is genuinely astonishing is not that computers can prove deep results, but how rapidly the technology has evolved to navigate that immense search space and consistently choose viable paths toward a proof.

There is, however, a gentle historical irony in how we got here. For generations, parts of our community maintained a quiet intellectual hierarchy. In textbook margins and conference coffee breaks, we have all heard the playful—and sometimes not-so-playful—jokes about physicists, computer scientists, and especially engineers, portraying them as a bit sloppy or heuristic-driven. It was always a slightly misplaced sense of superiority; step outside our own technical niches and talk to most of us about any everyday topic, and we are rarely any more insightful than anyone else.

Yet look at who actually built the machine now tackling the pinnacle of pure mathematical rigor: not a department of 500 pure mathematicians, but a lab guided by physicists’ empirical scaling laws, designed by computer scientists, and built by thousands of veteran software, systems, and hardware engineers—who then handed the keys to a handful of mathematicians.

In fact, perhaps the most positive outcome of this shift is that AI is bringing a healthy dose of humility back to the mathematical community. Watching empirical engineering and large-scale compute break into what we long treated as the exclusive fortress of pure human genius is a grounding, and probably overdue, reality check.

Pure mathematics is no longer a solo marathon of raw intellect. It has become a constructors’ championship—and the most urgent question for academia is how open research survives when the fastest engines remain behind corporate doors.

P.S. To be clear, this isn’t directed at Terence Tao or mathematicians in general—only at that small, overly elitist corner of the culture we have all encountered at some point. I write this as an established research mathematician with no personal axe to grind—just someone who has always known that an engineer can possess the exact same deductive power as any brilliant mathematician; it is only the methods and the language that differ.
