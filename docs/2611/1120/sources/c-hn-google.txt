K-12 IT employee here, I lurk HN a lot and made an account to throw in my 2 cents.
Anyway, both are generally true. My district uses a hub model, where support staff are dispatched out to schools and network infrastructure/business ops are centrally managed. But our model is common to larger districts; smaller ones often rely on small general-purpose IT teams - maybe a handful of people.
Obviously, being both public-sector and education, the pay isn't great because the funding isn't there. I like to think most of my colleagues are competent, service-minded people, we're just understaffed. We don't usually experience a lot of turnover, just growth from 2020-2024, but the economy has made some folks reluctantly move on lately. I'm hiring now and the strongest candidates withdrew when they realized what our salaries were. We're in a metropolitan area - rural districts have even weaker candidate pools and lower funding.
My district's leadership has embraced AI as part of our students' future, so they're taking an "allow it, but regulate it" approach and Google's change doesn't impact us. I have mixed feelings about how tech is integrated in grade schools in general, but this _is_ something of an added burden to workaround for under-resourced districts who don't want it.

They need 50% + 1 percent of the bargaining unit. Including others (vendors, extended workforce, contractors) in that not only makes it so that you need more people, but also that those individuals have conflicting goals which the union cannot represent (FTEs may have a workplace issue that conflicts with vendors).
As such, it cannot be a union registered with the NRLB and so will never have the bargaining power.
If it was instead a single group (e.g. subcontractors working for Google Fiber) then it would be easier to get the 50% vote and would have a single focus and could be a union that has collective bargaining power.
