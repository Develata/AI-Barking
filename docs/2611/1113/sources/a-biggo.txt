Claude Deciphers Gödel Manuscripts as Helsinki Team Unearths 663 Proto-Theorems

Add preferred source [https://www.google.com/preferences/source?q=finance.biggo.com]

1

A team at Finland's University of Helsinki used Anthropic's Claude model to decipher 16 working notebooks — 1,009 pages in all — that logician Kurt Gödel wrote in German shorthand in the 1940s. The model extracted 663 proto-theorems, roughly 400 of which were confirmed valid through formal verification in Coq. The manuscripts also yielded an early framework for a proof of the independence of the Axiom of Choice, predating Paul Cohen's forcing method by more than two decades, though a definitional error kept it from being published. Scholars remain divided over who deserves historical credit for the reconstructed proof.

A research team at the University of Helsinki in Finland has used Anthropic's Claude artificial intelligence model to complete a machine reading of 16 working notebooks that logician Kurt Gödel wrote in the 1940s. The notebooks, 1,009 pages in total, were written in a German shorthand system long since fallen out of everyday use and had never been published. The team extracted 663 results with the makings of theorems, of which about 400 were confirmed valid by the formal verification system Coq, about 200 passed after supplementary preconditions were added, and about 50 were judged to be erroneous.

The project's origins trace back to 2017. Jan von Plato, a logician who had turned 60 and faced mandatory retirement, decided to take on the "indecipherable book" and secured a €2.5 million (approximately $2.8 million) grant from the European Research Council. He taught himself Gabelsberger shorthand from scratch, and the team spent several years transcribing the shorthand into modern English page by page. The real difficulty came after transcription: Gödel's notes are highly compressed and leap forward rapidly, packed with intuitive sketches and undeveloped formulas, and manual study progressed at roughly one page per week.

The turning point came this summer at an academic conference in France devoted to "artificial intelligence and theorem proving." Scholars at the meeting suggested the team try Claude. Von Plato then fed the 1,009-page transcription, six monographs he had written himself and a large body of modern logic literature into the model. Within days, the model produced a report of roughly 650 pages, which expanded to 1,084 pages as follow-up questions were posed.

In a lecture at the University of Vienna, von Plato described the tool as a "digital monster." He noted that when humans read a long document, they have often forgotten page 10 by the time they reach page 500, whereas Gödel's thoughts across more than a thousand pages are "present simultaneously" before the model, allowing it to connect insights and formulas separated by hundreds of pages.

The most closely watched findings center on the independence of the Axiom of Choice. Paul Cohen proved the axiom's independence in 1963 using forcing and won the Fields Medal in 1966. The model reconstructed from Gödel's manuscripts a mathematical framework equivalent to Cohen's method, dating more than two decades earlier. The proof in the manuscripts contained a one-line error in a definition of "finite support," and the model recovered the correct definition from another of Gödel's notebooks and rebuilt the proof on that basis, which ultimately passed Coq verification.

The manuscripts also contain a number of logic results that were only rediscovered by other scholars in the 1970s and 1980s, such as the independence of constructive axioms and a strong normalization proof in functional interpretation. The team also catalogued 14 new topics that no one has yet studied.

The notes on physics and cosmology are equally striking. The model found that Gödel had already calculated state changes in coupled systems after measurement in the mid-1930s, using the logic of what became known as Lüders' rule — a rule not formally proposed by physicist Gerhart Lüders until 1951. The end of the manuscripts also contains derivations on rotating universes and closed timelike curves, as well as ideas for statistically testing cosmic rotation using astronomical observation data.

The results have also sparked debate over academic credit. Joel David Hamkins, a professor of logic at the University of Notre Dame, noted that a reconstructed proof passing formal verification and recognizing it as a historical achievement are two different questions. By academic convention, work unpublished during an author's lifetime rarely receives historical recognition; even if a draft contains ideas resembling later proofs, that only shows Gödel was thinking along the same path, not that he had completed a proof. Moreover, existing verification tools cannot precisely distinguish which parts of the reconstructed proof are Gödel's original ideas and which come from the model's capacity to fill gaps.

From a methodological standpoint, the project demonstrates a path for large language models working in tandem with formal provers: the model maintains context across extremely long texts and extracts chains of logic, while the formal tools weed out erroneous content the model may generate. The team said its next step is to apply the same method to Gödel's unorganized notes on physics and cosmology.
