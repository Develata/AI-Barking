A new system of accreditation

Andrew Ng [https://clandrei.github.io/], PhD student at the University of Bonn [https://www.math.uni-bonn.de/]

SERIES · AI is making papers cheap to produce but they remain costly to referee. Proofs and Prompts is publishing a series of posts from 26 October on the future of publishing, papers and credit. Questions such as how to disclose AI use, who bears the cost of checking, what authorship means and what papers of the future might look like will be addressed. This post is part of the series. Each is written independently and reflects only its authors’ views. All posts in the series [https://proofsandprompts.com/tag/publishing/]

It has become clear in recent years, and especially after the advent of LLMs, that the current publication system of journals is untenable and inequitable. I think this is an excellent opportunity to reform the system, so let me outline a suggestion.

I propose the following: the main way that a mathematician obtains “credit” for new results should be by writing publications which are at the level of doctoral theses and defending them in front of the mathematical community. The community can then give this publicly available reviews. Hiring committees then look at someone’s list of such publications, taking into account the community’s comments to judge quality if they so desire, and make hiring decisions based on that.

Concretely, how this would work is the following. Every year, everybody who has defended a PhD thesis has the right to ask for a defence of one of their preprints. (PhD theses are defended the same way as before.)

These are not submitted to any journals; we abolish journals by agreeing that nothing which is received from the 1st of January 2029 will be refereed or quick opinioned or given any of the unpaid and unacknowledged labour that journals need to function. In place of journals, some sort of international organisation, such as the IMU, receives requests from mathematicians to defend a preprint, which is uploaded to said organisation’s website and/or the arXiv. For simplicity’s sake let’s pretend that the IMU does this so I don’t have to write international organisation, but I want to emphasise that it need not be the IMU or any existing organisation: it could be a new thing entirely. The IMU then appoints someone to examine the publication, akin to how examiners examine doctoral theses. Theses should be tagged with keywords/ MSC codes etc, and all mathematicians who have defended a thesis or publication in related areas have the right to rate this by writing comments on a system hosted by the IMU’s server. These are publicly available and must be tied to the reviewing mathematician somehow. For instance, one might have to use ORCID to access this system, but the ORCID would not be publicly available (probably only to the examiner, if at all). The criteria used could be elegance of the argument, novelty of the techniques, and depth, as measured by establishing connections to other problems which are not obviously related or connections to other areas of mathematics.1 [#fn-5e352a5e-e71c-4334-a0d9-d41699757c69]

After a period of at least 3 months (also subject to debate, but some time so that there is enough time for others to read the publication and for the author incorporate comments, corrections etc.), a defence of the preprint should take place, broadcast online so that other people may take part. The purpose of this is for the author to convince the community of the correctness and interest of the results in the preprint, and to explain the proof/methods to all those who are interested. I cannot stress the importance of the latter enough: it is imperative that we cure ourselves of Hardy’s disease2 [#fn-8c917b79-a056-4315-a412-98ddc2a97027]. All present should additionally rate the defence on quality of exposition. At the end of the defence, the examiner should compile the opinions of the mathematicians who have rated the preprint, decide whether the defence was successful, and write a longer report. If successful, the preprint is published along with the examiner’s report and all the opinions of others who have rated them. The quality of a publication is judged by the examiner’s report and the collective opinion of the mathematicians who have rated it, but there are no more or less prestigious “places” to defend since all defences are centrally administered. This sort of publication is what is considered in the “proving results” part of hiring/promotion decisions.

Why do this, and how do other existing practices change/adapt?

This may sound like a lot of work. That’s because it is. However, I believe that in the age of generative LLMs a publication should merit this amount of work in order to be deserving of publication. As we all know by now, just having a proof of some theorem is not indicative of anything. It should form part of some larger body of work that points to new directions in research, and that is what, allegedly, a doctoral thesis is meant to be.

Since this is a lot of work for the examiner in particular, I believe that in career-related decisions, committees should weight examining a publication as highly as defending one. Examiners should be given credit for their work regardless of acceptance. Since the examiner is supposed to understand the publication well, responsibility for explanation and exposition of the results in the publication should fall on both the author and the examiner.

I think this proposal has a reasonable claim to stopping the arms race in publishing papers. I have heard senior people lament that students are now pressured to publish papers earlier and earlier, and that has the effect that often students don’t think about things as deeply as they used to. The proposed system incentivises everybody, not just PhD students, to think deeply about whatever mathematics they are engaged in. To prevent people from just shoving random results together and calling that a preprint, I suggest that we give the examiners the right to say that the results of a preprint are incoherent and there is no unifying theme. I would also say that preprints can be defended jointly if most of the results were obtained in collaboration, but the preprint should count towards the quota of all the authors.

I believe that a defence where one can grill the speaker is much better for communicating understanding/expositional purposes. I agree with Frank Calegari here [https://galoisrepresentations.org/2026/09/17/ways-to-improve-mathematics-an-introduction/] that “the median mathematics seminar could (perhaps harshly) be described as a waste of time for both the participants and the speaker”. In talks where I am actually interested in the speaker’s results, I have found the introductory spiel to be uninspiring, and to take up too much time so that the new results are only very cursorily alluded to. I cannot press the speaker on the details of their methods, because this could be considered heckling, derail the conference schedule, and disturb their flow. Consequently, I get nothing out of the talk and have to resort to pestering the speaker afterwards. I think if we really wanted to get anything more than a superficial acquaintance with the speaker’s work out of a talk, that talk has to resemble a doctoral defence more than it does a conference talk in the current model.

What of the arXiv? I think this is a great endeavour and we should work to preserve it. I think people should continue uploading preprints to the arXiv, and that the authors of a preprint have the right to use the results in it in a defence and call those “their results” for the next two years3 [#fn-1024b057-1511-4993-a3ef-bf022adcc301] after uploading. This is so that people can stake claims to results and not get scooped while they work on building their narrative. After the two year period, the results should be considered as folklore4 [#fn-7563f04d-2b49-4e7d-a177-c35c9a2cad26]: they can be used by anyone in a defence. The purpose of having an expiry date at all is this: some people choose not to fully explain or clean up their arguments, sometimes never publishing them. This results in an undesirable situation: obviously someone should do this work so that it can be verified, digested, and incorporated into the community’s repertoire, but there is currently no incentive to do this, since it would be, generally speaking, unacknowledged labour. Having an expiry date rewards someone who is willing to put in the effort to understand the mathematics in an orphaned preprint and integrate it into the community. This also deals with the issue of AI company produced preprints. Let’s take the construction of a complex structure on S6S^6 as an example. Engel took the initial 100 page preprint and turned it into a much more readable 20 page preprint (although I tried to read it, it is still beyond me since I don’t have the background in complex geometry, but I am told it is much better). In my proposed system, the result is his to claim, and I think this is fair.

While we are on the topic of reforming the system, I would also like to propose that the IMU have a dedicated branch towards public outreach and exposition, and substantial contributions towards this goal should be weighted the same as contributions such as defending or examining publications. Converting mathematics into something that the general public can appreciate is very hard work which deserves to be recognised. The public has funded our research and they deserve to get something out of it as well. If we do not want to rely on the blood money of the finance and tech companies and be beholden to their whims, we must convince the public that what we do is of value to them5 [#fn-db8b71fe-1ac4-422b-beef-110d9dd5401e]. I admit that I do not know how best to do this, but there are many people who have experience with successfully engaging with the public, and that expertise should be tapped.

I have dreamt up this system based on the assumption that it is depth of understanding that we want to preserve and encourage. I would like to address the concern that this is somehow unfair to the people who prefer problem solving, and I will say what I mean using combinatorics as an example. I think that it is fine to enjoy problem solving. I do. But I think that when doing research, especially in this day and age, just solving the problem isn’t enough. One should find better and/or alternative ways of solving the problem6 [#fn-73540454-3950-4d1a-8571-0906af9bf6d8], and try to hit on methods/techniques/heuristics which apply more generally. For instance, Turan’s theorem has a number of different proofs, many of which generalise to other problems or hint at more general theory which can be explored. If, in a hypothetical universe which had adopted this system, somebody had discovered what we call Turan’s theorem, then what they could do next is solve more Turan like problems and publish a treatise on forbidden subgraphs. Similarly, Erdos’ introduction of the probabilistic method to bound Ramsey numbers is an ingenious and extremely elegant argument, and one which is taught to many undergraduates for good reason: it generalises. If someone had discovered the probabilistic method, they could then prove some number of other results that were previously inaccessible and then write a publication on this method and how great and good it is. I have been taught, and have taught others, that solving problems helps you solve more problems. Research papers should show this in action.

LLMs

I have thus far been a bit coy about the exact status of LLMs in mathematics in this proposed universe. Only time will tell, but from my perspective the current unregulated system poses an existential risk comparable to that of nuclear weapons, if not worse. In my opinion, private companies absolutely should not be allowed to train any sort of large language model or AI. I agree with others who believe that such LLMs, trained on so much public data, should also be a public good whose benefits the public should reap7 [#fn-2502760a-2db2-4b8f-9ee8-7d85c7599d8c]. If we do believe that LLMs should continue to exist and play an important role, then they should be set up in such a way that it is accessible to as large a proportion of the public as we can manage, run on sustainable energy, and not have data centres popping up left right and centre. Mathematicians would then have access to this (open source) LLM. Perhaps access to LLMs/tokens/queries could be allocated in a similar way computing time on supercomputer clusters is. I have added this partly because, at a Maths and AI panel in Bonn, a panelist asks whether we would be willing to accept access to a slightly weaker model than what the AI companies have, and someone responded it depends on how we are assessed as junior mathematicians. I believe that the proposed system mitigates, to some extent, the inevitable difference between levels of AI access across different institutions.

There is also the massive uncertainty of what LLMs will be capable of in the future. They will, with high probability, be able to formalise arguments without much difficulty, so I have not paid much attention to verifying the formal correctness of preprints. I assume this will be dealt with as the times change.

There is still the issue of whether LLMs could generate publications at the level of doctoral theses that seem to be as good as human ones and have viewpoints which we find aesthetically agreeable. It would be foolish to bet against this, and I do not know how to get around this (yet), but I find Tarik Aougab statement from a previous post [https://proofsandprompts.com/2026/09/23/navigating-the-ai-crisis-a-humble-guide-for-students/] appealing: ” By definition, AI cannot be better than humans in the activity of human beings talking to one another. So insofar as humans sharing ideas with other humans is an essential part of what it means for mathematics to exist, this question is actually beside the point. In fact, there is nothing automatically wrong or bad about a human using an AI to help themselves understand some piece of mathematics in order to then go and explain it in a new or interesting way to another human or group of humans. But this is only doable at all if human mathematicians learn to value– and even reward – conversation.”.

I have a bit more to say about this sort of question/objection. First of all, I believe that, as Uri Bader said in an earlier post, maths just exists. The Poincare conjecture and Fermat’s Last Theorem have always both been true, but the point of research in these directions was to uncover why they are true, what structure lies beneath, and how they are connected to a great many other, often unexpected, questions in mathematics. And then communicate all of this to other people so that they might further build on that and learn even more about the mathematical universe. The Book of God has been written since the dawn of time and just because someone/something else can read it doesn’t that any individual or community has to stop trying to read it.

Second of all, suppose that tomorrow some alien race came to Earth and said that they are technologically much more advanced so we puny Earthlings should cede control over all our institutions, stop all research and scientific endeavours, and just do what they tell us. If this happened in a film and the people collectively shrugged their shoulders and said ok, I think most viewers would rightly call those people idiots. Now that this is happening in real life8 [#fn-4f8a3a63-78d9-4498-aaa9-502f1c9de6ec], I don’t think it is wise or prudent of us to leave our fates in the hands of people or technology of dubious9 [#fn-b0e79e27-7819-413d-85ec-f55a7e685ff3] trustworthiness.

Training students

On the topic of training students, I think very little has to change if we adopt these community norms. We can still teach and test for understanding, creativity, quality of exposition, choice of direction/questions, perseverance etc. All of the things which the system claimed to teach before the advent of LLMs. Perhaps some amount of work would have to go into thinking about how best to integrate LLMs into one’s workflow, but that is a relatively minor issue.

I would also like to address the oft-repeated analogy with chess, as somebody who, in a past life, spent a lot of time playing and studying chess. As other commentators have pointed out, chess is fundamentally different to maths: it is a finite game, played as a competition between two players. Maths is not finite, as far as I can tell, and it is not a competition in my view: it is a search for the truth. When chess engines arrived, this changed the game in the sense that the best player is no longer a human being, but it is still as enjoyable as it was before, and competitions are still between human players. The existence of extremely powerful LLMs impacts the field of maths differently, of course, but for illuminating which parts of the infinite search space we, as humans, find interesting, it seems to me that humans should be the ones to decide.

The most important lesson to be learned from chess, however, is when it comes to training students. The reader may already know this, but chess players have never ever improved by just reading what the chess engines spit out. Coaches always tell students to think about positions without an engine first, and only after a fair amount of thought maybe turn on the engine to see what they have missed. For all intents and purposes, the most powerful chess engines are oracles, and there are correct ways to use them to improve. I believe the same applies to mathematical oracles too, and it is up to the individual mathematician to figure out what the correct way is10 [#fn-4accaf9d-009e-4678-af5f-2066919798e2], but we cannot possibly hope to do so in the current journal system which has such perversely distorted incentives.

2. Criticisms, Drawbacks, Alternatives

I admit that this proposal is far from perfect. For instance, there is still a fair amount of subjectivity and bias in this system, but I don’t see how we could eliminate that completely if we insist on a large human aspect of the profession. I don’t know if we want to argue that we can/should eliminate subjectivity completely. This proposal is very much inspired by thinking about how academia in other disciplines works: in computer science papers are defended at conferences, but they also have the problem of slop, so one must raise the bar somehow, and I have opted for something similar to the practice of writing books that is commonplace in the humanities.

In earlier conversations about a version of this proposal I was told that I was insanely optimistic and had never experienced a system where widespread corruption was the norm11 [#fn-b3d495fd-a29c-4841-b32b-51d3d7b9f4f5]. This is because I suggested that there should additionally be numerical ratings for the judging criteria, and that the person attached to ratings/comments should be public. There are good reasons for having anonymous refereeing: to prevent “you scratch my back, I scratch yours” kind of behaviour, and because people might feel uncomfortable giving their peers honest negative feedback if their name is attached to it12 [#fn-c3425ff2-2f8d-4d3f-a12c-477b516bed53]. It is still possible that it is worth having numerical ratings which are only available to the examiner, but many people have pointed out to me the drawbacks of having explicit numerical ratings that are public, such as these being one-dimensional metrics which committees will use instead of considering mathematicians holistically, or this devolving into a social media-esque popularity contest.

As for choosing the examiners, in this industry it is usually an open secret who is corrupt or who doesn’t get along, so this can be avoided when choosing examiners, who I believe really should be publicly known. This is of course very much up for debate, but I think that journals deciding what to publish based on the opinions of a couple of arbitrarily chosen referees and a handful of overworked editors who often aren’t that related to the area of the submitted manuscript is insane and fertile ground for corruption. There is also the concern that this puts too much burden on the ‘senior’ people. I would imagine that the examiner doesn’t have to be the most senior person in the field: anybody who is competent and objective would do perfectly well, just as postdocs referee papers currently.

I have gotten multiple questions on the issue of how to assess the standards of examiners’ work. I think this has to be because their identity and the report that they write are public. The argument that anonymous refereeing is objective is, in my opinion, dubious since it offers no way of ensuring that the referee did their job properly. I also expect that no person will be allocated the task of examining more than once or twice a year, so there shouldn’t be any incentive to do a bad job to collect more ‘examining credits’.

I was asked an interesting question that I had not thought of: if I insist that every publication be at the level/scope of a doctoral thesis, what happens to small/cute results that don’t manage to get used/fit into some narrative? My suggestion is that they simply get left as preprints on the arXiv. It seems to me impossible to award very many credits for a small thing which might end up really being a prompt and not very many tokens away in the age of powerful generative LLMs. Perhaps they could get published in a sort of maths magazine like EMS press, or one could send them to a maths contest (e.g. Miklos–Schweitzer), or if enough of these are accumulated one could publish them as a book. Alternatively, one could obtain two types of credit: big theses level credits which are what is taken into consideration in career decisions, and smaller community level credits for this sort of thing.

This question in turn arose because, in an earlier draft, I considered the possibility of everyone having the right to 3 defences a year. I’d argue that very few people can consistently write 3 doctoral theses a year, but this probably does not alleviate the pressure to publish. This lead me to the suggestion of everyone having the right to a single defence every year, which I got from Lawk Mineh, where they report on what they have done since their last defence (but are not obliged to defend every year). This potentially lets the small/cute results in. However, if there are too many small results that are spread across too wide an area, it makes it difficult for any single examiner to assess the publication as a whole. I’m sure there are refinements/ways around this, but I do favour more directed efforts to tell a story about a part of mathematics rather than just a smattering of very loosely related results.

3. Conclusion

I do think that most versions of what I have suggested are strictly better than the current unsustainable journal system13 [#fn-00b6a261-ee19-4164-b979-402af26231ea], and absent any better suggestions this system is something that we should move towards. It has also been pointed out to me there is no need to try to enforce this very much, since it is aligned with the interests of the community and the institutions that fund us. I was told that CNRS spent a whopping 3 million euros on publishing fees in 2025, and not a single cent of this went to anybody who did any mathematical work. I think that, upon reflection, mathematicians will collectively realise just how ridiculous the publishing system is and how little it actually serves the interests of the community. It would be best if the proposal were to get tested, and given the new journals for video recordings of talks, I hope it’s not too much of a stretch for some slightly more senior people to give a proposal like this a test run and see what works, what doesn’t, what needs tweaking, whether it is workable etc.14 [#fn-2fa4292e-bf34-494e-ae61-7012dcd47be6] Let me also offer something so that it doesn’t seem like I am just telling others what to do15 [#fn-4cffcf05-411e-49a8-8934-cbaa7152a5de]. If there is consensus around a proposal for what defences should look like and people would want to test it to see how that works, I am willing to test that out on my defence in summer 2027. If it comes to it, every single mathematician in the world is welcome to come and grill me about my thesis.

I applaud this attempt to provide a specific vision of how to solve some of the challenges AI poses to our professional institutions. I agree broadly that if we want to assess human understanding/communication, then perhaps some system that includes real-time human interaction is the way to go.

I do think it is worth noting though, that there are a lot of research mathematicians, and probably a great deal of math research is someone applying existing methods to solve a problem. Results that develop a novel technique that can be widely generalized or that uncover some deep, hidden connection between different areas of math are less common. So it seems that under this model the standard of what counts as research is different, and possibly most of what mathematicians are doing now no longer counts. In my own career, I would say I have mostly solved problems that people were interested in by adapting existing techniques, perhaps in somewhat clever ways if I want to give myself a little credit. Would I no longer be a research mathematician unless I managed to come up with some bold new idea?

The justification is that problem-solving alone is not valuable because LLMs can easily do it, and one could argue that perhaps we should not have been writing these routine problem-solving papers in the first place. But what about, as the author suggests, when LLMs can also do all the things proposed in this new model for assessing math research? Even if AI cannot do this right now, there is no reason to expect that some future AI system won’t be able to write a human-readable preprint and defend it exactly in the terms the author suggests here. So then why should this be valuable?

For me, the answer is that trying to assign value to mathematical tasks that LLMs cannot do right now is a losing game, because what if in the future LLMs are better than humans at all mathematical and related tasks? The author mentions this question and admits they do not yet have a solution to this problem. But then why should problem solving have no value just because AI can do it if AI can do everything anyway? If I solve some problem, develop understanding of the problem and methods I used by doing so, and communicate that the to community, should this not be a contribution under this model? Sure, my audience could have asked AI rather than listened to me, but in this hypothetical they could do that for any mathematical question they might have, or even ask the AI to suggest to them what mathematical questions to ask.

Hi Andrew, thank you for your well-specified and thoughtful proposal! It was an interesting read and resonated with me strongly by its combining a philosophically grounded vision of what mathematics should be and a concrete way to implement those ideas.

I believe it could help to weaken some requirements to make the proposal easier to implement. For example, a centralized institution that regulates all instances of the single most important activity for a mathematical career seems like a bad idea. This would create language, cultural and maybe even geographical barriers and could also serve as a breeding ground for corruption. I think what we need is not an international institution, but international standards implementable locally worldwide in a decentralized manner. Mathematics, in languages other than English, by and for communities outside the Anglophone, must and will live on as a legitimate profession. 

I think we can adapt your proposal to this sentiment without any trouble. We could have a well-defined, globally recognized notion of a defence, much like what a PhD currently is. Once institutions authorized to host defences are set up in some leading countries and backed up by leading universities, I think the rest of the world would soon follow.

Come to think about it, why don’t the good old universities host defences directly? Or, we could have several “Defence Associations” across the world, each consisting of various partnered universities and maybe other research institutions.

Without getting ahead of myself, I want to express gratitude to you for laying out this fertile ground to fork further proposals, and I would like to stay in touch about this.
