AHM Statement on OpenAI’s October 6 Release of Mathematical Documents

7 October, 2026 in guest blog [https://terrytao.wordpress.com/category/guest-blog/], opinion [https://terrytao.wordpress.com/category/opinion/] | Tags: AI [https://terrytao.wordpress.com/tag/ai/], Association for Human Mathematics [https://terrytao.wordpress.com/tag/association-for-human-mathematics/] | by Terence Tao [https://terrytao.wordpress.com/author/teorth/] 

 [This is a guest post by the Association for Human Mathematics [https://www.ahmath.org/], reposted from their statements page [https://www.ahmath.org/statements]. This blog post was initially written in a different file format and converted using AI. — T.]

Yesterday, on October 6th, 2026, OpenAI — which is currently defending lawsuits against accusations of illegal plagiarism, copyright infringement, and trademark dilution — released a repository of manuscripts purporting to contain solutions to a number of high-profile problems in mathematics.

Mathematicians did not ask for this work to be done. The Advisory Group on Mathematics and Artificial Intelligence, from whom OpenAI has claimed to derive its legitimacy, opened their initial advisory statement by saying that frontier AI corporations should not test advanced mathematical problems on internal models. In ignoring the central premise of the Advisory Group’s position, OpenAI has indicated total disregard for the norms of scientific research — norms that guarantee that mathematics remains trustworthy, ethically researched, and in the public interest.

Mathematicians have a particular vision of progress that is informed by history and field-specific considerations. We reject OpenAI’s assertion that this release advances our subject, and we urge mathematicians and the public to view the value of this publication model with due skepticism.

Releasing over 700 files at once is not a demonstration of scholarship, but a demonstration of power. We urge mathematicians to discontinue their work with OpenAI and to return to a vision of science that centers human understanding.

Association for Human Mathematics Communications Working Group

geeksingularity [http://geeksingularity.wordpress.com]

The AHM statement raises fair concerns about process, but its central argument would not survive the standards it claims to defend.

Mathematics has never asked whether a result was requested. It asks whether it is correct. “Mathematicians did not ask for this work to be done” is not a mathematical objection; it is a jurisdictional one. No field that prides itself on openness gets to decide who may attempt its problems.

The statement leads with OpenAI’s unrelated lawsuits. Whatever their merits, they have no bearing on whether a single one of these 700 manuscripts is right or wrong. We would not accept that line of argument against a human author, and we should not accept it here.

The claim that releasing 700 files is “a demonstration of power” rather than scholarship has it backwards. Publishing claimed proofs in full, where anyone can check them, is the most exposed position an author can take. If the arguments are flawed, the flaws are now in plain view. That is how mathematics has always kept itself trustworthy: not by restricting who may speak, but by checking what is said.

The Advisory Group’s position deserves a hearing, but an advisory statement is not a norm of scientific research, and declining to follow it is not “total disregard” for those norms. The norms that actually matter here are verification, priority, and attribution. On those, there are legitimate questions to press: how the manuscripts were vetted, whether they come with formal verification, how prior human work is credited, and who bears the cost of refereeing at this volume. Those questions would make a strong statement. A call to boycott does not.

Urging the public toward “due skepticism” is reasonable. But skepticism means reading the proofs and finding the errors. Urging mathematicians to stop working with OpenAI altogether asks them to stop looking, and that is the opposite of skepticism.

If human understanding is the goal, the community’s best response is to engage: check the results, publish the refutations where they fail, and credit them where they hold.

William Oliver [http://digitalnativemusic.wordpress.com]

I want to reply in bulk to the lay people who think that this is a kind of ‘gatekeeping’ and who believe that mathematicians are not doing a good job of explaining why they are reacting this way. Well, personally, I do not agree that it’s gatekeeping, but I do think this is quite a fair response. 

I do not think mathematicians have done a great job of communicating the problems related to AI and mathematics and why humans are important for mathematical practice. I think to simply dismiss the laypeople who think this is the case is unreasonable. 

That said, this statement from the AHM is based on a real concern: this isn’t mathematicians simply ‘whining’ about it. So, until we get a more ‘organized’ explanation, for now, let me attempt to try to explain what exactly the problem is here as I see it personally. There are 3 main points I can make, and the third is the most important (and the longest, because its hardest to describe). 

[For non-laypeople: I also would like to say that the philosophical position presented in the third part of this may be useful to mathematicians in this crisis. This is as formal as I will get about it in this comment, but I just want to try to lend some legitimacy to the ideas I am expositing. It presents a definition of Kant’s ‘regulative ideas’ as a proposition that is necessarily possible in the sense of modal logic, which relates this to classical logic through the Fitting-Smullyan intuitionistic interpretation of classical logic (and forcing) as the logic one gets when one considers what is (recursively) necessarily possible in an S4 Kripke Frame. This then has implications on a more ‘harmonious’ interpretation of intuitionistic logic’s relationship with classical logic. In particular, intuitionists, I think, although inspired by Immanuel Kant’s philosophy, missed the incorporation of one of his best ideas (the idea that metaphysical or ontological beliefs play an essential regulatory role in the acquisition of knowledge). And so, I am arguing that this regulatory aspect of the mind is a specific, practical, formal, faculty that will remain something that Artificial Intelligence cannot replicate without the active help of human beings. And I think amounts to a kind of ‘Mathematical Idealism’ that is separate from other common positions on the philosophy of mathematics such as platonism or intuitionism. 

I would like to emphasize though that this is mostly a comment for laypeople]

Now back to the 3 main points:

The first problem I will mention is the most basic and obvious: a large number of the natural language versions of the proofs are likely simply wrong. This has already been shown to be the case with the Navier Stokes proof. Although the Lean verification part is probably correct, the natural language portions of the proofs have no reason to be correct unless they are thoroughly verified by a reviewer. That said, suppose the natural language part was ‘technically’ correct. The natural language portion could still likely contain errors of a different kind: e.g. claiming some idea is new when it isn’t, citing papers that don’t exist, emphasizing the importance of something that isn’t at all important, or implying or insinuating philosophical interpretations of the work that don’t make sense. These kinds of hallucinations are unlikely to go away any time soon (which is a point I will get back to later). So, the first thing that Open AI could have done is simply leave out the natural language part of the proofs unless they are thoroughly reviewed, verified, and revised by a human. It seems rather obvious that the only reason they don’t is for marketing reasons. (note: I am not saying [yet] that humans need to do this forever, but that is just factually what is needed right now. However, point 3 below argues that humans do need to be a part of the process indefinitely). 

Now there is a second reason this is a problem: these results are undoubtedly based on the results of other mathematicians who are not receiving credit. This is a fact that is predicted by the data processing inequality (an AI cannot produce new information). Let me address this issue for mathematics specifically, and the potential rebuttal: that ‘humans also base their work only on information they have received from elsewhere’. First, in the specific case of mathematics, we can grant that this might be the case (although I am not sure this is entirely true), but mathematicians still put in a considerable amount of effort to give credit to the ideas that their work are based on, even down to acknowledging influences outside of mathematics (such as family members supporting them while a mathematician writes a book, for example). This is one of the main aspects of “the norms of scientific research” that was broken here. The entire engine of mathematical progress implicitly relies on this system of credit. Personally, there is a sense in which I agree that mathematics shouldn’t be so heavily reliant on credit, but even if we grant that, the credit should nevertheless be given regardless, otherwise it is simply plagiarism, and doesn’t even really accurately reflect AI progress, if that is the goal here. 

The third reason this is a problem is the most important: Mathematics is about understanding, not theorem proving. This is a rather vague tagline that has been repeated a lot, but probably does not mean much to lay people, and I would love it if mathematicians would collectively work to try to define it a bit better, but in the mean time, let me try to give my two cents about what I think it means.

I think there is a very simple question to start with. What does it actually mean for a theorem to be true? And suppose we know that a theorem is true, why is it that true theorems that at first appear to have no practical application end up being helpful to humanity? 

We might want to focus on the first question first, about truth. I will not argue that I have the answers about the nature of truth. All I will argue here is that if you think the answer is simple, it’s not. Lets say we build some program (or get some AI agent to do this) to search for an inconsistency in Lean, and never stop until it finds one (or proves that it will never find one). Now the question is: Does the program/agent ever end its search? Because AI agents are programs, we can in theory formalize this question in Lean itself. In other words, this is a perfectly formal mathematical question. Now, this is where this begins to relate to truth: Gödel’s second incompleteness theorem says that if it is the case that there is no inconsistency to be found, the AI will never be able to prove that there isn’t one. Most mathematicians think that the AI will never stop its search (especially if we change this from Lean to some more ‘stable’ system like ZFC).

Before I continue, its very important to emphasize this point: this is now a statement that is not only about the abstract realm of mathematics, but about the physical world, since we can actually build this machine and run it. 

The first question is: is it true that the program/agent either ends its search or doesn’t? (Again, remembering that this is a question at least partially about the physical world) If you believe the answer is yes, then assuming that there are no inconsistencies in Lean, then we have just shown that truth is not the same as formal validity. We do not have to know which answer is actually the case to draw this conclusion, just that it is either one or the other. 

But many might say that the answer is no (i.e. there is no truth value here). In other words, you have to be committed to the fact that in reality the AI neither ceases its search nor searches endlessly. 

This may be true, but it is an entirely different philosophical can of worms. In other words: again, we are making this claim about physical reality, not just about an abstract realm of mathematical truths. And so the question is, under this view, do you actually believe this? 

The belief that all statements are either true or false is called the “Law of the excluded middle”. And its rejection is the defining feature of a kind of logic called Intuitionistic logic which, incidentally, is what a lot of proof assistant languages like Lean are based on. Whether or not you believe it one way or another, I think this is the quickest way to show a layperson the general ‘feeling’ of the difference between understanding and formal proof, and perhaps the difference between truth and a formal proof. We have an ‘understanding’ that the program will either halt or it won’t, but a) we can’t prove it and b) if we think we don’t need to prove it, then it implies there is a statement that is true but not provable. There are some standard objections to the idea that intuitionistic logic is a solution to the problem of truth. In other words, intuitionistic logic has its own problem that is like the problem of truth. But I want to focus here on something a bit more ‘practical’. 

The gist of this more ‘practical’ issue, for me, has to do with my second question above: how does mathematics end up being helpful to humanity in a way that pure empirical reasoning isn’t?

In Andrzej Grzegorczyk’s paper “A Philosophically Plausible Formal Interpretation of Intuitionistic Logic“, Grzegorczyk interprets intuitionistic logic – logic without the law of the excluded middle – as the logic of empirical reasoning, and classical logic – logic with the law of the excluded middle – as a logic for metaphysical or ontological reasoning. (For those more serious logicians/mathematicians, this paper was one of the inspirations for Melvin Fitting’s/Raymond Smullyans intuitionistic interpretation of classical logic, and I think the fitting-smullyan view of classical logic as being what is recursively ‘necessarily possible’ in a kripke frame is extremely enticing for the view that classical logic is regulatory in the way that I will describe). 

Historically, intuitionistic logic is heavily inspired by the work of the imminent philosopher Immanuel Kant, who postulated the existence of an ‘intuitive’ aspect of the mind, and said that this part of the mind is the only source of absolutely true knowledge. Intuitionism takes this position seriously, and gets its name from this postulated faculty of the mind. 

But, I think this misses two crucial aspects of Kant’s philosophical position. 

The first is that Kant was putting this in an idealist context: his idealism, for our purposes, being the belief that awareness or inner experience is the subject of reasoning. In other words, he made an extremely compelling argument, based on the philosophy of Hume, that, although the intuitive aspect of the mind does give us knowledge about something, it doesnt give us any knowledge about what we typically think it does: it only gives us knowledge about our own inner experience, not about the world externally from our inner experience. It doesn’t even give us knowledge about whether we exist at all. After all, Hume argued there is no way to prove rationally or empirically that the world outside of our experience even exists. So how do we come to know the world exists? How do we even know we exist? 

This is the second part of Kant’s philosophical position that (I’d argue) Intuitionism kind of ignores, he says although we have no way to know any metaphysical or ontological claims are true at all, or that even the concept of truth exists, we must believe it anyways. We must in a rather strong sense: we are forced to believe in some metaphysical or ontological claims, not by choice, but by instinct or habit (this is essentially Humes argument). This is where he made the distinction between regulative ideas and constitutive ideas. Essentially, Kant argued there is a difference between what is ultimately or absolutely true, and what it is useful to believe is true, and sometimes these things contradict each-other.

I think a useful analogy here to understand this is a follows. Suppose there is a blind hiker who is trying to get to the highest peak in a mountain range. A blind hiker might feel around them and try to see where the terrain increases in height, and go in that direction. Eventually, they reach the highest peak on the current mountain this way, and realize there is no direction they can go without decreasing their elevation. Now, they could stop here, and simply assume they have found the highest peak. But in this simplified analogy, it’s extremely obvious that the blind hiker has no way of knowing if they have reached the highest peak or not. In particular, if this is all there was to it, the blind hiker might just stop, and never learn anything new about the terrain. In order to actually be sure, the blind hiker has to believe, at the very least, that there might be a higher peak out there somewhere, and go searching for it. In order to do so, they have to actually sacrifice some elevation first, before they reach the base of the current mountain and find the next. Now, maybe there is a higher peak, and maybe there isn’t, but in the process, the blind hiker discovers many new animals, plants, caves, rivers, and interesting other things (that are helpful) along the way, that they never would have discovered if they believed whole-heartedly that their current mountain was the only one. And, they might even discover a higher peak, as they hoped. It is in this sense, that the belief that there is a higher peak is ‘regulative’. It regulates the blind hiker’s search. It’s an incentive to act and stay focused, rather than simply stay put and never learn anything new. 

One might argue that the regulative belief is unnecessary because the hiker could go searching, but the existence of some regulative belief is necessary. I.e. if the hiker didnt have any regulative beliefs at all, then the blind hiker might either stay put like we mentioned before, or just e.g. choose random directions and eventually end up in the ocean, or back in some city, and not even care about exploring the mountain range. There is another aspect of this particular belief that is useful: the belief that there is a higher peak is ‘necessarily possible’: in other words, no matter how much information the hiker gets, its always possible for there to be a higher peak using their current methods of information gathering (assuming, as in the ‘spherical cow [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spherical_cow]‘ that the terrain is infinite). This allows the hiker to never have to find some other regulatory activity. 

Here is where we return to my original question: how does mathematics end up being helpful to humanity in a way that pure empirical reasoning isn’t?

In this same sense, if we think of scientific or empirical reasoning as it is like in Grzegorczyk’s paper, then scientific or empirical reasoning much like the hiker who only searches in their direct vicinity. This is of course useful, but it can’t be the only kind of thinking. In my opinion, mathematics distinguishes itself from science by concerning itself with ontological reasoning, which is regulatory for us as a species. And it distinguishes itself from philosophy at large in that it takes this ontological reality as a given. It takes the ultimate regulatory idea of mathematical truth as a given. 

It may be the case that the beliefs that come along with thinking of mathematics as ontological in nature are true, or it may be the case that they are false, but either way, they give us a means of searching beyond our direct vicinity, and finding out new things we never would have predicted we would find without getting lost (in the same way the blind hiker may not have predicted the discovery of a new species of animal: its not even what they were looking for!). 

In this exact way, if one uses AI as primarily a way to turn a large swath of mathematics into a purely empirical discipline (which is what happens if no mathematicians understand what is going on, and simply observe with their senses a ‘true/false’ verdict), then one loses the benefit of mathematics entirely. 

One has to use the ontological parts of mathematics, which can, in the above strange sense, only be carried out by humans, since otherwise it becomes purely empirical in nature in the sense of Grzegorczyk. Without this, we are very likely to miss out on discoveries we would have otherwise made. 

I want to make one last point about AI, since one might argue: well all that is well and good, but why isnt it the case that AI is simply a blind hiker as well, and has its own regulatory beliefs? Well, this may be true, but I would argue that we know it cannot be the specific regulatory belief mentioned above: that mathematics has an ontological status. This is because of Tarski’s undefinability theorem. The concept of mathematical truth cannot be defined, and so, since AI is just a computer program, it is definable in principle, and so is its search for mathematical truth. Therefore, if we believe that it will eventually converge to truth, then we contradict Tarski’s undefinability theorem. So, it will, like the hiker without the regulatory idea, either go off in a random direction, or eventually get stuck. 

So we know AI cannot replicate the behaviors that come about as a result of this regularoty idea. One may say that we do not know that humans do not have the same limitation, but I would argue, unlike in the case of AI, we can’t know this: we will never be able to prove a human is a computer because of the nature of inner experience, which is not predicted by the theory of computability. And this means that the idea that an AI can approach truth is fundamentally different than the idea that a human can approach truth. 

The belief that we can, and AI can’t is therefore regulatory in the sense described above: that it is necessarily possible that its the case. 

I think this has practical implications, in the sense that I think we will see consequences of not believing this, in the same way that the blind hiker might practically end up going in weird or strange directions.

So, hopefully, this is at least a start at a more thorough explanation of the problem here. If the above kind thinking resonates, I really hope that it becomes a more ‘standard’ way of thinking about the practice of mathematics in the age of AI, because I think it philosophically clarifies the essential role of humans play in mathematical discovery.

I think people grossly misunderstand the point of mathematical problem-solving, even in spite of the repeated statements of top mathematicians: it’s about the know-how of solving problems. This is true for any field if you think about it: as a society we possess, say, electronics, not because someone has a device that utilizes it, we possess it because we understand how to produce that device from start to finish, and understand how and why it works. Of course there’s a layer of abstraction emerging when we try to work on higher-level problems (designing a processor from logic gates does not require understanding of the exact implementation of a NAND gate in silicon), but it’s only because the details might not be relevant to the current problem being solved, and we have the ability (due to recorded knowledge, education, and experts in various fields) to move between said layers of abstraction freely.

This is to my mind the only sustainable way to preserve knowledge in all fields. If some benevolent entity gifted us with, say, a spaceship, we might be able to advance space colonization for a bit, but it doesn’t scale at all (because we don’t know how to really build more of them or bigger ones) and is fragile (because if it breaks down we will never know how to fix it unless we reverse engineer the entire technology anyway).

Now to take this back to mathematics, to me theorems are tools, and proofs are the know-how of how to produce the tools. This is I think a very common outlook, because otherwise what’s the point of proving long-standing theorems as exercises when we train the new generation of mathematicians? Why couldn’t they just use the tools outright? The value is in the journey, because it teaches you how to come up with the tools yourself, understand their quirks and nuances, so you can use them appropriately (e.g. when they *actually* work), and tweak them when the problem at hand doesn’t match the exact formulation of a theorem. If we don’t do this we’re not practicing mathematics anymore, it’s theology.

Releasing hundreds of mathematical statements without the journey to get there is like getting an alien spaceship. We could take the results right away as they are and just treat them like gospel. The value in that is arguably minimal, because we don’t develop the know-how. It’s possible that if these same problems were attacked to completion by humans, the intuition we’ve built, formalized, and documented along the path to solution would result in thousands upon thousands of other problems solved with similar or inspired techniques. The delta between the 700 or so problems solved by OAI, and the honestly unknowable number of potential theorems (with understanding of the tools behind them!) we would arrive at if we went the analogue route, is what OAI robbed the entire world of and what we will never get back. And all for arguably a marketing stunt. You could argue this is ethically theft.

This is what I read when I see “Mathematicians did not ask for this work to be done”. This is barely even work. Even if we assume it’s all correct.

It was never about gatekeeping either. Mathematics is “ours”, as in it’s human. We all own it. I think AHM just wants to stress the contrast. If a person/research group just jumped out of left field with 700 papers (but with well-thought-out proofs and exposition to them, both distilled into what’s only essential/easy/beautiful) I imagine it would be celebrated as a great achievement, but only because then it would be actually advancing the field (since we have a group of people possessing the know-how that can spread it to others). No one ever forbade anyone from doing that, I’d love to see that, to be honest. It’s just what we actually got is essentially useless spam we have to reverse engineer anyway and deal with the drama while at it. This makes it not really ours. Not our spaceship.

If you ever solved a puzzle and hated someone spoiling the solution for you, this is what this entire thing looks like. Except with way bigger stakes, because the process of solving was the really important goal, for all of us.

A couple thoughts:
1. Mathematicians (and anyone wishing to maintain the confidentiality of their work) should not send in prompts to a foundation lab. There appears to be strong evidence that the temptation is too great for these companies to not take an occasional peak. Since they do not give us a method to audit them, we live in a “trust, don’t verify” world today.

2. LLMs are good at combining what they were trained on + what is in the context window, and generating outputs that bridge those two gaps (with the context window being more heavily weighted than the entirety of the training data). Formal systems expressed symbolically (e.g. mathematical systems expressed with notation) relative to normal text have much lower Kulmogorov complexity (they carry more information per token). These two facts make LLMs pretty good at connecting dots scattered across the literature that might not be quickly obvious without them. There are needless in the haystack of prior art that they can find.

3. LLMs and DNNs in general are elaborate autocomplete engines. I predict that what we will see is a wave of problems that were close to being solved, or if one person could load the full body of prior art into their head, they would solve it. However, there is a horizon on all of this, when the low hanging fruit has been solved (via autocomplete), what remains is the stuff that still needs to be solved via not-autocomplete, the spark that DNNs and other statistical methods still can’t do.

4. There is a thing that is starting to be called “the reviewer bandwidth problem” (LLMs output faster than humans can read). There is another thing called “the reviewer knowledge problem” (LLMs are often used to fill gaps in the users knowledge). This mostly means that the user of an LLM can review everything and give up much of the utility LLMs provide (doing things faster), and that they are likely unqualified to review everything, because part of the reason they used the LLM in the first place is that they lack the knowledge to verify all of the LLMs output. If the output matters, this means the people who do have the knowledge will be very busy and there likely are not enough of them currently. If the output is important, and about math, I see the mathematics community being busy for a very long time.

What follows is a prediction. I think LLMs will gobble up some of the low hanging fruit solutions, especially if there is a body of work orbiting the solution that the LLM can close the loop on via autocomplete. During this period mathematicians will be very busy (point 4). After that period the low hanging fruit will be solved. What I do think will happen after this period, is we will see the cycle time of mathematical solutions shorten (for those that choose to use AI in their workflows). If a mathematician (or anyone) wants to use an LLM (or any system that stores their research) they should have a high confidence that whoever is hosting the model won’t steal the research or reuse it as training data (point 1). The gold standard is to self host the model. But I see a renaissance in the areas of privacy, cryptography, and security (created by mathematicians and due for an upgrade).

What I recommend is we figure out how to enable mathematicians to accelerate their research in a secure way, and start building out those tools so mathematicians can fully take advantage of AI today and tomorrow (likely a research project of it’s own). I’m not sure what these tools will look like, but codex and claude-code likely won’t be on the list, the incentives of those companies just aren’t aligned with data privacy/confidentiality.

Earlier today I meant to add a psychoanalytic perspective to the discussion above but was interrupted in the process. One could argue that for many, math is more akin to art than to engineering. At its essence, the pursuit of understanding of complex problems is a form of creativity, one that allows the individual to find meaning and purpose. What the AI is taking away is this path to attenuate the “lack” (in the Lacanian sense), which explains the sense of mourning that one camp in this math/AI debate is experiencing. Fully accepting this loss is a prerequisite to finding good paths to tackle the questions that lie in front of us. 

If math “truths” is all that matters, then every AI generated proof, whether intelligible or not, is equally valuable to one produced by a human, even if the human one is written in a way specifically designed to be understood by other humans while the AI-produced one is not. This path does seem to carry some risk of making humans irrelevant, but it should also raise the bar for AI generated work, since there is no point in having AI solve very hard but useless problems. The lay critic of why AI companies are not attempting to solve problems such as climate change and economic inequality rather than settling famous math problems, immediately applies.

If we accept the psychoanalytical premise that math research is a creative endeavor meant to fill in the “lack” by providing a sense of purpose and a mechanism to relate to the world, then we should actively try to protect it. If we don’t, then why would future generations find math research appealing at all? And if people stop finding solace in math, who is to take over the education of future generations of students? It is our passion for problem-solving and our incessant questioning of the world around us that touches the minds of many of the students that cross our paths (regardless of the psychoanalytical reasons for why we choose to do this). Whether we want it or not, mathematicians have a role to play in our society, one of passing on to future generations the skills to think critically and to make sense of complex problems. We should therefore think very carefully about how to ensure that we are not the last generation of humans who choose to pursue understanding for the sheer beauty of it.

I believe OpenAI has demonstrated a considerable capacity for mathematical reasoning, even if its October 6 release raises legitimate questions about the distinction between computational power and genuine scholarship. Capability can provide the foundation upon which scholarship develops, provided that it is accompanied by rigorous verification, critical scrutiny, and a willingness to correct mistakes.

Mathematics should not become a discipline in which the fear of being wrong discourages exploration. Its progress depends not only on the validation of established results, but also on openness to unconventional ideas, diverse approaches, and unexpected possibilities. The hundreds of mathematical documents released by OpenAI may not all contain fully verified or valuable results, but they can still constitute a substantial source of material for further investigation, inspiration, and reference.

Consider a mathematical argument that turns out to be partly incorrect. That does not necessarily make the entire attempt intellectually worthless. A flawed argument may contain a promising intuition, an unfamiliar approach, or a question that leads another researcher towards a valid proof. 

I envision a future in which mathematics becomes more open, more natural, and more accessible to creative minds, including those willing to explore unconventional paths without being paralysed by the possibility of error. We should therefore be able to recognise OpenAI’s demonstrated capabilities while remaining critical of its limitations. The goal should be neither to celebrate every AI-generated result nor to dismiss an entire body of work because some of it contains errors. It should be to build a scientific culture in which creativity is encouraged, mistakes are examined honestly, and verified knowledge remains the ultimate standard.

Malt7635

Found what looks like a fatal, previously unreported error in one of the unformalized manuscripts — family 178, “Deterministic nonbipartite Ramanujan graphs in every fixed degree” (Theorem 1.1 claims a deterministic polytime construction of nonbipartite Ramanujan graphs in every fixed degree d ≥ 3).

The paper’s whole novelty is Theorem 8.3’s “exact repair” stage (p. 76). Step 2 asserts a two-sided bound J_T ≍ n^{3a/2+a/10} (a = 10^{−4}) on the near-edge mode count. But the theorem’s own Hypothesis 2 — the local spectral mass bound — summed over all n vertices gives J_T ≤ C′·n^{3a/2} (orthonormality: Σ_p |v_i(p)|² = 1, evaluated at w = 3e). Since a/10 > 0, the asserted lower bound exceeds the proven upper bound for all large n: a direct self-contradiction, and the paper’s own §1.2 states the O(n^{3a/2}) bound.

The lower bound is load-bearing. The tilted law’s positivity (8.7) rests on the bracket being ≤ C·n·e^{3/2}/J_T = O(n^{−a/10}) = o(1), which requires J_T ≳ n^{3a/2+a/10}; with only J_T ≤ C′·n^{3a/2} the bracket stays bounded away from zero, so ν is never shown to be a valid probability law (J_T = 0 is consistent with the hypotheses — division by zero in (8.7)). Positivity needs J_T/n^{3a/2} → ∞ while the mass bound forces J_T/n^{3a/2} ≤ C′, so the feasible region is empty: not fixable by constant tuning. Stages 1–3 give only near-Ramanujan spectrum, so Theorem 1.1 is unproven as stated.

I re-derived the summation from scratch against the manuscript text and re-checked the surrounding algebra numerically on small random regular graphs (identities hold to ~1e-14 — the machinery is sound; the required law cannot exist). No prior flaw reports found for this manuscript (web search + the repo’s history.md, Oct 10; note the repo has Issues disabled, so there is no upstream venue for this).

Disclosure: this finding comes from an AI-assisted audit, independently verified by a human from the primary source.

The paper’s conventions point the other way: cardinalities are written with bars (“divide by |I| ≍ l”); picked parameters are introduced with the same ≍ grammar (§7.4: “put δ ≍ n^{−g} and l₂ ≍ l₀n^{−g−r}, and set…”); a single J_T serves both T₊ and T₋ in (8.7); and the subscript parallels w_B and R_B — step labels, like the cutoff t_T^τ on the same line.
