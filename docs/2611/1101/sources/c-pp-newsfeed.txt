29 October 2026

Classification of Finite Simple Groups formalised

Axiom, a startup focused on models that excel at mathematical reasoning, has announced [https://axiommath.ai/research/the-enormous-theorem-formalized/] the complete formalisation of the classification of finite simple groups. The classification was one of the greatest achievements in 20th century mathematics, and (by some estimates) contains tens of thousands of pages of maths published by around 100 authors. This follows Anthropics’ formalisation [https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/9e431dff043da6538d99d6c2d231b670aa3da263.pdf] of the proof of Fermat’s Last Theorem, which was achieved in early September (recall also Kevin Buzzards’ reaction [https://xenaproject.wordpress.com/2026/09/04/flt-anthropic-has-beaten-me-to-it/]).

29 October 2026

Google release their new interactive world model, Genie 4

Google DeepMind have released [https://deepmind.google/blog/genie-4-a-new-generation-of-world-simulation/] the latest iteration of their world model family, Genie 4, which boasts better reasoning, and native integrations with their voice and video models. It allows users to prompt interactive scenarios, and then prompt changes to the environment (it’s best understood by watching videos of what it does). World models are different to LLMs, and are meant to mimic spatial reasoning rather than verbal reasoning. Many prominent labs are rushing to develop these models, despite their relative obscurity compared to LLMs (at least as of now).

27 October 2026

OpenAI share three more advancements in Mathematics, Computer Science, and Physics

The advancements [https://openai.com/index/building-scientific-understanding-with-academic-communities/] are a claimed proof of the local smoothing conjecture (which implies the Kakeya conjecture), a disproof of the Exponential Time Hypothesis (for 3SAT), and work related to Yang-Mills. Notably the Local Smoothing result, as opposed to the other two, is released without external collaboration and digestion. This appears to be OpenAI’s attempt to meet the guidelines set out by the AGMAI [https://agmai.org], at least in part.

It appears that the Exponential Time Hypothesis was previously disproved [https://github.com/anthropics/formal-math/tree/16a5ce41304fcdaea4e0be7c132e1c361dc26da5/eth] in the days before by an internal model at Anthropic. It is yet another example of the rivalry between the two labs, following on from the Navier-Stokes controversy.

Let’s take a break from talking about Maths I don’t understand (Hodge) to talk about cybersecurity events I don’t understand. Not to alarm anyone, but some experts are describing this event as ‘Black October [https://www.axios.com/2026/10/22/php-vulnerability-security-update-websites]‘, and it may lead to significant changes in access to the open internet in this new (age of AI).

20 October 2026

Multiple journals in Probability and Statistics pause new submissions

Six prominent journals in the fields of Probability and Statistics have begun [https://www.edpsciences.org/en/news-highlights/3058-esaim-probability-and-statistics-takes-action-to-address-increasing-manuscript-submissions?utm_source=chatgpt.com] a coordinated pause of new submissions, from 19 October to 08 November. This is due to an influx of papers, largely due to the use of AI tools, and will allow the Editorial Boards to set out new policies and guidelines.

The journals undertaking this joint initiative are: Electronic Journal of Probability (EJP), Electronic Communications in Probability (ECP), ESAIM: Probability and Statistics (ESAIM: PS), Stochastic Processes and their Applications (SPA), Bernoulli, and Annales de l’Institut Henri Poincaré, Probabilités et Statistiques (AIHP).

We can expect many other journals to adapt as the use of AI tools further proliferates, and we may see similar pauses instigated elsewhere. Recall that arXiv, from 01 October, set out a limit [https://blog.arxiv.org/2026/10/01/updated-rate-limit-policy/] of two new submissions per calendar month.

In fact, some of OpenAI’s 300+ problems were related to it, including a claimed proof of the rational Hodge conjecture for products of K3 surfaces, but these were retracted [https://github.com/openai/math/blob/main/history.md] due to a mistake.

Trackers for progress on Quasi-Riemann and integer multiplication

Tim Gehrunger [https://people.math.ethz.ch/~timge/] has created this GitHub repository [https://github.com/GTimG/QuasiRiemannTracker] and website [https://gtimg.github.io/QuasiRiemannTracker/] to track the best known zero-free region for the Riemann Zeta Function. The fact that there are no zeroes for Im⁡(z)>1\operatorname{Im}(z) > 1 is classical; OpenAI’s big breakthrough was reducing this to 78\frac{7}{8}, which has since been reduced slightly. We’re waiting for 12\frac{1}{2}, get to work everyone!

Similarly, OpenAI provided [https://github.com/openai/math/blob/main/preprints/Integer-multiplication-below-n-log-n-September-23-2026/paper.pdf] a deterministic algorithm to multiply two nn-bit integers in O(n(log⁡n)1−κ)O(n(\log n)^{1-\kappa}) steps, with an absurdly small κ\kappa. This website [https://beyond-n-log-n.netlify.app] and GitHub [https://github.com/CrocSwap/integer-mult-bounds] track the best improvements on κ\kappa (at least in some instances).

06 October 2026

Polynomial speedup for 3SUM found, settling a major open problem in complexity theory

In this preprint [https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.06783v1], Alman and Vassilevska Williams provide an algorithm to solve 3SUM (determining whether for nn given integers, 3 of the sum to 0) in O(n1.9992)O(n^{1.9992}) time, below the quadratic threshold previously known. This leads to refutations of many other hypothesised thresholds in complexity theory. In the ‘Acknowledgements and Methodology’ section at the end of the preprint, the authors state that this result was originally found and shared with them by Anthropic; where an “employee used an internal research model to investigate open problems in the theory of cryptography”. The authors then simplified and extended the algorithm, wrote the article, and were offered compensation by Anthropic.

8 September 2026

Solution to Navier-Stokes found

As was hotly anticipated, OpenAI have announced [https://openai.com/index/navier-stokes-solution/] that their internal models have solved the Navier-Stokes problem; specifically they produced a singularity in finite time with smooth forcing, establishing variations (C) and (D) in Fefferman’s problem statement [https://www.claymath.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/06/navierstokes.pdf] for the Clay Millennium prize. They used 10,000 agents running concurrently, using a staggering 300 billion output tokens overall. There is however significant controversy surrounding attribution of credit, specifically with Buckmaster-Alpöge, who had previously solved related problems following a year-long collaboration. This issue is probably better discussed in other places, but for an introduction see Buckmaster’s statement [https://cims.nyu.edu/~tristanb/statement.pdf] and Bubeck’s response [https://x.com/SebastienBubeck/status/2097379411691516310]. Credit is also due to Diego Córdoba and Luis Martínez-Zoroa, who initiated the program that led to the eventual solution.

7 September 2026

Blow-ups for smooth forced-Euler found

Tristan Buckmaster and Levent Alpöge have announced [https://mastodon.social/@tristanbuckmaster/117233413705701198] the existence of finite-time blow ups for three problems (smooth-forced Euler, Boussinesq, and porous medium), extending the program initiated by Diego Córdoba and Luis Martínez-Zoroa. They claim they used commercially-available AI tools, including Claude models and GPT-5.6 Sol in the Codex harness. See Terence Tao’s explanation [https://terrytao.wordpress.com/2026/09/07/finite-time-blowup-with-smooth-forcing-term-for-the-incompressible-porous-medium-boussinesq-and-incompressible-euler-equations/] about these problems, and expect another update on this topic shortly.

OpenAI achieve new reasoning breakthrough

OpenAI’s upcoming model ‘Astra’ has reportedly been trained with a new reasoning method that allows it to be more ‘opaque’. In a sense, if one views the model’s chain of thought as writing out its thinking on paper, then this breakthrough translates to abstract reasoning in between writing things out on paper. Obviously this could improve model capabilities and efficiency, but also raises concerns [https://x.com/RyanGreenblatt/status/2094996656186081642?s=20] over monitorability, especially if this initiates a ‘race to the bottom’. OpenAI for their part are claiming [https://x.com/merettm/status/2095023204993490967?s=20] this is unfounded and that they are committed to safety. This also raises questions about the use of these models in Mathematics: read The question of reasoning traces [https://proofsandprompts.com/2026/08/11/the-question-of-reasoning-traces/] on this blog from 11 August 2026, and notice how these developments change the calculus.

Complex structure on S6S^6 constructed

Anthropic’s Levent Alpöge announced [https://x.com/__alpoge__/status/2091639597193368014?s=20] that a (presumably internal) Claude model constructed a complex structure on the 6-sphere S6S^6, see also Philip Engel’s digestion [https://philip-engel.github.io/S6.pdf] of it. It is easy to find an almost complex structure on S6S^6 (by embedding it in the octonions), but this is non-integrable, and hence the question of whether any complex structure on S6S^6 exists. It became a long-standing open problem, and attracted many incorrect constructions and claimed disproofs over the years. This result has since been formalised in Lean.

22 August 2026

‘Mathematical Discourse [https://www.mathematicaldiscourse.org/]’, a new video-based Mathematics journal, is launched

It is an online peer-reviewed journal, which ‘publishes research talks in pure mathematics of the highest quality, promoting a culture which values communication as an essential part of the research process’. The managing editors are Katie Mann, Akshay Venkatesh, and Rachel Webb, and the journal has an impressive scientific and editorial board [https://www.mathematicaldiscourse.org/editors]. The journal matches the philosophy behind Terry Tao’s ICM lecture, and the corresponding essay [https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.16753].

20 August 2026

Elliptic curves of rank at least 30 found

Anthropic’s Levent Alpöge and Ava Howell, presumably using an internal model, constructed an elliptic curve of rank 30 [https://elliptic-rank.icarm.cloud/curve/273] (and on 23.08 one of rank 31 [https://elliptic-rank.icarm.cloud/curve/302]), beating the previous best of rank 29 due to Elkies and Klagsbrun (their arXiv [https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.00077]), assuming GHR and BSD. Notably, this was listed as one of FrontierMath’s Open problems, in the ‘Moderately Interesting’ category. Not to be outdone, Elkies and Klagsburn later found an elliptic curve of rank 30 [https://elliptic-rank.icarm.cloud/curve/398], with a small conductor relative to the trend [https://elliptic-rank.icarm.cloud/].
