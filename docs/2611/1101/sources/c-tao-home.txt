The mathematical sciences in an AI-enabled world [https://terrytao.wordpress.com/2026/10/31/the-mathematical-sciences-in-an-ai-enabled-world/]

31 October, 2026 in paper [https://terrytao.wordpress.com/category/paper/] | Tags: Academy for the Mathematical Sciences [https://terrytao.wordpress.com/tag/academy-for-the-mathematical-sciences/], AI [https://terrytao.wordpress.com/tag/ai/] | by Terence Tao [https://terrytao.wordpress.com/author/teorth/] | 5 comments [https://terrytao.wordpress.com/2026/10/31/the-mathematical-sciences-in-an-ai-enabled-world/#comments] 

The Academy for the Mathematical Sciences [https://www.acadmathsci.org.uk/], which is based in the UK, has released a report [https://www.acadmathsci.org.uk/the-mathematical-sciences-in-an-ai-enabled-world-acadmathsci-shares-new-preprint/] “The mathematical sciences in an AI-enabled world [https://www.acadmathsci.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/Mathematical_Sciences_AI_AcadMathsSci_Oct2026_preprint_final.docx.pdf]“. To quote from the release:

The paper examines how recent advances in artificial intelligence are changing mathematical research and its applications, bringing new opportunities for discovery, verification and the use of mathematics across science, industry and society. It also considers the risks associated with these developments and identifies the support and infrastructure needed to realise the opportunities while addressing the challenges.

A central theme is that, as AI becomes increasingly capable of carrying out technical mathematical work, human expertise will be crucial to direct advances. Identifying promising questions, providing key ideas, assessing the reliability of AI-generated results, developing mathematical understanding and translating discoveries into wider use will remain essential.

The paper identifies priorities for the community, including education and skills, independent verification, access to AI capabilities, research assessment, publication and the infrastructure needed to support mathematical knowledge. It sets out areas for action in the near term and over the coming years.

Last week we organized an intensive two-day summit Math Research in the Age of AI. It was held at the Harvard Center for Mathematical Sciences and Applications (CMSA [https://cmsa.fas.harvard.edu]). Thirty-two mathematicians from academia, government, and industry came together to help navigate this period of rapid change in mathematics and, most importantly, to offer ideas for building the future. There were five working groups organized around the following themes:

 Evaluation, production, and dissemination of research 

 Coordinated actions for the AI era 

 Open-source tools 

 Formalization in mathematics 

 Collaboration between academia and AI labs 

Our report [https://cmsa.fas.harvard.edu/media/2026/10/Summit-on-Math-Research-in-the-Age-of-AI.pdf] can be found on the summit website [https://cmsa.fas.harvard.edu/aimathresearch_summit/]. AI, Mathematics, Education, and a public goods problem [https://terrytao.wordpress.com/2026/10/29/ai-mathematics-education-and-a-public-goods-problem/]

 [This is a guest post by Gireeja Ranade [https://people.eecs.berkeley.edu/~gireeja/]. This blog post was initially written in a different file format and converted using AI. — T.]

The day after the Navier-Stokes result was announced, a student came to my office hours. They said, “Can we please talk about the Navier-Stokes result and what it means? I really need some therapy.” Two days ago another student mentioned, “I used to be really motivated, but now that all the models can do everything with just a prompt, I don’t feel like putting in any effort at all.”

Every time I have such a conversation with students, I appreciate how lucky my generation has been. We didn’t have to grapple with the temptations or threats of AI when we were students. Not being able to quickly look up answers forced us to challenge ourselves, and do it together with our friends. And years of challenging ourselves somehow made us love it [1], and led us to challenging jobs across academia and industry. These jobs are simultaneously wonderful and hard. But I wonder and worry for my students, for the next generation. What do we need to do to make sure these jobs [2] are (1) available for the next generation and (2) that we have enough students who are motivated to train for them?

 — 1. The Hilbert transform — 

In this set of notes we begin the theory of singular integral operators [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singular_integral] – operators which are almost integral operators, except that their kernel just barely fails to be integrable near the diagonal . (This is in contrast to, say, fractional integral operators such as , whose kernels go to infinity at the diagonal but remain locally integrable.) These operators arise naturally in complex analysis, Fourier analysis, and also in PDE (in particular, they include the zeroth order pseudodifferential operators [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudo-differential_operator] as a special case, about which more will be said later). It is here for the first time that we must make essential use of cancellation: cruder tools such as Schur’s test which do not take advantage of the sign of the kernel will not be effective here.

In our September 29 recommendations [https://agmai.org/general-sep29/], we outlined an approach to the release of AI-generated mathematical output by AI labs. Since then, we have been asked by people from multiple labs and by several mathematicians about a specific aspect of the recommendations — that the development of human understanding following a release must remain organic and community led — and what it says about the question of the labs choosing specific mathematicians to participate in various ways in the release of their output. The purpose of this document is to clarify this point and to explain the reasoning behind our position on it. We welcome further feedback from the community and engagement with these arguments.
