Mathematicians Must Rethink Incentives as AI Transforms Mathematical Research

OpenAI's October 6 release of more than 700 mathematics research papers has intensified debate over how the field should respond to rapidly improving machine proof capabilities. In a guest commentary, mathematician Frank Calegari describes the event as a watershed rather than a reason for despair, arguing that mathematical communities should channel concern into practical changes to hiring, publishing, training and recognition.

Calegari acknowledges frustration with AI companies for publishing work that is often poorly written, lacks proper attribution and sometimes contains errors. He notes that mathematicians have more influence over their own institutions than over large technology firms, suggesting that reform of hiring, publication and student training should be the priority. He also warns against treating AI as though it has solved core areas such as number theory or the Langlands program. Solving major problems, he argues, would not hollow out mathematics any more than the periodic table ended chemistry.

He draws a parallel with the classification of finite simple groups, where the resolution of a central problem shifted research attention away from some open questions but did not exhaust the field. In the same way, he says, AI may make some previously difficult proofs routine while leaving deeper structural questions, new conjectures and connections across mathematical fields more important than before.

Calegari argues that AI can help mathematicians pursue broader, riskier ideas that once required prohibitive background knowledge. The value of human work, he says, may shift from producing proofs to choosing worthwhile problems, developing new research programs, understanding results and explaining their significance to wider audiences. He cautions against a culture of rushing to publish machine-generated results simply to claim priority.

To guide the community, Calegari proposes evaluating mathematical work as though it may be AI-assisted, with authors expected to clarify the importance, context and future directions of their results. He calls for hiring decisions to rely more heavily on interviews and conversations that reveal a candidate's vision and technical judgment, including for postdoctoral and graduate roles. He urges journals to demand stronger exposition and significance, and suggests that some arXiv papers should not be published at all. He also recommends broadening graduate education so students retain technical depth while gaining a wider view of mathematics and stronger communication skills.

Finally, Calegari calls for seminars to focus less on incremental novelties and more on larger mathematical questions and directions. He argues that mathematicians should take collective ownership of AI-generated results, recognizing them as built on centuries of human knowledge, and should reassure the public that mathematics remains vibrant. The central argument is that AI has not ended mathematics; it has changed the game by making old skills more transferable and new forms of intellectual leadership essential.
