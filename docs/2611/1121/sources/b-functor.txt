The 3SUM and APSP hypotheses are false

Bogdan Grechuk [https://functor.network/user/3333]

13 Oct 2026

On October 5, 2026, Josh Alman and Virginia Vassilevska Williams
posted a remarkable preprint (Alman and Vassilevska Williams 2026)
disproving two long-standing conjectures in theoretical computer
science: the 3SUM hypothesis and the all-pairs shortest
paths (APSP) hypothesis. These conjectures were central to
fine-grained complexity theory, which studies the precise
running times of algorithms. The authors obtained the first polynomial
improvements over the classical quadratic and cubic running times for
these two problems.

The all-pairs shortest paths problem, or APSP,
concerns a directed graph with numerical weights assigned to its edges.
The length of a path is the sum of its edge weights, and the task is to
find the shortest-path distance between every ordered pair of vertices.
Negative edge weights are allowed, but we assume that the graph contains
no negative-weight cycles, since otherwise some shortest-path distances
would not be well-defined.

The classical Floyd–Warshall algorithm solves APSP on a graph with

Both problems can therefore be solved in polynomial time. However,
despite decades of research, nobody had been able to improve either
running time by a fixed positive power of

subpolynomial factors were known, but these improvements did not change
the leading exponents.

This led to two famous conjectures. The 3SUM hypothesis
asserted that no algorithm could solve 3SUM in time
 for any fixed

that no algorithm could solve APSP in time
 for any fixed

Such algorithms are called truly subquadratic and truly
subcubic, respectively. More precisely, the standard hypotheses
concern polynomially bounded integer inputs in the word RAM
model, in which arithmetic operations on integers occupying
 bits take constant time.

These conjectures became cornerstones of fine-grained complexity
theory. Researchers established numerous reductions showing
that a sufficiently fast algorithm for one problem would imply a faster
algorithm for another. Consequently, assuming the 3SUM or APSP
hypothesis, one could obtain conditional lower bounds for the running
times of many other algorithms.

Alman and Vassilevska Williams have now disproved both
hypotheses.

Another problem, called Exact Triangle, asks whether a graph
with integer edge weights contains a triangle whose three weights sum to
zero. Earlier research established that both 3SUM and APSP can be

The result is particularly striking because the same reductions had
previously been used to argue that many problems were probably
difficult. Now they work in the opposite direction: a faster algorithm
for one problem immediately gives faster algorithms for many others. In
fact, the paper also disproves several further hypotheses in
fine-grained complexity and gives polynomial speedups for problems
involving weighted cliques, matrix products, and graph algorithms.

The authors explicitly acknowledge that the core algorithm was
discovered by Claude, an AI model developed by Anthropic. According to
the paper, the algorithm emerged during an automated investigation of
problems related to cryptography. Anthropic shared it with Alman and
Vassilevska Williams, who then simplified, strengthened, and extended
the argument, established further applications, and prepared the paper.
The authors also report that the main results were formally verified
using the Lean 4 proof assistant and Mathlib.

Of course, the new algorithms are primarily of theoretical interest.
Their exponents are only marginally smaller than
 and
, and the hidden constants are very large. They are
not expected to replace the classical algorithms in everyday
computations.

Nevertheless, the conceptual significance is enormous. Two
conjectures that had shaped algorithmic complexity for decades have been
disproved, and a new algebraic technique has emerged that may lead to
much larger improvements in the future. At the same time, other central
hypotheses, such as the Strong Exponential Time Hypothesis, remain
unaffected. The challenge now is to understand how far the new method
can be pushed.
