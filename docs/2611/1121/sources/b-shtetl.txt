The stochastic parrots have apparently proved the Riemann Hypothesis [https://scottaaronson.blog/?p=10344]

 November 20th, 2026 

“… it is very probable that all roots are real. Of course one would wish for a rigorous proof here; I have for the time being, after some fleeting vain attempts, provisionally put aside the search for this, as it appears dispensable for the immediate objective of my investigation.” –Bernhard Riemann, 1859

[Update: According to “Poe’s Law,” there is no irony or sarcasm on the Internet so obvious that it won’t fly over countless people’s heads. In line with that, lots of people on social media are now saying variants of “how dare Scott Aaronson call LLMs ‘stochastic parrots,’ that demeaning and trivializing term from 2021? Doesn’t he understand that these so-called ‘stochastic parrots’ just proved the Riemann Hypothesis?” ROFL.]

I’m in California to visit Oratomic [https://www.oratomic.com/] and then Inkhaven [https://www.inkhaven.blog/fall-26]. I woke up this morning to the news that Levent Alpöge says that he’s gotten Anthropic models to prove the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis [https://alpo.ge/remark.pdf], producing 17.4 million (!!) lines of Lean code [https://github.com/CocoSgt/riemann-hypothesis-lean].

At a dinner last night, someone asked me “so, how much longer until an AI proves the Riemann Hypothesis? Still several more years?” I replied, don’t be sure of such things anymore. After all, AI has already proved that the nontrivial zeros of the Riemann zeta function are at most 3/8 away from the critical line [https://www.quantamagazine.org/ai-proved-the-quasi-riemann-hypothesis-what-happens-now-20261019/] (Quanta just published its article on that breakthrough yesterday!), and that already carries most of the implications for number theory that a full proof of RH would have. So who the hell knows what else is possible, and how quickly?

For those tuning in from home, the Riemann Hypothesis [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemann_hypothesis] says (in its easiest-to-understand version) that the number of prime numbers from 1 to n is very close to what it would be if each integer x>1 had an independent 1/ln(x) probability of being prime, up to a standard statistical error term that never exceeds Õ(√n). While we’ve known since 1896 that the number of primes from 1 to n is asymptotic to n/ln(n) (that’s the famous Prime Number Theorem [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_number_theorem]), you could’ve imagined the deviations being much larger.

David Hilbert famously said, “If I were to awaken after having slept for a thousand years, my first question would be: Has the Riemann Hypothesis been proven?” Meanwhile G. H. Hardy, on a ship that he feared might sink, sent his colleagues a telegram claiming to have proven RH, so that if the worst came to pass, at least the world might believe that a proof sank with him.

Many will of course question whether the proof announced today will stand. They’ll point out that, despite how heavily the world is now “lean-ing” on it, the Lean formal verification system still has issues, from kernel bugs (which have, in the past, allowed proofs of 0=1) to uncertain soundness of the underlying type theory. In my view, these issues can and should urgently be fixed! Until they are, a sufficiently motivated AI could almost certainly exploit them to prove a false statement!

[Update (Nov 21): The proof has now been verified independently by the Lean community, using con-leche, a piece of Lean that itself is formally verified all the way down to the axioms of set theory. This is a much higher degree of verification than a typical human paper accepted to a math journal. The proof is correct.]

All the same, I see zero indications that that’s happening right now. I note that, so far, not one of the AI results with Lean certificates has needed to be retracted (while several without Lean certificates have needed to be). So I’m not 100% sure, but my bet is that the proof will stand.

Mathematicians of course won’t be satisfied with 17 million lines of Lean. They’ll be satisfied when, and only when, they’re able to explain and distill and reconstruct the ideas for themselves. And that will probably take years, since human mathematicians move only at biological speeds. And while they’re working on it, how many more such wonders will there be, in this age of wonders and terrors?

My 9-year-old son, informed that RH was proved, replied that he’s not even surprised at this point. Maybe you need to have come of age in the human math era to be surprised by such things.

The P=?NP problem now moves firmly into place as the Final Boss of the Math We’ve Cared About, the position I always thought it should occupy. But for how much longer? For how much longer will anything last that we currently take for granted?

It was only a couple days ago that my wife and I had the following conversation:

Dana: I turned off the irrigation, yet somehow water in our house is still leaking. We need to…

Me: Look, a lot of the smartest people I know now think that recursive self-improvement and the end of the entire known world are coming in 12-18 months. This leak…

Dana: We should still fix the leak.

Aaronson-Ambainis Conjecture proved! (By AI, of course) [https://scottaaronson.blog/?p=10295]

 November 8th, 2026 

The “For Dummies” Version (apologies for not including this earlier):

Yes, an AI solved a big open problem about quantum computing

No, this doesn’t kill quantum supremacy, or change how we build quantum computers, or whatever else you read on Twitter

What it does, is to rule out a potential class of quantum speedups that most of us didn’t believe would exist anyway (namely, those that would compute a yes-or-no property of exponentially many random bits, superpolynomially faster than any classical algorithm)

It means my prediction was right, so you should listen to me more in the future!

We now, of course, live in age where longstanding open problems in quantum computing theory (and all theoretical computer science, and all math) are dropping like flies, to the extent that there’s no way I could possibly do a post on each and every bombshell. But I can’t exactly avoid the Aaronson-Ambainis Conjecture, given that my name is on it, and that I cared about the answer to this one (back in 2019, I even blogged [https://scottaaronson.blog/?p=4414] about an attempted solution that ultimately turned out to be fallacious).

For those tuning in from home, the AA Conjecture, which Andris and I posed [https://arxiv.org/abs/0911.0996] back in 2009, says that every degree-d polynomial p:{0,1}n→[0,1] in n Boolean variables, with constant variance, has some variable whose influence is at least 1/poly(d). The reason Andris and I cared about this conjecture is that, as we proved, it implies that there’s no superpolynomial advantage of quantum query complexity over classical query complexity for decision problems that works on most Boolean inputs. Or, in terms of complexity classes, that there’s no hope of separating P from BQP relative to a random oracle, without proving P≠BQP or something of the kind in the unrelativized world. This would finally answer a question of Fortnow and Rogers [https://arxiv.org/abs/cs/9811023] from 1998.

The connection between quantum algorithms and polynomials came from the famous 1998 paper of Beals et al. [https://arxiv.org/abs/quant-ph/9802049], which showed that, if Q is a quantum algorithm that makes T queries to an oracle X, then Pr[Q(X) accepts] can be written as a multivariate real polynomial p(X) in the bits of X, of degree at most 2T. The proof of my and Andris’s result is then simply this: take the polynomial p, find in it a variable of influence at least 1/poly(T), and query that variable, and keep repeating until there’s no longer such an influential variable—at which point, by assumption, p is nearly a constant polynomial, which means you’ve learned whether Q accepts with high probability over X. This provides a classical simulation of the quantum algorithm Q—a simulation that makes poly(T) queries to the oracle X and that works for most X’s.

The AA Conjecture caught on to an extent Andris and I couldn’t have predicted. Some who worked on it barely even cared about the implication for quantum computing, and just wanted to solve the problem about polynomials! There were, I’m both proud and sorry to report, Fourier analysis experts and grad students who oriented their whole research programs around trying to prove AA, with lots of proofs of special cases, some of them very [https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.19158] recent [https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.20297]. A proof of full AA was one of the last remaining open problems in quantum query complexity that everyone seemed to agree was centrally important. I was surprised, honestly, to see it missing from OpenAI’s Mathocalypse dump [https://scottaaronson.blog/?p=10169] a few weeks ago. Surely they tried it, so was it really so much harder than all the problems that were solved?

Now a startup called Physical Superintelligence has announced a proof of the AA Conjecture [https://psi.inc/research/aaronson-ambainis/], specifically showing that there’s a variable of influence at least ~1/d6, which they say they obtained using their proprietary research harness on an internal Claude model. (What role the harness played, compared to the internal model, is something I don’t know.) The effort was led by David Turturean and Jim Halverson at PSI. They actually let me know about this several weeks ago, but I had to keep it secret until the announcement late last week.

I haven’t yet understood the proof, although I hope to. In the meantime, though, I believe the proof is correct for two reasons. Firstly, there’s a Lean certificate, and secondly, human experts (I’m no longer an expert on my own conjecture!) have already gone over it and understood it. In fact, there’s already a beautiful-looking exposition [https://guyblanc.github.io/AA_Exposition.pdf] written by Guy Blanc of MIT and Jordan Docter, Carmen Strassle, and Li-Yang Tan of Stanford, which looks like a perfect starting point for those seeking to go much deeper. Or, for those seeking to go only slightly deeper, PSI’s blog post on the advance [https://psi.inc/research/aaronson-ambainis/] is sufficiently well-written that it saves me from the need to write too much more here.

Alas, as reported [https://x.com/quantumashley/status/2119160138711904463] by Ashley Montanaro, there were the usual forehead-banging misconceptions about what this result means for quantum computing. So for example, Pedro Domingos of U. of Washington blasted out to his 137,000 X followers [https://x.com/pmddomingos/status/2118865209637179826] that the proof means that “the dream of quantum supremacy is dead.” The truth, as Pedro knows perfectly well (or easily could know), is that it rules out one particular type of quantum supremacy (namely, for yes-or-no problems relative to random oracles) that we’d already conjectured didn’t exist anyway. It doesn’t undermine any form of quantum supremacy that we did conjecture to exist! It has zero effect, for example, on Shor’s factoring algorithm, which uses a highly non-random (periodic) oracle for the key internal step. It has zero effect on the simulation of quantum physics and chemistry, which generally doesn’t depend on oracle algorithms at all. Even just relative to random oracles, the Yamakawa-Zhandry breakthrough [https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.02063] from 2022 dramatically underscored that, if we switch our attention from decision problems to other kind of problems (in Yamakawa and Zhandry’s case, to NP search problems), then exponential quantum speedups do become possible. Anyway, this was sufficiently intellectually dishonest on Pedro’s part that it makes me disinclined to take him seriously about anything ever again [ADDED: I appreciate that Pedro has now posted a partial correction [https://x.com/pmddomingos/status/2119862160398639365?s=46], saying “or maybe not”].

But, OK, given how radically new it is to have an AI solve so many of our longstanding open problems, I suppose “people are wildly misinterpreting a QC advance on social media” is an almost comfortingly familiar problem to have!

If you’re wondering how I feel about the proof of AA—well, much like my wife Dana with the Unique Games Conjecture, it’s many different emotions at once.

Satisfied that our conjecture turned out to be true after all (it’s nice to be 100% right about something from time to time!)

Awestruck by the new reality of the world in which we live in

Disappointed that humans didn’t get to snag this conjecture before the robots did (with some more years, I think it’s very likely that they would have)

Fatigued by the endless wonders from Aladdin’s Cave, the neverending buffet of solutions to longstanding open problems, wondering when if ever we’ll have time to digest it all

Anyway, huge congratulations to David Turturean and Jim Halverson, to Physical Superintelligence, and of course to Claude on this achievement!

Talk by Dana Moshkovitz: The Unique Games Conjecture – Proved (!!?) [https://scottaaronson.blog/?p=10232]

 October 30th, 2026 

 [https://scottaaronson.blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/image-3.png]

In my “Mathocalypse” post, I mentioned [https://scottaaronson.blog/?p=10169] that my wife Dana—who’s been working on Subhash Khot’s Unique Games Conjecture [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unique_games_conjecture] since ~2009, for the entire time I’ve known her—has for the past few weeks (while on sabbatical at the Simons Institute in Berkeley) been spending essentially all of her time laboring to understand OpenAI’s recent proof of the conjecture [https://github.com/openai/math/blob/main/preprints/The-Unique-Games-Theorem-September-23-2026/paper.pdf]. Having now studied the proof closely in collaboration with Subhash Khot himself, Johan Håstad, Dor Minzer, and other colleagues, and (naturally) GPT-6 Astra, Dana probably now understands this crown jewel of OpenAI’s release as well as anyone does. This week, she gave two well-received whiteboard talks on the proof at Simons Institute. So, I and others encouraged her to give the same talks next week at UT Austin when she’s back here, and Dana agreed! Because of the large timely interest, we’re going to do a live Zoom option, and will also record the talk for later sharing on YouTube. Please note: While all are welcome, this is a 2-hour technical talk. The first hour will review key ideas from hardness of approximation, while the second hour will describe the new construction. —SA

Title: The Unique Games Conjecture – Proved (!!?)

Speaker: Dana Moshkovitz [https://www.cs.utexas.edu/~danama/] (UT Austin)

When: Wednesday Nov. 4, 3:30-5:30pm US Central (with a brief break in the middle)

Where: UT Austin, Gates Dell Complex 6.302. Live Zoom will be available here [https://utexas.zoom.us/j/99833119884], and a recording will be available later.

Abstract: The Unique Games Conjecture of Khot says that it is NP-hard, given a system of linear equations of the form xi-xj=bij (mod p) over variables x1…xn, where 99% fraction of the equations can be satisfied, to find an assignment satisfying 1% of the equations (here p is a sufficiently large constant).

This conjecture implies optimal inapproximability results for many optimization problems. 

A few weeks ago OpenAI released a writeup with a proof of the Unique Games Conjecture, as well as a Lean formalization of the proof. The writeup, like others released that day, is not written in a way that’s especially readable to human mathematicians. 

My goal in this talk is to offer my mental map of the proof, in a way that’s helpful to theoretical computer scientists who are familiar with the area. I think of the innovation as a new code construction, “Hadamard with a noise test.” I’ll sketch how it fits into the NP-hardness reduction. Then I’ll describe the code construction fully and sketch its soundness analysis. 

I’ll also point out a couple of ingredients of the proof that are of independent interest, like a simple quadratic that behaves “like a linear,” and a probability bound using a trick with harmonic numbers.

Vote! (Israeli edition) [https://scottaaronson.blog/?p=10235]

“1. What is something about Israel that you admire/appreciate over the years?

2. What are you worried the most about Israel these days?”

So I replied as follows:

“It’s hard to know where to start with either of your questions! But briefly:

Tiny Israel standing up for itself, against an often hostile and unjust world, is a huge part of what’s given me the self-confidence to stand up for myself in my own life. That was true even before I had visited Israel or spent significant time there, gazed across the desert at the still-clear ruins of the Roman siege of Masada, staggered overwhelmed out of Yad Vashem into the sunlight of Jerusalem, tasted the hummus or pomegranate juice or ice cream with blended fruit, befriended or had academic collaborations with dozens of Israelis, or married an Israeli woman.

My fear is merely the ‘obvious’ one — that, in response to the staggering hostility directed against the ‘Jew among the nations,’ Israel will become more and more belligerent, isolated, and right-wing. It will get demographically overwhelmed by those who don’t want to work or serve in the army, while hundreds of thousands of secular educated Israelis leave. It will be internally divided almost to the point of civil war. It will repudiate the liberal-democratic vision of Herzl and Ben-Gurion, of world-class universities and making the deserts bloom, for the swaggering, theocratic vision of Smotrich and Ben-Gvir, of chasing Palestinians from their olive groves. It will become more and more like the hateful caricature of itself that the anti-Israel people in the West always imagined. And then, having been internally weakened in all of these ways, it will finally be destroyed by its external enemies, who will never have rested the whole time, who never once wavered in their dream of a second Holocaust.

I hope and pray that in the upcoming election, the Israeli voters make optimistic choices that repudiate these fears.”

As I’ll do more and more with political posts, I’m leaving the comments off. Why? Because my remaining life is too short to spend it battling an endless parade of trolls and griefers, leftists and rightists and just nihilists, who might or might not be AI.

Vote! [https://scottaaronson.blog/?p=10222]

 October 21st, 2026 

Today, I take a break from your regularly-scheduled AI mathocalypse programming to urge my American readers to vote in the Congressional midterms, early if possible, if you haven’t already. Especially if your state faces one or more major contests, as does my adopted home state of Texas.

Me, I voted straight-ticket Democrat today, as I’ve done in every election since I became eligible over a quarter-century ago (except for when I vote-swapped with someone from another state).

So yes, I voted for the guy who studied theology a short walk from my house, and who seems to have really internalized the whole Jesus thing about kindness to everyone—

 [https://scottaaronson.blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/image-1.png]

—over his sneering, thuggish, cartoonishly mafia-like [https://www.houstonpublicmedia.org/articles/news/politics/election-2026/2026/06/22/555172/texas-ken-paxton-james-talarico-senate-election-scandals/] opponent. Fingers crossed that Talarico pulls off a historic upset for our state (he’s currently a 2-to-1 favorite [https://polymarket.com/event/texas-senate-election-winner] in the prediction markets).

The Texas Republicans did everything they possibly could to make this choice easy for me, by being corrupt, conspiratorial, racist, and Taliban-like even by the jaw-droppingly debased standards of MAGA in 2026. As I draw breath, I could never vote for a slate one of whose candidates attacked UT students [https://www.dallasnews.com/news/politics/article/students-speak-after-bo-french-attack-22431674.php] for the crime of being visibly Southeast Asian while cheering their school’s football victory from the stands—and whose other candidates could barely bring themselves to condemn this knuckle-dragging racist, for fear of angering their base. (It’s like, didn’t you want immigrants to assimilate to American culture? What more could these kids possibly do, short of skin bleach?)

All the same, this was also the first US election in my lifetime where I felt like my vote was genuinely in play—especially when I listened to Talarico’s condemnations of Israel, for having the temerity to fight the same kind of war any random other nation would’ve fought had it been attacked as Israel was on October 7. When Hasan Piker came to speak to a cheering crowd here at UT Austin, I actually wrote to someone I know in the Talarico campaign, to ask whether Talarico would show enough balls to condemn Piker’s many antisemitic and anti-American statements, and renounce his support. My contact was nice enough to reply to me, saying: don’t worry, James is taking care to be far away from Austin during Piker’s visit, so he won’t need to engage at all!

So then, this is my candidate: the guy who’s strategically silent on the wildly-popular streamer who’d gladly see my Israeli family members raped and beheaded, rather than campaigning with that streamer like Abdul El-Sayed did in Michigan. I unironically believe that, for Talarico, it took genuine moral courage to resist the whole momentum of his party’s youth even to this extent.

To earn my vote in the near future under these circumstances, all a Texas Republican would need to do is be somewhat sane or reasonable. He or she might affirm, for example, that racism is bad, that abortion is a woman’s choice, that Christianity is not the state religion of the US, that Covid vaccines work, that human emissions are worryingly changing the earth’s climate, and/or that Joe Biden won in 2020, while still being arbitrarily right-wing on taxes and other stuff. He or she could then simply wait for the Democrats to descend fully into the worldview of the masked hooligans who are currently setting France’s beautiful cities aflame, terrorizing its shopkeepers, and looting as many Louis Vuitton handbags as they can carry … all for Palestine, of course, and for the death of global capitalism, and for the violent overthrow of the scheming, money-grubbing, hook-nosed, international Talmudic … err, I mean “Zionists.”

What I’m trying to say is, it was a genuine achievement on the part of the Texas Republicans to fail to earn a single vote from me this cycle.
