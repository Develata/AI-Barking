Goldman Sachs AI Coding Restrictions Frustrate Its Traders and Bankers

 By Abdelaziz Fathi [https://financefeeds.com/author/abdelaziz/] 

 November 4, 2026 [https://financefeeds.com/2026/11/04/] 

 Share 

Preferred source [https://www.google.com/preferences/source?q=financefeeds.com]

Goldman Sachs has restricted advanced AI coding tools such as Anthropic’s Claude Code and Microsoft’s GitHub products to engineering staff after reviewing their productivity benefits and risk implications, reversing a period in which traders and bankers were allowed to experiment with the technology.

Internal guidance updated in October limits access to the coding tools to engineers, according to Bloomberg. Some traders had been using them to monitor client trading flows, build analytical material and model changes in markets and positions.

The decision does not amount to a broader retreat from artificial intelligence at Goldman. Instead, it draws a tighter boundary around which employees can use AI systems capable of writing and modifying code inside a financial institution where analytical errors, data handling and production controls can have direct operational consequences.

Why Did Goldman Pull AI Coding Tools From Traders?

Goldman said it had deliberately allowed employees across different parts of the firm to test AI tools before deciding where they were most appropriate.

“We make certain AI tools available to our people to determine the best use cases across different parts of the firm,” Goldman spokesperson Nick Carcaterra said. “After a period of experimentation where we assess a variety of factors including productivity and risk management, we put them in the hands of those who are best suited to leverage them for the long term.”

The restriction suggests Goldman found a difference between using generative AI for research or assistance and allowing non-engineers to create software that may influence trading, client analysis or internal workflows.

That distinction is becoming more important across financial markets. FinanceFeeds has previously examined the additional risks that emerge when AI moves from providing analysis to generating code or executing financial tasks [https://financefeeds.com/beyond-the-chatbot-securing-ai-execution-in-the-forex-securities-crypto-and-derivatives-cfds-industry/], where an apparently plausible output can have operational consequences.

Investor Takeaway

Goldman’s decision suggests the productivity case for AI is being judged differently depending on the workflow. Generating a draft or summarizing research carries a different risk profile from producing code used in trading or market analysis.

What Happened to Goldman’s “Vibe Coding” Experiment?

The restriction follows months in which Goldman executives had discussed the potential for employees without traditional software-development backgrounds to create tools using natural-language prompts.

Chief Information Officer Marco Argenti had described how non-technical employees could use AI to prototype software and then pass those projects to professional engineers for further development. The approach, often called “vibe coding,” offered a way for employees closest to a business problem to create an initial solution without waiting for a development team.

But that model also creates a governance problem. AI-generated code can work during a limited test while still containing errors, weak controls or assumptions that become problematic when it is used more widely.

The contrast is visible elsewhere in financial technology. Bloomberg recently added an AI coding assistant to its BQuant platform [https://financefeeds.com/bloomberg-adds-ai-coding-assistant-to-bquant-as-data-workflows-converge/], but requires user approval before generated code can be inserted or executed inside a notebook. The design keeps human review between the AI-generated proposal and the resulting action.

Investor Takeaway

The productivity benefit of allowing business users to create their own tools has to be weighed against validation and maintenance costs. A prototype that saves a trader time can become a risk if nobody is clearly responsible for testing, reviewing and supporting the underlying code.

Is Goldman Pulling Back From AI More Broadly?

There is little evidence that Goldman is reducing its overall AI investment. The bank continues to use artificial intelligence across engineering and financial-market workflows, while senior management has repeatedly discussed AI as an important part of its technology strategy.

Goldman has also been expanding its involvement with AI-enabled trading infrastructure. Earlier this year, Goldman Sachs joined Broadridge’s AI-driven LTX corporate bond platform as a liquidity provider [https://financefeeds.com/goldman-sachs-and-jpmorgan-join-ltx-as-fixed-income-liquidity-providers/], alongside JPMorgan and several other large banks.

The current restriction is therefore better understood as a decision about access and controls rather than whether Goldman intends to use AI.

The bank has already taken similar steps where model access created other forms of risk. Goldman previously restricted Claude access for employees in Hong Kong [https://financefeeds.com/crypto-com-to-exit-hong-kong-retail-market-as-exchange-license-remains-pending/] after determining that its agreement with Anthropic did not cover use in the territory, demonstrating that availability can be constrained by legal and contractual considerations as well as technical ones.

What Does Goldman’s Decision Mean for AI Adoption on Wall Street?

The episode illustrates a broader issue facing banks as generative AI becomes capable of doing more than drafting text. Coding agents can now create analytical scripts, automate workflows and interact with datasets, making the boundary between an employee productivity tool and production software increasingly difficult to define.

For regulated financial firms, that boundary matters because code affecting trading, risk calculations or client information normally passes through controls covering testing, permissions, auditability and change management.

Goldman’s latest policy leaves advanced coding tools available to engineers while removing them from most business users. That preserves access to the technology while concentrating responsibility for software creation among employees already operating within those development controls.

Investor Takeaway

The next phase of AI adoption at large banks may be defined less by which models firms can access and more by who is permitted to use them for high-impact tasks. Governance, review and accountability are becoming part of the productivity calculation.
