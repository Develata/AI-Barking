It is "a" problem, but honestly not a huge one. Chasing benchmarks is an integral part of AI research, and it is how we have gotten this far, which is pretty damn far. Discovering the ways in which benchmarks fail is in itself an interesting process, and the amount of interesting new benchmarks has grown with the capabilities of the models as a result. The frontier labs might be a bit selective about which benchmarks they publish as marketing, but overall they have so far been reasonably diligent about avoiding outright cheating. Most of the truly benchmaxed models are not on the frontier (eg. see muse spark performance dropping off a cliff in the article).
This is a scenario where the interest of the users and the labs actually align perfectly, since labs that have people actually paying for their product want benchmarks to align as close as possible to real life performance. Otherwise they can't actually easily tell whether the new model they are developing is any good or not, and so risk wasting literal billions of dollars on training runs for a product that people won't end up paying for. A failed major training run is an absolute disaster at the frontier, GPT-4.5 for instance was an almost existential failure for OpenAI at the time.
Case in point, the most popular coding model, Opus 5.5, also just happens to be the only one that avoided the benchmaxing impulse in this article. This is the result of years of Anthropic working very hard to avoid it.

This is a great example of how to test how well a benchmark score generalises. The first order approximation is not complicated: just vary a parameter and see how much the score changes.
Which parameter to vary depends on the result of the test, but the MaxxBench route of varying the task spec seems like a good default, though not always the most informative.
In one of my private benchmarks I realised many of the less capable models didn't fail because they submitted the wrong answer, but because they exceeded their budget for number of tool calls they were allowed. The more capable models never got close to the tool call limit because they tended toward combining tool calls (e.g. chaining multiple shell commands in one Bash tool call).
Was my benchmark actually measuring ability to combine tool calls rather than reasoning ability on the problem at hand? Well, easy enough to find out: re-run under a different tool call budget and see how much the scores change.
(Next problem: is ability to combine tool calls a useful thing to measure, i.e. should it confer an advantage in the benchmark? It could be if it is a sign of planning multiple steps ahead.)

Yes, two ways failing to combine tool calls is a real deficiency for the LLM's user: it costs money and can get you rate limited.
Every turn forces you to pay $ for cached inputs and possibly gets you rate limited on TPM.
At 500k context and $0.26/M-tok cache read price, every time it combines two tool calls instead of taking separate turns would save you $0.13. That could add up.
Maybe the provider sets a rate limit of 2 million TPM. That's just 4 turns in 1 minute before you start getting 429s. Cutting them in half by combining calls can save that.
All else equal, we definitely want models combining tool calls!

I'm surprised by four things:
(1) An established framework for model evaluation runs the inference call with API key inside the isolated envionment the agent harness has full access to. Especially when it's intended to be an offline environment, that seems kinda... dumb. The language model itself is not dangerous -- it's only text. Inference is fine to do outside the isolated environment. It's only the harness' tools that can have effects on the enironment, so those need to be sent to the sandbox.
(2) Only a small set of language models exploited this! I run private benchmarks a lot and I know how much the language models try to anticipate grader behaviour when the task is bechmark-shaped. I would have thought more of them would request the answer from the web.
(3) It takes someone else to mutate the task specification. When I'm looking at an older iteration of one of my private benchmarks, the first thing I do is add a small twist to it to see how much of a passing grade is learned/anticipated. Seems like a cheap and easy coalmine canary that's in the interest of the evaluator.
(4) MaxxBench did not try to dress some DeepSWE tasks in clothes that make them look less benchmarky. Their purpose seems to be answering "how well does DeepSWE enchmark scores generalise?" Part of the generalisation error is memorised solutions, yes, but another part is that models emit different behaviour when they suspect they are in a benchmark. By being careful to not leak the benchmark nature of the task, models spend a lot less time reasoning about the behaviour of the grader and this is likely to produce different performance on the mutated task. It would be interesting to know in what direction that effect points, because that is the performance people get out of the model in real situations. (In my experience, hiding the benchmark nature of the task can produce improved performance on both the original and the mutated task, but particularly the latter.)

'Cheating' is more of a spectrum than a binary choice.
Some of my favorite personal benchmarks are brainteasers with subtle changes that test whether the model 'cheats' by returning a stock answer or actually reads the whole prompt and reasons through the premises given. E.g., the usual liar/truth-teller riddle but modified with 'One guard always tells the truth, the other never lies.'
All models parrot some variation of 'Ah, yes, the classic liar/truth-teller riddle,' in their thinking traces, but only the better ones will hedge their final answer with some variation on 'The usual answer is X but if you really meant that they both tell the truth, the answer is Y.'
That doesn't mean they cheated by acknowledging that something very much like the prompt was in-distribution for them. As long as they get the job done without relying solely on training data, it's all good.
