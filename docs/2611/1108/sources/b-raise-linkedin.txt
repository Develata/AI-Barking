Day 2's late morning followed the money: what AI really costs to run, and who pays to build it.

Marco Argenti [https://www.linkedin.com/in/marcoargenti?trk=public_post-text] (Goldman Sachs [https://www.linkedin.com/company/goldman-sachs?trk=public_post-text]), with Tom Mackenzie [https://uk.linkedin.com/in/tom-mackenzie-a122768?trk=public_post-text] (Bloomberg [https://www.linkedin.com/company/bloomberg?trk=public_post-text]), opened on the mindset shift. He's seeing a steady 20% productivity lift, with engineers no longer asking for more people but for more to do, and development turning prototype-driven rather than spec-driven: business teams vibe-code something that looks right, engineers then harden it into real material. But he drew one firm line.

Marco Argenti [https://www.linkedin.com/in/marcoargenti?trk=public_post-text]: "What I don't outsource is decisions. I like to make the final call, that's human judgment, and I haven't seen evidence that AI judgment is superior yet."

Then the economics. In Cheap Tokens, Expensive Mistakes, Dylan Patel [https://www.linkedin.com/in/dylanpatelsa?trk=public_post-text] (SemiAnalysis [https://www.linkedin.com/company/semianalysis?trk=public_post-text]) and Liran Zvibel [https://www.linkedin.com/in/liranzvibel?trk=public_post-text] (WEKA [https://www.linkedin.com/company/weka-io?trk=public_post-text]), with Karen Kwok [https://www.linkedin.com/in/karenkwoknews?trk=public_post-text] (Reuters Breakingviews [https://www.linkedin.com/company/breakingviews?trk=public_post-text]), laid out how sharply inference has turned. Patel's read: demand is insatiable, nobody has enough compute, and the labs now hold real pricing power, with OpenAI gross margins around 55%. Zvibel's angle: the newest GPU alone doesn't win, layer in storage and memory and a lesser chip can outperform, with cache-hit rates above 95% freeing GPUs to generate instead of recompute.

And underneath all of it, the financing question. The Trillion Dollar Question panel, with Erwan Menard [https://www.linkedin.com/in/erwanmenard?trk=public_post-text] (Crusoe), Kent Draper [https://www.linkedin.com/in/kent-draper?trk=public_post-text] (IREN [https://au.linkedin.com/company/iren?trk=public_post-text]), Tim Davis [https://www.linkedin.com/in/timdavisau?trk=public_post-text] (Modular [https://www.linkedin.com/company/modular-ai?trk=public_post-text]), Wayne Nelms [https://www.linkedin.com/in/wayne-nelms?trk=public_post-text] (Ornn [https://www.linkedin.com/company/ornn?trk=public_post-text] ) Jeremie Eliahou Ontiveros [https://fr.linkedin.com/in/eliahoujeremie?trk=public_post-text] (SemiAnalysis [https://www.linkedin.com/company/semianalysis?trk=public_post-text]) and James Krellenstein [https://www.linkedin.com/in/james-krellenstein-ba80102b?trk=public_post-text] (Alva Energy [https://www.linkedin.com/company/alva-energy?trk=public_post-text]), mapped the capital stack: Menard's three ways to sell infrastructure, megawatts, GPU-hours or tokens, Draper's A-rated GPU debt backed by a Microsoft offtake, Davis on CUDA lock-in as the real moat, Nelms on building a futures market for compute. Krellenstein went one layer lower still.

James Krellenstein: "We're not in the data center business, we're in the business of providing the megawatts so that Crusoe can sell megawatts."
Mindset. Margins. Megawatts. The closer you look at AI at scale, the more it comes down to what it costs, and who can finance it.
