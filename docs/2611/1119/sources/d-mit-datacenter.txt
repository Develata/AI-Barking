Study reveals data centers’ long-distance damage to air quality 

 Researchers find air pollution from data center electricity use can affect communities far from the facilities, with some of the highest impacts in the Midwest. 

 Adam Zewe

The rapid expansion of data centers for artificial intelligence causes negative health and air quality impacts in regions far from where the brick-and-mortar facilities are located, according to a new study by MIT researchers.

The team estimates that additional air pollution from the electricity demands of data centers in 2023 resulted in about 650 premature deaths across the United States. 

Some Midwestern states had among the highest estimated rates of premature deaths per capita attributable to data centers, even though much of the data center electricity demand was concentrated in Virginia and Texas.

The researchers built a modeling framework that captures how the electricity demanded by data centers propagates through the nation’s interconnected power grid. The model also considers how atmospheric conditions impact the formation of air pollution in different regions.

The framework identifies power plants across the country whose emissions increase in response to data center electricity demand. In parts of the Midwest, these emissions combine with atmospheric conditions that favor the formation of fine particulate matter, a pollutant that can cause heart attacks, stroke, and lung disease.

The researchers say these results underscore the importance of considering unintended regional health impacts of data centers, and how the location and operations of these facilities affect public health on a broader scale.

“When people think about the negative impacts of data centers, they usually focus on the neighbors. But our study indicates that the air quality impacts of data centers are very widespread across a large region. Even if you don’t think you are affected because there is not a data center near you, think again,” says Noelle Selin, a professor in the MIT Institute for Data, Systems, and Society (IDSS) and the Department of Earth, Atmospheric and Planetary Sciences (EAPS); director of the Center for Sustainability Science and Strategy; and senior author of the study.

Selin is joined on the paper by Yuang (Albert) Chen, an MIT graduate student; as well as others at MIT, the University of California at Irvine, and Yale University. The research appears today in Environmental Research Letters [https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.1088/1748-9326/aebbd4].

Capturing regional impacts

The Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory estimated [https://escholarship.org/uc/item/32d6m0d1] that electricity demands of data centers represented about 4 percent of total U.S. energy demand in 2023 and resulted in about 4.4 percent of the nation’s overall emissions. 

That proportion has likely increased as new data centers continue to be built at a rapid pace.

Many prior studies estimate impacts using average electricity-grid emissions. Like those studies, the researchers assume data centers draw power to run computing tasks 24/7. Their approach also identifies which generators respond to that additional demand hour by hour, allowing them to trace where the resulting emissions and health impacts occur, Chen explains.

“Generators that respond to additional electricity demand are often natural gas plants, although the response varies by place and hour,” he says.

To drill down on regional health impacts, the MIT researchers developed a modeling approach that enables them to identify which generators are contributing to data center electricity demand within each modeled region. 

This allows them to incorporate a spatial component into the analysis.

They built an electricity system model that simulates how much electricity each generator produces and pumps into the grid every hour. This model also considers how that electricity is transmitted between regions. 

It gives the researchers data on emissions of nitrogen oxides (NOx) and sulfur dioxide (SO2) for each generator across the United States. NOx contributes to the formation of ground-level ozone, or smog, while both NOx and SO2 contribute to the formation of fine particulate matter (PM2.5) in the atmosphere.

“It was a challenge to gather all the data,” Chen says. “There are more than 10,000 generators in the U.S. grid, and we needed to make sure the data provided for each generator is valid and validated across other data sources.”

The researchers incorporated these data into a chemical transport model. This model simulates how pollutants form and travel through the atmosphere across regions. 

From there, they translated the additional air pollution from data center operations into premature mortality rates using evidence from epidemiological literature.

The model estimates that emissions from data centers caused about 650 premature deaths in 2023 (513 from PM2.5 and 137 from smog), which represents about 5.3 percent of the nation’s total electricity-related mortality. 

“While it is not surprising that any additional source of air pollution will result in mortalities, with data centers, when and where these deaths are occurring are different than you might expect,” Selin says.

A pollution mismatch

The premature mortality rates were highest in Iowa, Nevada, South Dakota, and Kansas, but the bulk of the electricity demand was concentrated in northern Virginia and in Texas. Premature mortality rates in Virginia and Texas are only about half as much as most Midwestern states.

One reason for this mismatch is the interconnected nature of the power grid, Chen says. 

Generators that respond to the increased electricity demand in Virginia are likely located in the Ohio Valley, where there is a higher proportion of older power plants that generate more emissions from burning coal and natural gas. 

“Different regions have different mixes of power. If you turn on a major new demand source, by plugging a data center into the grid, you might have to fire up your really dirty power plant to meet that new demand,” Selin says.

Plus, due to atmospheric conditions, many pollutants generated in these regions are transported to downwind states.

Midwest states like Nebraska and Iowa are traditionally agricultural regions. Additional ammonia in the atmosphere from fertilizer enhances the formation of PM2.5. The estimated health impacts also extend to Kansas, South Dakota, Missouri, and Arkansas, illustrating that the effects can reach states with relatively few data centers.

The study shows that rural populations are expected to have significantly higher mortality risk from data center electricity demand than urban populations, Chen says.

“When people are thinking about data center siting and operations, it is important to consider more than just things like land and energy prices. They should also take air quality considerations into the equation,” he says. “This is a good baseline to think about where we should build data centers and how we should supply those data centers with electricity.”

In the future, the researchers want to consider air quality impacts of onsite generation — a growing trend where data centers incorporate power plants that supply electricity. They could also study the flexibility of data center operations, and how adaptively adjusting electricity demand could impact air quality. 

This research was funded, in part, by the MIT Energy Initiative Society of Energy Scholars and the MIT Martin Family Society of Fellows for Sustainability.
