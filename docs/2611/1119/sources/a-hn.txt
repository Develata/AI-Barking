> "I am interested to have end products with really good UX driven by peak performance and optimizations [..] Instead what I see is that everyone is in a race to have as much product out as possible by the same standards that applied to products made by humans"
AI is not equipped for this. Humans are part of the experience process, and AI doesn't "understand" individual humans better than other humans do.
UX relies very much on human psychology. That's why, despite all the efforts of scoring it, UX is not an objective metric:
1. AI doesn't know what "good UX" is, as this is judged by the sum of humans using it. --> Structured data of actual usage would be needed for AI to ingest, in a way that it can derive intent from every action and identify friction.
2. A "good UX" is not a transferrable metric. The best UX for a ticket vending machine is not applicable to video-editing software. --> AI would need to understand what to apply when, and at which point it needs to evolve its expectation to the user
3. A "good UX" is not an absolute metric. Photoshop/Premiere/... has a UX which was extended and refined over decades, it is considered good by experienced users but still overwhelmingly confusing for beginners. --> Someone would need to scope the AI for the correct target-group
All this is done today by doing extensive user-testing with actual humans. There are people experienced in this who can create better UX-designs from scratch than others can, and still we keep learning something when we put a group of humans in front of it and watch them use it.
"Intelligence" alone doesn't solve this.
I'm reminded of the story of Stephen Hawking's sister, telling how he invented a "better board game" as a child and always wanted to play it with her, which was apparently the most frustrating and boring game she ever played...

I think we are starting to see this in some areas: For example, for years desktop apps were built on top of slow and heavy cross-platform frameworks like Electron, because writing them natively was too burdensome. Now we're seeing rewrites in rust using native APIs and toolkits, resulting in lighter and faster apps.
Software engineering as a discipline is also shifting, and will continue shifting, to a process of continuous automated improvement. In the past, test suites were mostly a way to catch regressions. In future, they will be essential to provide a verified baseline of correct behaviour, and then you will have models looped in the background to continually merge performance and reliability improvements. Similarly you will have error and performance logs fed directly into agents to resolve incidents as soon as they arise.
We are in the baby steps of these changes at the moment, but I think in at most ten years, all non-hobbyist software will be built this way.

The performance per watt of very slow CPUs is always very good, so that alone is not impressive unless much more data is provided to prove that is really better than what is typical.
On the other hand, the performance per area of the "traditional" CPU that is used for comparison, which I assume that it is some kind of RISC-V, is very poor, so that the performance per area of the new CPU is likely to be also poor, even if much better than that of the strawman.
Their "traditional" CPU has an area that is slightly bigger than that of Cortex-A720 made with a CMOS process almost twice as dense.
So if they were made with the same process, the "traditional" CPU and the new CPU would have an area somewhat bigger than a half of a Cortex-A720.
But Cortex-A720 is an out-of-order 4-wide superscalar core with advanced SIMD and scalable vector extensions, with a good branch predictor and with many other enhancements, for which the area ratio in the same process is expected to be much greater than 2 in comparison with the CPUs evaluated in TFA, while its performance must be several times greater than that of a 2-wide in-order CPU core.
So both the "traditional" CPU and the Frostbite CPU appear to have a much bigger area than expected for their performance.
Moreover, the performance of in-order CPUs is very sensitive to the benchmark selection and to the quality of the compiler optimizations.
So the performance obtained for the benchmarks selected by them, like FFT or LU decomposition, is expected to be much better than for benchmarks that would execute an application not dominated by such regular loops, but having lots of hard to predict branches, e.g. a compiler benchmark.
They say that the ISA will be published. At that time it will be easier to appreciate whether this CPU architecture is really innovative. For now, they have done a lot of work, by fully implementing the CPU core, but the data presented is not sufficiently convincing to demonstrate that it is indeed an architecture that will be able to compete with SOTA CPU cores, e.g. with those from the Arm company.

The second paragraph of the linked page contains their first performance claim. It reads:
> On a set of 25 initially chosen workloads, including 15 held out until the final evaluation, we achieved a 4.59x performance-per-watt multiple when compared to a modern, silicon-proven superscalar (dual-issue, in-order) CPU core.
They do start claiming performance per area in the following paragraph, but that's not an interesting claim for a couple of different reasons:
- As I said above, MISC designs get dramatically better performance per area than any mainstream CPU.
- We're already in the dark-silicon era, where even the relatively low performance-per-area designs used in laptops and cellphones must thermally throttle on a regular basis to avoid burning themselves up, and contain many specialized accelerators used only a small fraction of the time (AES-NI, AVX-512, saturating 8-bit arithmetic, popcount, etc.) instead of additional small general-purpose cores that could run all the time.
Their claims would be a lot more interesting if they provided some kind of absolute numbers to compare against.

Not all CPU/GPU architectures have a single global memory space. AMD GCN GPUs have global VRAM, local SRAM for each compute unit(CU), and a global SRAM that all CUs could access. Neither GCC nor LLVM were great at optimizing OpenCL to make effective use of those different memories, so getting the highest performance sometimes required the use of AMD GCN assembler.

You need either the compiler or the programmer to do the parallelization, and in practice the communication costs between cores can be large enough for the parallelization to not be worth it for simple programs.
This ISA seems to be designed to:
1. make the automatic parallelization easy (with workload split between compiler and hardware)
2. make communication costs between tiles very small, so that even very simple workloads can be automatically parallelized.

hajile [user?id=hajile] 1 hour ago [item?id=50573830] | root [#50573199] | parent [#50573258] | prev [#50573320] | next [#50572778] [–] [javascript:void(0)]

Intel's Lion Cove core is 3.43mm2 without L2 cache and 4.53mm2 with private L2.
M5 S-core is 3.4mm2 including private L2.
But Intel's Lion cove is on N3B while M5 is on N3E which is about 10% less dense. do the math to scale up Lion Cove to N3E and it becomes around 5mm2.
That's 25-35% bigger for worse performance on the same node.
AMD could have gone with 3nm, but went with 5nm (N4P is 5nm++) instead. 3.72mm2 for the core and 4.77mm2 with private L2 cache.
N3E has the same size SRAM as N5/N4. Theoretical logic scaling is around 1.5x (216 MTr/mm² and 143.7 MTr/mm), but high-performance layouts don't get anywhere near that number due to the extra spacing needed for wires and transistors.
If we assume there's zero SRAM anywhere in the core (not true with large register files, L1, uop cache, etc), we get about 2.51mm2 for the core plus another 1.05mm2 (no cache shrink) for L2 giving 3.56mm2 (bigger than M5 S-core). With a more realistic shrink, we could be talking a total area of 3.9-4.2mm2.
TL;DR -- even in the absolute best-case scenario, both Zen5 and Lion Cove are bigger than Apple's M5 S-core.

Cache hierarchies also exist because memory latency and capacity tend to be inversely proportional to each other, and directly proportional to cost. The underlying process and physics of chip design works against you on this front.
