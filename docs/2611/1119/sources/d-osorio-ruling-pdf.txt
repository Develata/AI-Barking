      In this year, when we celebrate the two-hundred-fiftieth anniversary of our

Nation’s independence, it is as important as ever to reflect on the principles that

animated the creation of our Republic. Among those principles is the protection of

the people from unreasonable and arbitrary seizures of their persons by the

government. This principle is directly implicated in this case, where the Plaintiff

alleges that he was wrongly arrested and jailed for more than two weeks simply

because artificial intelligence misidentified him as the perpetrator of a crime. Of

course, discovery might show that there is more to the story than the Plaintiff alleges.

But assuming the truth of the Plaintiff’s allegations—which the Court must do at this

juncture—the Plaintiff has adequately stated claims upon which relief can be

granted. The Fourth Amendment to the United States Constitution does not allow a

detective to delegate to AI the task of assessing probable cause. In other words, the

Fourth Amendment does not permit an individual to be arrested based on nothing

more than an uncorroborated conclusion by AI that the individual in question has

II.    Analysis

       A motion for judgment on the pleadings under Federal Rule 12(c) invokes the

same standard as a motion to dismiss for failure to state a claim under Rule 12(b)(6).

Fritz v. Charter Twp. of Comstock, 592 F.3d 718, 722 (6th Cir. 2010) (citing Ziegler v.

IBP Hog Mkt., Inc., 249 F.3d 509, 511–12 (6th Cir. 2001)). To survive a Rule 12(c) or

Rule 12(b)(6) motion, “a complaint must present facts that, if accepted as true,

sufficiently ‘state a claim to relief that is plausible on its face.’” Coley v. Lucas County,

799 F.3d 530, 537 (6th Cir. 2015) (citing Bell Atl. Corp. v. Twombly, 550 U.S. 544, 570

(2007); Ashcroft v. Iqbal, 556 U.S. 662, 678 (2009)). “A claim is facially plausible when

a plaintiff ‘pleads factual content that allows the court to draw the reasonable

inference that the defendant is liable for the misconduct alleged.’” Id. (quoting Iqbal,

556 U.S. at 678); see also Iqbal, 556 U.S. at 678 (“Threadbare recitals of the elements

of a cause of action, supported by mere conclusory statements, do not suffice.”). The

“[f]actual allegations must be enough to raise a right to relief above the speculative




                                             5
Case: 2:26-cv-00004-SCM        Doc #: 26    Filed: 11/09/26    Page: 6 of 21 - Page ID#:
                                           159



level.” Twombly, 550 U.S. at 555.

      The Court views the complaint in the light most favorable to the plaintiff and

must accept all factual allegations as true. Riverview Health Inst. LLC v. Med. Mut.

of Ohio, 601 F.3d 505, 512 (6th Cir. 2010). All reasonable inferences are drawn in

favor of the plaintiff. Coley, 799 F.3d at 537. “The Court need not, however, ‘accept

the plaintiff’s legal conclusions or unwarranted factual inferences as true.’”

Sandmann v. WP Co., 401 F. Supp. 3d 781, 787 (E.D. Ky. 2019) (quoting Com. Money

Ctr., Inc. v. Ill. Union Ins. Co., 508 F.3d 327, 336 (6th Cir. 2007)).

      Detective Kilmer’s Motion argues not just that Osorio has failed to satisfy these

pleading standards, but also that he should prevail on qualified immunity. Qualified

immunity shields an officer like Detective Kilmer from liability unless he “violated a

federal statutory or constitutional right” and “the unlawfulness of [his] conduct was

‘clearly established at the time’” of the violation. District of Columbia v. Wesby, 583

U.S. 48, 62–63 (2018) (quoting Reichle v. Howards, 566 U.S. 658, 664 (2012)). At the

pleading stage, courts assess the officer’s conduct based on the facts alleged by the

plaintiff. See Warman v. Mount St. Joseph Univ., 144 F.4th 880, 896 (6th Cir. 2025).

So it is often inappropriate for a court to dismiss a complaint on qualified immunity

grounds before commencing discovery. See id. (quoting Wesley v. Campbell, 779 F.3d

421, 433 (6th Cir. 2015)); see also Myers v. City of Centerville, 41 F.4th 746, 758 (6th

Cir. 2022) (“[W]hether the alleged constitutional violation is clearly established—‘is

sometimes difficult’ on the pleadings, since that ‘inquiry may turn on case-specific

details that must be fleshed out in discovery.’” (quotation omitted)). Indeed, the Sixth

Case: 2:26-cv-00004-SCM       Doc #: 26    Filed: 11/09/26   Page: 7 of 21 - Page ID#:

Circuit “disfavors” granting qualified immunity based on the pleadings. Romero v.

City of Lansing, 159 F.4th 1002, 1008 (6th Cir. 2025). “That is because, in most cases,

‘[a]bsent any factual development beyond the allegations in a complaint, a court

cannot fairly tell whether a case is ‘obvious’ or ‘squarely govern[ed]’ by precedent,

which prevents us from determining whether the facts of [the] case parallel a prior

decision or not.’” Conner v. City of Covington, No. 2:25-cv-00042-SCM-CJS, 2026 WL

2494576, at *12 (E.D. Ky. July 22, 2026) (quoting Evans-Marshall v. Bd. of Educ. of

Tipp City Exempted Vill. Sch. Dist., 428 F.3d 223, 235 (6th Cir. 2005) (Sutton, J.,

concurring)) (alteration in original). Nevertheless, it is still Osorio’s burden to show

that Detective Kilmer is not entitled to qualified immunity. Burgess v. Fischer, 735

F.3d 462, 472 (6th Cir. 2013) (citing Chappell v. City of Cleveland, 585 F.3d 901, 907

(6th Cir. 2009)).

      Section 1983 provides a plaintiff with a private cause of action for the injurious

deprivation of his or her rights by a defendant who acted under the color of state law.

In this case, the Plaintiff asserts claims under § 1983 based on an alleged deprivation

of rights under the Fourth Amendment.

that he was arrested without probable cause. See Tlapanco, 969 F.3d at 652. “[I]t is

well-established in the Sixth Circuit that a facially valid warrant is a complete

defense to a federal constitutional claim for false arrest and false imprisonment.”

Trent v. Huff, 178 F. Supp. 3d 565, 569 (E.D. Ky. 2016) (citing Voyticky v. Vill. of

Timberlake, 412 F.3d 669, 677 (6th Cir. 2005)). And, in this case, Osorio was arrested

with a warrant. But that does not end the inquiry because there is an exception for

situations where the warrant was issued on the basis of materially false or misleading

v. Shaw, 335 F.3d 509, 517 (6th Cir. 2003)) (alteration in original); Voyticky, 412 F.3d

at 677 n.4. A false or misleading statement in a warrant affidavit is material “if the

    B. Osorio also states a viable claim under § 1983 for malicious
       prosecution in violation of the Fourth Amendment.

      Osorio’s § 1983 claim for continued detention without probable cause—

denominated as a claim of “malicious prosecution” 3—requires him to allege: (1)

Detective Kilmer “ma[d]e, influence[d], or participate[d] in the decision to prosecute”

Osorio in a criminal case; (2) there was no “probable cause for the prosecution”; (3)

Osorio’s liberty was deprived as a result of the prosecution, not counting his initial

arrest; and (4) the prosecution was resolved in Osorio’s favor. Sykes, 625 F.3d at 308–

09 (alterations in original) (citations omitted). Contrary to its name, a claim of

“malicious prosecution” under § 1983 does not require a showing of malice. See

Tlapanco, 969 F.3d at 659 (Thapar, J., concurring) (citing Howse v. Hodous, 953 F.3d

402, 408–09 (6th Cir. 2020)).

      Ordinarily, the first element cannot be satisfied simply on the basis that a

“prosecution was ‘initiated by the citations and arrests issued by the [police]

[D]efendant[ ] against the [P]laintiff[ ].’” Hartman v. Thompson, 931 F.3d 471, 485

(6th Cir. 2019). In other words, the mere fact that a police officer’s investigative work

and arrest may have led to the decision to prosecute—and therefore likewise led to

   C. Detective Kilmer is not entitled to qualified immunity at this juncture.

      Osorio states viable § 1983 claims based on Detective Kilmer’s alleged material

falsehoods or omissions, but his claims may not proceed unless he can show that

Detective Kilmer’s alleged conduct violated clearly established law. In other words,

it is not enough for Osorio to plead his claims sufficiently; he must also show that

Detective Kilmer is not entitled to qualified immunity.

      Detective Kilmer is entitled to qualified immunity unless his conduct violated

a federal statutory or constitutional right and the unlawfulness of his conduct was

clearly established at the time. Wesby, 583 U.S. at 62–63 (quoting Reichle, 566 U.S.

at 664). For a law to be clearly established, it must be “beyond debate” that the

officer’s conduct violated the plaintiff’s right, such that any reasonable officer would

know that his conduct was unlawful. Id. at 63 (quoting Ashcroft v. al-Kidd, 563 U.S.

731, 741 (2011)). This standard typically requires the plaintiff to point to then-

existing legal precedent that clearly applies to the officer’s conduct under the

circumstances with a “high ‘degree of specificity.’” Id. (quoting Mullenix v. Luna, 577

U.S. 7, 13 (2015)). Even on a motion for judgment on the pleadings, a court may not

define clearly established law at a high level of generality. Saalim v. Walmart, Inc.,

97 F.4th 995, 1009 (6th Cir. 2024) (citing Wesby, 583 U.S. at 63–64); see also Siefert

v. Hamilton County, 951 F.3d 753, 765 (6th Cir. 2020) (finding that the degree of

specificity required for the “right” is determined by the scope of the allegations in the

complaint). Ultimately, the rule must be specific enough that “the unlawfulness of

the officer’s conduct” immediately follows “from the conclusion that [the rule] was


                                           15
Case: 2:26-cv-00004-SCM         Doc #: 26    Filed: 11/09/26   Page: 16 of 21 - Page ID#:
                                            169



firmly established.” Wesby, 583 U.S. at 64 (alteration in original) (quoting Anderson

v. Creighton, 483 U.S. 635, 641 (1987)).

      Osorio claims that he has a clearly established right to be free from arrest

without probable cause and that no reasonable officer would rely solely on AI to

establish probable cause, nor would any reasonable officer have believed that Osorio

was the suspect depicted by the security footage. [Dkt. 13 at 11–12 (citing Rieves v.

Town of Smyrna, 959 F.3d 678, 696 (6th Cir. 2020))]. Osorio relies primarily on

Rieves, a Sixth Circuit case where business owners selling CBD products alleged that

two prosecutors knew or should have known there was “no evidence of the products’

illegality” under state law when they “pushed law enforcement” to arrest the business

owners.    Rieves, 959 F.3d at 696.         This alleged conduct violated the Fourth

Amendment, and the Sixth Circuit found “[i]t is clearly established that prosecutors

may not make a probable cause determination based on unreliable evidence” or

“information too vague and from too untested a source.” Id. (citations omitted). Thus,

the prosecutors were not entitled to qualified immunity based on the facts alleged in

the complaint.    Id. at 698.    Osorio argues that, like the prosecutors in Rieves,

Detective Kilmer allegedly knew or should have known that his evidence of Osorio’s

wrongdoing was based solely on an unreliable source—namely, AI facial recognition

technology. [Dkt. 13 at 12].

      Osorio also cites Tlapanco to say, even more specifically, that he has a “right

to be free from searches and seizures predicated on an officer’s reckless submission

of false statements in a warrant affidavit,” and that this right “is clearly established.”

Case: 2:26-cv-00004-SCM       Doc #: 26    Filed: 11/09/26   Page: 17 of 21 - Page ID#:

969 F.3d at 648.     In Tlapanco, a defendant officer misidentified the plaintiff’s

username on a messaging application and had him arrested for soliciting nude images

from a minor. Id. at 644. The charges were ultimately dismissed. Id. at 644. The

misidentification occurred because, rather than including the suspect’s entire

username in the affidavit, the defendant officer used a shortened version that

implicated the plaintiff. Id. The Sixth Circuit reversed the district court’s grant of

summary judgment for the defendant officer on qualified immunity grounds because

a reasonable jury could find a violation of the plaintiff’s right to be free from arrest

based on recklessly false statements in the affidavit. Id. at 648. Like the plaintiff in

Tlapanco, Osorio alleges that Detective Kilmer’s false statements or omissions caused

him to be arrested without an adequate basis for probable cause. [Dkt. 13 at 12–13].

      Detective Kilmer responds that these cases would not put a reasonable officer

in Detective Kilmer’s position on notice that there was no probable cause to arrest

Osorio. [Dkt. 16 at 4]. Specifically, Detective Kilmer argues that “[b]ecause Osorio

has identified no such case establishing that exclusively relying on [AI] facial-

recognition technology to establish probable cause violated Osorio’s Fourth

Amendment rights, his ‘argument comes up short.’” [Id. (quoting Mulcahey v. Twp.

of Chocolay, No. 25-1396, 2026 WL 64236, at *3 (6th Cir. Jan. 8, 2026))]. Detective

Kilmer’s argument misses the mark. While the allegations here and the facts in

Rieves and Tlapanco are not one-to-one, Osorio need not rely on case law that is

“directly on point” if the “lawfulness of the particular arrest” is “beyond debate.”

Wesby, 583 U.S. at 64. And the unlawfulness of Detective Kilmer’s conduct—at least

Case: 2:26-cv-00004-SCM      Doc #: 26     Filed: 11/09/26   Page: 18 of 21 - Page ID#:

as Osorio alleges—is beyond debate. It was Detective Kilmer’s obligation under the

law to find probable cause when he applied for an arrest warrant, and yet—according

to Osorio’s allegations—Detective Kilmer delegated that entire task to AI facial

recognition technology without the exercise of any independent judgment on his part

and then made material misstatements or omissions in the affidavit to conceal the

nature of the investigation (or the lack thereof). [Dkt. 14-1 at 5–6]. Based on the

principles in Rieves and Tlapanco, no reasonable officer would think that Detective

Kilmer’s reliance on AI facial recognition technology and the representations in his

affidavit would satisfy probable cause.

      Even without Osorio’s reliance on Rieves and Tlapanco, courts have recognized

an exception for the “rare ‘obvious case,’ where the unlawfulness of the officer’s

conduct is sufficiently clear even though existing precedent does not address similar

circumstances.” Wesby, 583 U.S. at 64 (quoting Brosseau v. Haugen, 543 U.S. 194,

199 (2004)); Saalim, 97 F.4th at 1009. For a § 1983 claim based on false arrest, the

obvious-case exception will apply if “any reasonable officer would have recognized the

absence of probable cause.” Blackwell v. Nocerini, 123 F.4th 479, 492 (6th Cir. 2024)

(quoting Wesby, 583 U.S. at 64). In Blackwell, the plaintiff alleged that he was

prosecuted for stalking in the absence of probable cause after he supposedly took

photos of the city manager, followed the city manager’s car for a few blocks, and

loitered in a parking lot with others after city council meetings. Id. at 482. The city

manager reported these activities to the police following public criticism from the

plaintiff and insisted that a criminal complaint be filed against the plaintiff. Id. at

482–83. The Sixth Circuit agreed that no reasonable officer would believe that any

of those activities would qualify as stalking or harassment to support a finding of

probable cause. Id. at 489–90. Thus, the obvious-case exception applied and the

plaintiff “did not need to identify analogous probable-cause caselaw to get past the

pleading stage.” Id. at 492 (“[W]e can save for another day whether he must do so at

the summary-judgment stage.”). Detective Kilmer’s conduct—as Osorio alleges—is

even more obviously unlawful than the alleged conduct of the city officials in

Blackwell.

      The following hypothetical illustrates how the rare obvious-case exception

applies here: Say that Detective Doe is called in to investigate a theft that occurred

at a bank earlier in the day. As Detective Doe walks on the street in the vicinity of

the bank, he uses a Magic 8 Ball to determine whether each pedestrian passerby is a

potential suspect. The first pedestrian walks by, so Detective Doe shakes the Magic

8 Ball and asks whether that individual is the suspect: “My sources say no.” Then,

Plaintiff Peter walks by, and Detective Doe once again shakes the Magic 8 Ball and

asks whether Plaintiff Peter is the suspect: “You may rely on it.” Using the output

from this “tool,” Detective Doe applies for a warrant to arrest Plaintiff Peter and

states in his affidavit that he used “investigative tools” to match the suspect to

Plaintiff Peter. Officers arrest Plaintiff Peter and he is charged with theft, but the

charges are dropped and he is released when his alibi checks out. In Plaintiff Peter’s

subsequent suit for false arrest, Detective Doe claims qualified immunity and argues

that Plaintiff Peter cannot point to any case law saying that the use of a Magic 8 Ball




                                          19
Case: 2:26-cv-00004-SCM      Doc #: 26     Filed: 11/09/26     Page: 20 of 21 - Page ID#:
                                          173



in this manner is a constitutional violation. But the unconstitutionality of Detective

Doe’s exclusive reliance on such an unreliable tool is so obvious that Plaintiff Peter

does not need to cite a factually similar case. Any reasonable officer would know that

additional investigative work is necessary to obtain sufficient probable cause for

arrest; a Magic 8 Ball alone will not cut it. Similarly, while AI facial recognition

technology has rapidly developed over recent years and is likely more reliable than a

Magic 8 Ball, placing unquestioned reliance on such technology is still not much

different in principle from placing unquestioned reliance on a Magic 8 Ball. No

reasonable officer would think that Detective Kilmer’s allegedly exclusive reliance on

AI is lawful—especially given how Osorio’s booking photo looks nothing like the

suspect from the security camera footage.

      Lurking in the background of all of this is the fact that this case is still at the

pleading stage. It is certainly possible that discovery might uncover facts that would

entitle Detective Kilmer to qualified immunity.              But without more factual

development, it is difficult to know whether Detective Kilmer’s conduct actually

violated clearly established law. That is why the Sixth Circuit has expressed a

general hesitancy to resolve questions of qualified immunity at the pleading stage.

See Evans-Marshall, 428 F.3d at 235 (Sutton, J., concurring). At this juncture, the

Court can only evaluate qualified immunity based on the allegations in the complaint.

And those allegations—when taken as true—show that Detective Kilmer is not

entitled to qualified immunity. Things might change in light of discovery, but as it

stands now, the Court will deny Detective Kilmer’s request for qualified immunity.
