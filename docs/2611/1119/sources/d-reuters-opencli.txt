WASHINGTON, Nov 10 (Reuters) - A federal judge in Kentucky has allowed a lawsuit to proceed against a police detective for allegedly relying exclusively on AI facial-recognition technology to identify a check fraud suspect, ​leading to the arrest and imprisonment of the wrong person.

US District Judge S. Chad ‌Meredith on Friday denied the detective's bid to dismiss the case for now, [ruling, opens new tab](https://fingfx.thomsonreuters.com/gfx/legaldocs/gkplennlgvb/Osorio%20Kilmer.pdf) that US constitutional protections forbid arrests “based on nothing more than an uncorroborated conclusion by AI that the individual in question has committed a crime.”

Meredith said the detective, ​employed by the Florence Police Department in eastern Kentucky, did not have qualified immunity that ​could automatically [shield](https://www.reuters.com/investigates/special-report/usa-police-immunity-methodology/) him from liability.

The ruling adds to a growing number of cases ⁠over law enforcement's [use of AI](https://www.reuters.com/legal/government/arizona-court-says-judge-wrongly-allowed-ai-generated-victim-video-2026-09-30/), including facial-recognition technology, and how the US Constitution's Fourth Amendment safeguards against unreasonable ​searches and seizures apply to AI-generated evidence.

The plaintiff in the Kentucky case, Alain Osorio, is asserting claims ​including false arrest and malicious prosecution. Prosecutors dismissed the charges against him last year after he showed he was not at the bank at the time an alleged fraudulent check was presented.

Lawyers for Osorio had no immediate comment. Defense attorneys ​for the detective did not immediately respond to a request for comment.

The lawsuit stems from Osorio's 2024 ​arrest for alleged check fraud. The complaint says Florence Police Department policy treats facial-recognition technology only as a source ‌of investigative ⁠leads and does not permit it to establish a suspect's identity on its own.

The lawsuit alleges detective Ryan Kilmer “failed to completely and adequately investigate or verify the identity of Alain Vizcay Osorio.”

Kilmer has denied any wrongdoing and countered that Osorio failed to show that the alleged misconduct violated clearly established law.

Meredith said there ​was “good reason” to believe ​a warrant for Osorio’s ⁠arrest would not have been issued if the detective “painted a full picture of how he arrived at the conclusion that Osorio was the individual who attempted ​to cash the fraudulent check.”

The case is Alain Vizcay Osorio v. Ryan ​Kilmer, US District ⁠Court, Eastern District of Kentucky, No. 2:26-cv-00004-SCM.
