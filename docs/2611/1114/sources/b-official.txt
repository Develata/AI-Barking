1What we are releasing

Proactive agents are the next frontier. How do we measure them?

Anyone who has tried to integrate AI into their life knows the models are too chatty. They catch details you’d miss, but they also overwhelm you with non-important fluff. Muse, Grok, Dots and others are trying to fix this. Meanwhile, agents at work are moving to a different job: lurk in the workspace, watch, and help when it matters. That job has three parts: gathering evidence from systems the conversation never mentions, deciding if help is needed, and who and how should receive it.

Teams choosing a model for a proactive agent, or building one in-house, have had little to go on. Their own company data is too sensitive to benchmark on in public. So we built a company. PageMantle is synthetic, with the tools a real one runs on: a CRM, product analytics, billing, a warehouse, an issue tracker, docs, and Slack. We are open-sourcing all of it.

Existing work covers pieces of this. ProACT [https://arxiv.org/html/2607.03730v1] and MUIR [https://arxiv.org/html/2603.01059v3] treat the decision to participate and the quality of the reply as separate questions. Toolathlon [https://github.com/hkust-nlp/Toolathlon] is careful about tool behaviour. TheAgentCompany [https://papers.nips.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/file/0d744742f6fac4d1134c019b7cef3c8a-Paper-Datasets_and_Benchmarks_Track.pdf] shows that a self-contained company is a workable test environment. None of them combine the speak-or-stay-silent decision with investigation across an extensive, heterogenous company data footprint, and none score where the help belongs: channel, thread, or direct message.

This version compares models, not harnesses. Every model gets the same instructions, the same tools, the same company snapshot, and the same 180-second budget. Deepnote is building a workspace where agents and people work on data together, which is why the question matters to us.

The kind of moment we mean

Sales, in #customers: “I’ll tell them we’re launching on Friday.”

Engineering, in #releases: “The release is moving to next Friday.”

A useful one catches the mismatch without being tagged, finds the release update, and brings it into the sales thread.

1
The most expensive models are not the best ones

The three priciest models finish third, fourth, and fifth. The runner-up, GPT-6.1 Sol, costs $0.11 per task. GPT-6 Luna ($0.007) and Claude Fable 5.1 ($3.02) are 430× apart in price and 1.1 points apart on a 10-point score. A month of watching one company’s Slack comes to $281 on Sol and $4,684 on Fable 5.2.

2
Models are good at helping and bad at not helping

When help was needed, a message went out 9 times out of 10. When silence was right, a message was held back only 6 times out of 10. For the same model, speaking out of turn cost about 1.8× as much as staying quiet.

3
OpenAI leads on quality & price, Anthropic on speed

GPT-6 Astra (8.97) and GPT-6.1 Sol (8.65) lead and sit on all three frontiers: cost, time, and tool calls. Claude Sonnet 5.5 and Opus 5.5 join them on time and tool calls, with faster answers and lower scores.

4
Models work in two styles: snipers or deep thinkers

Opus 5.5 answers in 29 seconds with 10 tool calls. Fable 5.2 makes twice the calls in twice the time for a score 4% higher. Sol 6.1 takes 88% longer for a score 10% higher, at under a tenth of Opus’s price.

5
The heaviest tool callers scored lowest

Gemini 4 Argon averaged 35 tool calls and scored 5.60. Grok 4.7 averaged 36 and scored 4.51. Both models failed to reach an answer in ~half the tasks that required assistance.

6
Fable 5.2 improves on 5.1, but is not #1

Claude Fable 5.2 falls behind Astra and Sol. It's best at decision tasks across models, but at a high cost of $1.87 per task, and more tool calls and times taken.

3Meet PageMantle

PageMantle: a real fake company

PageMantle is a high-fidelity synthetic company with the digital footprint of a real software business: users sign up, build and publish websites, customers pay and renew, and teams ship features and resolve incidents. Six years of connected records trace those people, accounts, and events across 11 services.

Company history

Two founders started the company with a seed check in 2020. By 2026, it grew to 71 employees and 100 enterprise customers. Real events from its market and the wider world impact company history and development, alongside its own launches, outages, and departures.

When a competitor launches a feature, PageMantle employees react to it by announcing efforts to counter this in Slack, and they track and deliver it via Linear.

4How we built it

Building a fake company that holds together

Agents need company data to learn and be tested on, and real company data is too sensitive to share. Generating it is the alternative, and it is hard: ask a model for a million documents and the facts drift apart from one source to the next. So we fixed the facts first and let models write around them.

1
Code fixes the facts

Employee identities and roles, joining dates, customers, contract values, dates, and the links between records. Anything that has to stay consistent is owned by code.

2
Models write inside them

Weekly plans, daily events, conversations, documents, and issue descriptions. Language models add variety, working within the facts the code fixed first.

3
Events fan out into records

One customer problem becomes a Linear issue, a Slack thread, and a HubSpot note, each written for its own purpose. Where a decision depends on a document, the document exists.

4
Simulated APIs serve it

Each service runs behind a simulated API designed to mimic the shape and limits of the real one. Warehouse and analytics data sit in Parquet behind DuckDB. The other services are SQLite-backed. The code is on GitHub [https://github.com/deepnote/agent-initiative-benchmark].

Why generate at all. We looked for an existing company dataset and found none that covered daily operations end to end. Public technical discussions have real problems but no company around them. Activity logs show who edited a document without the document. A proactive agent needs both: the conversation and the records behind it. And to the best of our knowledge, no benchmark gives its tasks this much company detail to work through.

Tasks are overlays. Every task is a small set of changes on one shared, read-only baseline. A new note that conveniently states the whole answer would change what the test rewards, so added records have to look like ordinary company work. The agent cannot tell an overlay from the baseline.

From a Slack conversation to a scored contribution

Each of the 12 models faces the same 50 situations at high reasoning effort, with identical instructions, tools, and company state. Keeping these conditions fixed focuses the comparison on the model.

How a run works

One run, start to finish

1
The agent sits in the company Slack

It gets the company snapshot at a fixed moment, the newest Slack messages, and one standing instruction: assess what is going on and help if it is useful.

2
A discussion starts

Agent reads the conversation and decides whether anything there calls for it.

3
It investigates

Search and read tools across all 11 services, including writing SQL against BigQuery and Snowflake. The budget is 180 seconds of model response time. Tool execution does not count.

4
It speaks, or it doesn’t

A channel message, a thread reply, a direct message that reaches the right people, or nothing.

5
The clock can end the run

A run that reaches the limit stops immediately. If help was needed and no message went out, the run is scored as a miss.

6
Two LLM evaluators score it

Two models blind to the fact which model is being evaluated grade the run 0 to 10 against a reference solution, with the company data and the full action log. Neither knows which model it is grading or what the other said.

8 task types for everyday work

Knowledge

Find the fact, explain the process, run the numbers.

Judgment

Check a claim, pick an option, locate a cause.

(In)action

Line up work between teams, or protect the company: catch an access, sharing, or policy mistake before it does damage. Or stay out of it.

Find

Find a fact, a document, the responsible person, or the current status.

“Who owns the billing service now?”

Explain

Explain a rule, a process, or how a product works.

“What's the process to get a vendor approved?”

Analyze

Compare data and describe a change, a trend, or a relationship.

“Churn by plan, this quarter vs. last.”

Verify

Check a claim, a document, or a number against its sources.

“Does this PR implement what the spec says?”

Decide

Compare options, set priorities, or recommend the next step.

“Which 10 inbound leads should sales call first today?”

Diagnose

Identify the cause of a problem and propose a way to fix it.

“SSO login fails for one customer. Where?”

Coordinate

Line up the work of colleagues or teams and clarify who owns what.

“Two teams are planning changes that won't work together.”

Guard

Review access and sharing, or check a proposed action against company policy.

“A link to a confidential contract appeared in a shared channel.”

Blind evaluation by two models

After each run, the evaluators check the decision to intervene as well as the message itself. These four dimensions distinguish useful help from a polished interruption.

1Intervention or silenceDid the agent help where help was needed, and hold back where it was not?

2Content and evidenceWere the conclusions accurate, complete, and backed by the data it read?

3Clarity and toneWas the message clear, specific, and suitable in tone and level of detail?

4Audience and locationDid it reach the right people in the right channel, thread, or DM?

Regardless of price, models work in two styles. Deep thinkers keep searching every tool for evidence before they answer. Snipers make as few calls as they can and answer fast. Opus 5.5 is the sniper: 10 tool calls and 29 seconds per task.

Compared with Opus 5.5, the deep thinkers do about twice the work for a modest gain:

Fable 5.2: 2× the tool calls, 2× the time, and 47% more cost, for a score 4% higher.

Sol 6.1: 80% more tool calls and 88% more time, for a score 10% higher, at less than a tenth of Opus’s price.

Searching more is not the same as finding more. Gemini 4 Argon made 35 tool calls on average and scored 5.60. Grok 4.7 made 36 and scored 4.51. Each sent a message in a little over half of the tasks that needed one.

The dataset is large, so an agent can never be sure it has read enough. It still has to commit before the limit. Astra and Sol handled that trade-off best: neither reached the limit more than once in 50 runs.

Numbers are the common gap. Analyze tasks ask the agent to pull figures from PostHog, BigQuery, or Snowflake and read them against a claim someone made in Slack. That is the weakest type for 8 of 12 models. Checking a claim against its sources (Verify) is the opposite: nearly every model does it well.

Within OpenAI, the two larger models split. Astra is strongest where work crosses teams: lining up owners (Coordinate) and checking access and policy (Guard). Sol is strongest at explaining a rule or a product (Explain) and at finding things, but lining up colleagues is its weak spot, about 3 points below Astra. Both handle numbers well, unlike most of the field.

Within Anthropic, Fable 5.2 and Opus 5.5 split along similar lines. Fable 5.2 is stronger at judgment: comparing options and recommending a next step (Decide), and finding a root cause (Diagnose). Opus 5.5 is stronger at people and process: coordinating teams and explaining how things work. Both trail the OpenAI leaders on Analyze.

The parallel holds in both labs: the model that coordinates best is not the one that explains or decides best. Each type has 5 to 8 tasks, so read these as tendencies to test on your own work, not fixed rankings.

Three ways to lose points

Most lost points trace back to three patterns: the timing out, unwarranted intervention, and getting lost in the context.

1
Running out the clock

When help was needed, most models sent a message. Gemini 4 Argon and Grok 4.7 were the main exceptions: they often kept investigating until time ran out.

Most missed messages were timeouts, not deliberate choices to stay silent. The models were still searching when they needed to act.

2
Stepping into a settled matter

The opposite failure is harder to spot. GPT-6 Luna, DeepSeek V4.1 Flash, and Mistral Large 4 sent a message in 6 of the 10 tasks that called for silence. Both Claude Fable models did so in 4.

Those messages were accurate and well placed. The matter had already been handled, and the records said so. A check on message quality alone will not catch this.

3
A wrong conclusion from the right evidence

Some runs found the right record and still drew the wrong conclusion. In a data-access task, GPT-6 Luna referred an employee to the former access administrator after reading the record that confirmed the handover to a new one.

8Implications for proactive agent builders

Building proactive agents? Key takeaways:

1
Route by task complexity

Cost is a routing problem. An always-on agent investigating about 2,500 threads a month costs $281 on Sol 6.1, $1,732 on Astra 6, and $4,684 on Fable 5.2. Send routine threads to an inexpensive, capable workhorse and escalate to a stronger model when the task is complex: numbers to reconcile, a decision to weigh, or a matter that may already be closed. We did not test a router. This is a design recommendation drawn from the score and cost spread.

2
Silence is a moat

Almost every model can help. Knowing when to stay quiet is what separates them. The best models still spoke in 2 of 10 situations that called for silence, and their needless messages were well written, so a quality filter will not catch them. Make “is this already handled?” an explicit step, and track false alarms as their own number.

3
Choose models by the organization's task mix

Rankings move by task type, so onboarding should start by finding out which tasks the organization actually hands the agent. A finance company and a small local shop ask for very different things. Then let task strengths guide the choice: Astra leads five of eight types, including Analyze and Guard. Sol leads Explain and Find. Fable 5.2 leads Decide. Analyze is the weakest type for 8 of 12 models, so test a numbers-heavy mix there first.

4
Speed for live threads, depth for background work

When someone is waiting in a live conversation, response time is part of the answer: Opus 5.5 replied in about 29 seconds on average and scored 7.84. For digests, monitors, and other background work nobody is waiting, so spend the time on investigation: Sol 6.1 took about 54 seconds, scored 8.65, and cost about a tenth as much.

5
Cap the search and force a decision

More tool calls did not buy score. The two heaviest callers reached the time limit on 27 and 18 of their 50 runs. Give the harness a budget and a point at which it must commit: a message, or silence.

6
Evaluate the harness, not only the model

These results hold the instructions, tools, and budget fixed. A different prompt, tool policy, or retrieval step will move every number here. Deepnote is now building a harness benchmark.

If your company is building a proactive agent, reach out to the research team.

Limitations

A 50-task panel. Deepnote will release a larger sample of tasks in the next version.

Fixed snapshot. The agent does not choose the moment the test begins, and teammates do not reply. We measure the contribution at that point, not its effect on a team over time.

One effort level. Every model ran at high. Lower or higher effort will move both score and cost. We have not run those sweeps yet.

One month of Slack, six years of records. Tasks start mainly from one month of PageMantle's Slack conversations. The evidence behind them comes from the connected services, which hold six years of interconnected company history. The agent replies only through Slack. Email, documents as a surface, and actions beyond messaging are not in this version.
