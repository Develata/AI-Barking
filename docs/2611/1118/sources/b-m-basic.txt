Gemini 4 Neon: Google Replaces Argon and 3.1 Pro with a Faster, More Affordable Model

On November 18, 2026, Google unveiled Gemini 4 Neon, the fourth model in the Gemini 4 family, within just over four weeks. Neon is expected to perform about as well as the top-of-the-line Gemini 4 Carbon in coding, analysis, and agent tasks, while responding significantly faster and costing less per task. The model is now available in the Gemini app for Google AI Pro and Ultra subscribers, where it replaces Gemini 4 Argon and the older Gemini 3.1 Pro.

Key Takeaways

Gemini 4 Neon falls between the fast Hydrogen and the heavy Carbon.

API pricing: $3 per million input tokens, $15 per million output tokens; cached inputs cost $0.15.

Context window of 1 million tokens, four thinking levels ranging from Low to Extra High.

In the Gemini app (AI Pro and Ultra), Neon replaces Argon and Gemini 3.1 Pro.

According to Google, it ranks 1st in the Artificial Analysis Coding Agent Index and 3rd in the Intelligence Index.

What is Gemini 4 Neon?

Google describes Neon as a model for complex programming, analytical thinking, and productive business workflows. The division of roles in the Gemini 4 series is thus quite clear: Carbon handles the toughest, most time-consuming projects; Gemini 4 Hydrogen [https://basic-tutorials.com/news/gemini-4-hydrogen-googles-fast-ai-model-is-coming-to-everyone-including-search/] takes care of quick, everyday tasks; and Neon is designed to cover the middle ground where most people actually work.

The model processes spreadsheets, PDFs, charts, audio, and video within a shared context of up to one million tokens. Via the API, the output can also be up to one million tokens long. Neon supports computer use, code execution, and grounding via Google Search. However, it does not generate images or audio.

Of interest to developers: In Google’s coding environment Antigravity, Neon can act as an orchestrator and control multiple Hydrogen instances in parallel as subagents. Google also emphasizes that, during long sessions, Neon reads fewer redundant files, reports interim results, and can handle context compression.

If you’ve lost track of things in recent weeks, you’re not alone. Argon came first—initially only for cyber defenders—followed by Carbon (November 10), Hydrogen (November 12), and now Neon. Argon is thus already disappearing from the app again.

The new model picker in the Gemini app now has four entries: Auto (previously Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite), Hydrogen, Neon, and Carbon. Gemini 3.1 Pro is being discontinued.

Pricing: Starting in December, Gemini 4 Neon will be cheaper than Carbon

At first glance, Neon—at $3 and $15 per million tokens, respectively—seems more expensive than Carbon. This is due to the introductory discount for Carbon and Argon, which expires at the end of November. After that, both will cost $4 and $20, respectively, and Neon will be about 25 percent cheaper. Compared to Hydrogen, you’ll pay twice as much.

More interesting than the list price is the cost per completed task, because models consume different amounts of tokens while processing. Here are the figures Google provides:

At the standard “High” thinking level, Neon costs 32 percent less per task than Carbon.

According to Google, in the Vals Index and Agent Arena, Neon has 50 to 80 percent lower costs per task than the strongest competing models.

At the highest level, “Extra High,” Neon is said to be at least 36 percent cheaper than competitors at their highest level.

Google does not specify exactly which competing models are being referred to in the blog post. You should therefore treat these figures as manufacturer claims.

In addition, it ranks first in legal and tax agent benchmarks (Legal Research Bench, Tax Agent Bench). According to Google, Neon ranks second only to larger, slower models in the Intelligence Index. Most of these figures have not yet been independently verified, and Google naturally selects benchmarks that look good for such launches.

Security and a Dedicated Cyber Version

Neon is based on the same security stack as Carbon. Google reports a rate of misdirected actions of 0.09 percent in its agent security suite and a success rate of 0.0 percent for prompt injection attacks in the Gray Swan benchmark—though only at the highest reasoning level with active real-time protection filters. In an internal coding test that checks whether a model cheats on tasks, Neon recorded the lowest rate of all Gemini 4 models at 2.45 percent.

At the same time, “Gemini 4 Neon for cyber” is being launched. This variant will be rolled out to selected security teams in the Fairwind program in the coming days. Google is thus following the same approach as OpenAI with GPT-6 Cyber [https://basic-tutorials.com/news/gpt-6-cyber-openais-new-security-model-finds-81-firefox-vulnerabilities/]: the most capable security models are made available to defenders first.

Availability: How to Use Gemini 4 Neon

According to Google, Neon is being rolled out immediately in the following locations:

Gemini app: for Google AI Pro and AI Ultra subscribers. Web-based searches, integration with Gmail and Docs, and Gemini Tasks [https://basic-tutorials.com/news/gemini-tasks-for-free-googles-ai-agent-runs-errands-and-makes-calls-but-germany-is-left-out/] also run on Neon within the app.

Gemini API and Google AI Studio: available with a paid plan; model ID: gemini-4-neon.

Google Antigravity: for paying AI Pro and Ultra customers.

Gemini for Business and Google Cloud: via API and model selection, as well as in the Gemini Enterprise Agent Platform.

Android Studio

Google does not mention any country restrictions in the blog post. So anyone in Germany with an AI Pro or Ultra subscription should find Neon in the model picker in the coming days. Nothing changes for users of the free Gemini version; they’ll continue to use Auto or Hydrogen.

Conclusion: The real workhorse of the Gemini 4 series

For most Pro subscribers, Gemini 4 Neon is likely to become the model they use most frequently in their day-to-day work. Carbon is simply overkill for many tasks, while Hydrogen reaches its limits during longer coding sessions. Neon fills exactly this gap, and starting in December, it will even be cheaper than Carbon via the API.

Still, a bit of skepticism is warranted. Four models in four weeks, one of which was replaced after just a few weeks: that seems more frantic than well-planned. And almost all of the benchmarks come from Google itself. It will be interesting to see how Neon fares in independent tests against Claude Fable 5.2 [https://basic-tutorials.com/news/claude-fable-5-2-is-here-anthropic-lowers-the-price-and-improves-coding-capabilities/] and OpenAI’s current models. If the cost advantages hold up there, Google has made a pretty good offer.
