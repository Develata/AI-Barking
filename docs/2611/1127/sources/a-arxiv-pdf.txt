                                            in the real world, and unifying heterogeneous conditioning inputs within a shared framework for
                                            view-consistent generation. Concretely, our architecture integrates View-Temporal Attention (VTA)
                                            to capture cross-view spatial dependencies and temporal dynamics, together with Multi-view Rotary
                                            Position Encoding (RoPE) to encode positional relationships across views and time. We further

1     Introduction
World models (Zhou et al., 2026c; Team et al., 2026c; Wu et al., 2025a; Team et al., 2026b; Wan et al.,
2025; Agarwal et al., 2026; Huang et al., 2026a; Wu et al., 2026; Zhu et al., 2026a; Xu et al., 2026a; Yang
et al., 2026b; Hu et al., 2026b,a,c,a) aim to generate the evolution of a scene at future time steps based
on environmental observations, actions, or task instructions. For Physical AI agents such as autonomous
vehicles and embodied robots, this capability is fundamental to anticipatory decision-making and closed-loop
control. Before changing lanes, avoiding obstacles, or performing emergency braking, a vehicle must anticipate
the motion of surrounding traffic participants and its potential consequences. Similarly, before grasping,
pushing, or placing an object, a robot must infer how its actions will change the state of the object and
the spatial configuration of the scene. Therefore, an effective world model should do more than generate
visually plausible future frames. It should also capture the relationships among actions, object states, and
environmental changes, and predict the physical consequences induced by different control conditions.
    Recent video world models have made substantial progress in predicting and generating future dynamics,
with growing applications in autonomous driving (Wang et al., 2024a,b; Gao et al., 2024b; Ren et al., 2025;
Zhang et al., 2026; Basant et al., 2026; Zheng et al., 2026; Gao et al., 2024a; Guan et al., 2026; Lu et al., 2026;
Luo et al., 2026; Zhu et al., 2026b; Ji et al., 2025; Jin et al., 2025; Guo et al., 2025; Zeng et al., 2025; Zhou
et al., 2026b) and embodied intelligence (Wu et al., 2023b; Xu et al., 2026e; Gao et al., 2026; Bi et al., 2026; Ye
et al., 2026; Xu et al., 2026c,d). Nevertheless, as summarized in Tab. 1, existing models provide limited unified
support for various domains, view configurations, control inputs, and downstream applications. Building
a general-purpose world model for Physical AI therefore requires addressing two fundamental challenges.
First, physical interactions in the real-world follow a highly long-tailed distribution (Wang et al., 2025b;
Collaboration et al., 2023). Naturally collected videos are dominated by routine and frequently occurring
events, providing limited coverage of rare but safety-critical interactions such as collisions, emergency evasive
maneuvers, and object failures. Scaling conventional video data alone is therefore insufficient for learning
the diverse physical consequences. Second, Physical AI systems operate under heterogeneous observation
and conditioning configurations, ranging from single-camera inputs to multi-camera systems with different
layouts and scene contexts (Russell et al., 2025; Team et al., 2024; Tan et al., 2026). Models designed for
fixed view configurations and conditioning formats generalize poorly across scenarios. Meanwhile, scaling to
more views and longer temporal contexts substantially increases computational and memory requirements,
limiting long-horizon closed-loop generation. Our goals are:

    Goal 1. Model and generate the causal evolution in the real world.
    Goal 2. Unify heterogeneous conditioning across variable-view configurations.
    Goal 3. Enable diverse downstream Physical AI applications.

    We therefore present Xiaomi Auto World Model 2.0, a unified streaming causal world model for Physical
AI. Fig. 1 highlights the model’s unified capabilities across general video generation, autonomous driving,
and embodied robotics. With variable-view configurations and heterogeneous conditioning inputs, our model
enables controllable generation for simulation-based training and evaluation, as well as WAM applications (Li
et al., 2026a; Xia et al., 2026).
    To improve physical-dynamics modeling, we construct a physical-interaction data pipeline that integrates
real-world driving and robotics data with physically causal data. The physically causal subset draws on
simulator engines, video generators, and public media, covering physically grounded interactions and long-tailed
events. From the Pre-Train stage onward, these data are jointly trained with large-scale general-purpose videos
and diverse visual representations, enabling the model to acquire general visual and dynamic priors, strengthen
its geometric understanding, and form an initial representation of physical evolution. During Mid-Train,
quality-aware training on high-quality data spanning diverse view configurations further strengthens the
modeling of how actions and object states shape subsequent scene evolution, improving the representation
and generation of real-world physical interactions and long-tailed dynamics while preserving generalization
across scenes and observation settings.
    We introduce a unified generation framework for heterogeneous conditioning inputs and variable-view future-
scene generation in a shared latent space. View-Temporal Attention (VTA) captures cross-view dependencies

and temporal dynamics across varying view configurations, while Multi-view Rotary Position Encoding (RoPE)
encodes positional relationships across views and time. Heterogeneous conditioning inputs are mapped into
compatible latent representations and fused through VAE-based feature concatenation. Through this interface,
we incorporate WorldRec 2.0 as explicit 3D scene memory grounded in real observations. It reconstructs
static and dynamic 3D Gaussians from multi-camera, multi-temporal observations in a single forward pass,
using scene-structured registers for efficient information exchange among point-aligned queries. Renderings at
target camera poses and timestamps provide geometry-aware priors that anchor geometry, appearance, and
cross-view correspondence in observed regions, while the generator completes appearance and motion details
beyond the observations. Together, these components support single-view, autonomous-driving multi-view,
and embodied multi-view generation within a shared architecture, with flexible conditioning and consistent
scene representations across views and time.
    Building on these designs, we develop a unified causal world model that supports variable-view generation
and flexible conditioning. To improve the efficiency of long-horizon multi-view rollouts, we incorporate Gated
Linear Attention (GLA) into the temporal attention layers of VTA, reducing the computational complexity
and the memory overhead associated with state caching during inference. Distillation-based post-training
further improves streaming efficiency and supports stable generation over extended sequences. This reduces
the computational and memory costs of multi-view rollouts, allowing the model to continuously generate
future scenes and extend short-term prediction to streaming world simulation over longer time horizons. We
evaluate the Post-trained model across three downstream settings. First, for simulation-based evaluation,
we assess its general generation and world-modeling capabilities through general video generation on PAI-
Bench-G (Zhou et al., 2026a), synchronized multi-view generation under long-tailed and physically causal
scenarios, scene perception via depth prediction and referring video segmentation, and camera-pose control
on WorldScore (Duan et al., 2025), TartanAir (Wang et al., 2020), and RealCam-Vid (Zheng et al., 2025).
We further use the model as a controllable world simulator to evaluate multi-view driving generation on
nuScenes (Caesar et al., 2019), embodied interaction generation on TriWorldBench (Liu et al., 2026a), and
long-horizon driving simulation on internal data. Second, for simulation-based training, the model generates
future states and interaction feedback in closed-loop rollouts to support policy learning. We evaluate the
resulting policy on NAVSIM (Dauner et al., 2024), using 1000 representative test scenes. Finally, for WAM
applications, we transfer the learned visual-dynamics and physical-interaction representations to downstream
decision-making and control, evaluating autonomous-driving planning on NAVSIM (Dauner et al., 2024),
embodied manipulation on LIBERO Plus (Fei et al., 2025). We additionally evaluate WorldRec 2.0 on the
Waymo Open Dataset (Xu et al., 2026b), use nuScenes (Caesar et al., 2019) and Argoverse 2 (Wilson et al.,
2023) for cross-dataset evaluation, while reporting qualitative results on the internal MiData dataset. Together,
these experiments evaluate the model’s generation quality, physical and geometric consistency, controllability,
simulation capability, and downstream utility across diverse Physical AI scenarios.

2      Data
Data are essential for model training. To train a causal world model that respects physical causal laws, we
built multi-view datasets that emphasize physical causality. We partition data sources into three categories: (i)
general data, comprising camera, text, and video data; (ii) physical-world data, comprising autonomous-driving
and robotics data; and (iii) physical-casual data. General data provide broad priors on visual appearance,
geometric structure, language, and motion patterns. Physical-world data ground these priors in embodied
observations and actions. Physical-causal data further include safety-critical corner cases and counterfactual
trajectories that are underrepresented in naturally collected data. All data modalities are subjected to a
unified cleaning and re-annotation pipeline. For high-quality physical world data, we also estimate associated
multi-modal conditional annotations, including geometric representations, segmentation masks, and scene
layout attributes. The following sections introduce data sources and their curation pipelines, followed by data
filtering, re-captioning, and condition annotation creation, as summarized in Fig. 2.

2.1      General Data
Before domain-specific specialization, we use large-scale general data to learn priors about visual appearance,
language, motion dynamics, and scene structure. These data are organized into camera, text, and video
sources and are independent of the downstream driving and robotics domains.
     Leveraging the broad availability of video data, we curate raw videos and superimpose high-fidelity
multimodal annotations. To jointly optimize dataset coverage and experimental controllability, we integrate
(i) internet-scale web videos to capture long-tail variation in scenes, objects, and motion patterns, and (ii)
structured open-source datasets that provide reliable metadata and support evaluation under more stringent,
domain-specific conditions.

Web-crawled Data. We follow established collection protocols used in recent video foundation models.
Specifically, we adopt Pandas 70M (Chen et al., 2024b) as the primary source and apply strict filtering criteria


                                                                    4
to ensure minimum visual quality and temporal consistency. In addition to their scale, these videos often
include textual descriptions that provide weak semantic supervision, thereby exposing the model to a broad
distribution of everyday events and dynamics.

Open-source Data. We target datasets that provide explicit supervisory signals that are typically absent in
unconstrained web content, including camera poses, geometry-related conditions, and driving or embodiment
metadata. These data sources focus on operationally grounded domains, such as egocentric, autonomous
driving, and robotic manipulation, and thus complement the breadth of web video with well-defined constraints.
Concretely, we aggregate video data from SpatialVID-HQ (Wang et al., 2026a), Sekai (Li et al., 2026b),
RealCam-Vid (Zheng et al., 2025), and TartanAir (Wang et al., 2020).

2.2     Physical World Data
Physical-world data connect visual observations to embodied states, actions, and consequences. They comprise
autonomous-driving data and robotics data.

2.2.1   Driving Data
The autonomous-driving data provide multi-view camera streams and vehicle-centric signals, including driving
trajectories, maps, and sensor metadata. Specifically, we use PhysicalAI-Autonomous-Vehicles (Wang et al.,
2025b), nuPlan (Caesar et al., 2021), nuScene (Caesar et al., 2019), and our self-collected MiData. PhysicalAI-
Autonomous-Vehicles captures a broad range of traffic densities, weather conditions, obstacles, and interactions
with vulnerable road users. Its sensor suite includes synchronized multi-camera and LiDAR recordings for
all clips, with radar data available for approximately half of the clips. MiData is primarily collected by our
data-acquisition fleet and spans heterogeneous operational design domains. Spatially, it includes urban and
rural roads, highways, underground garages, and parking lots. Temporally, it covers different times of day and
multiple seasons. Each MiData sequence contains 11 synchronized camera streams recorded at 30 Hz and one
LiDAR stream, together with ego-vehicle state measurements. Sequences are partitioned into approximately
30 s clips, and a cloud-based automatic labeling system is applied to produce core scene annotations, including
object detection, lane-boundary detection, traffic-sign recognition and traffic-light recognition.

2.2.2   Robotics Data
Robotics data cover egocentric observations and robot-manipulation episodes, together with actions, proprio-
ception, object states, and language instructions when available. We include datasets used for vision-language-
action training, such as AgiBot World Beta (Bu et al., 2025), DROID (Khazatsky et al., 2024), EgoDex (Hoque
et al., 2026), Galaxea Open-World Dataset (Jiang et al., 2026a), InternData-A1 (Tian et al., 2026), Open
X-Embodiment (Collaboration et al., 2023), RoboCOIN (Wu et al., 2025b), RoboMIND (Wu et al., 2024),
and RoboTwin 2.0 (Chen et al., 2025a). Collectively, these datasets span real-world and simulated collections,
single-arm and bimanual embodiments, tabletop and open-world scenes, and both human egocentric and
robot-centric views. They provide large-scale heterogeneous supervision, ranging from tens of thousands to
millions of trajectories, thousands of hours of video, and hundreds of tasks, skills, and scenes, with language
annotations where available. We align their observations and controls with the same temporal and geometric
annotation interface. These data extend the physical-world category from road interactions to contact-rich
manipulation and support transfer to embodied tasks.

2.3     Physical Causal Data
Although general data and physical world data exhibit substantial diversity, they frequently underrepresent
emergency events that occur in the physical world, including vehicular collisions and other rare or highly
specialized incidents. To mitigate this limitation, we curate (i) simulator engine data and (ii) video generator
data to construct these corner-cases and counterfactual simulation.

Simulator Engine Data. To address the limited prevalence of extreme physical interactions in real-world
driving corpora, such as collisions, roadway departures, and aggressive maneuvers, we construct a large-scale,
multi-view synthetic dataset using the simulator engine, including: Blender (Blender Online Community,


                                                       5
2025), UE5 (Epic Games, 2022), and CARLA (Dosovitskiy et al., 2017), following (Zhou et al., 2026b).
Blender and UE5 is used to generate higher-quality multi-view video and geometric conditions. CARLA is a
high-fidelity autonomous-driving simulation platform that supports controlled generation of road networks,
vehicles, traffic participants, and diverse environmental conditions, including weather. We build upon the route
configurations provided by Bench2Drive (Jia et al., 2024) and introduce localized behavioral perturbations
while keeping the underlying maps, traffic participants, weather settings, and background traffic behavior
unchanged. This protocol yields two subsets: CARLA Ego and CARLA Adv. In CARLA Ego, we directly
perturb the behavior of the ego vehicle. In CARLA Adv, we instantiate an additional vehicle near the ego
vehicle and perturb its behavior, such that the resulting physical challenges primarily arise from interactions
between the two vehicles. Although the data produced by physics simulators exhibit good physical consistency,
they are limited to specific traffic scenarios, thereby restricting diversity. To address this, we employ advanced
video generators for data simulation.

Video Generator Data. To complement the scenario coverage of physics-based simulation, we construct a
long-tail driving dataset with open-sourced video diffusion model. Starting from 10,000 unique forward-facing
driving clips and their scene descriptions, we independently sample road environments, traffic conditions,
weather and illumination, visibility constraints, hazardous objects, event progression, and ego-vehicle responses,
and combine them into structured generation prompts. We use three complementary generation modes: T2VA
provides text-only scene conditioning, I2VA uses the first frame of the source clip to preserve the initial scene
layout and camera view, and Ref2VA uses a 1s reference sequence to retain scene structure, traffic motion,
and camera dynamics while introducing new hazards or physical interactions. All prompts are reviewed
for semantic and physical consistency, including weather–illumination compatibility, road structure, object
motion, occlusion causality, vehicle reaction time, braking distance, and temporal continuity. The prompts
that fail these checks are repaired before generation. Before using them for training, we manually review the
generated videos and filter out low-quality or inconsistent samples.

2.4    Data Engine
After collecting the aforementioned data, we proceed quality filtering, scoring, captioning, and condition
preparation.

Initial Quality Filtering. We first apply a coarse filtering stage to exclude videos that are technically
invalid or exhibit obvious visual degradation. Web-crawled videos frequently have inconsistent or mislabeled
metadata, poor visual quality, or undesirable overlays. Consequently, we screen samples using basic structural
and visual criteria, including extreme aspect ratios, insufficient frame counts, and discrepancies between the
observed visual content and the associated metadata. We also remove pseudo-high-definition videos, such as
low-resolution videos upsampled to a higher nominal resolution or incorrectly annotated as high-resolution.
Videos with excessive on-screen text, subtitles, watermarks, or other salient overlays are also discarded. Clips
exhibiting severe blur, including Gaussian-like blurring artifacts, are further excluded. Moreover, each video is
converted to the YUV color space, and clips with extreme luminance distributions are filtered out to eliminate
markedly underexposed or overexposed content.

High-Quality Scoring. After the initial filtering stage, we apply a second-stage curation to retain video
clips with high visual quality, sufficient motion, and stable temporal dynamics. We first assess perceptual
quality from both aesthetic and imaging perspectives. Overall video quality is quantified using DOVER (Wu
et al., 2023a), and clips with a DOVER score below 0.4 are excluded. In addition, we evaluate imaging quality
with COVER (He et al., 2024), which targets degradations such as compression artifacts and transmission-
induced distortions. Samples exhibiting pronounced image-quality impairments are removed. Next, we
characterize clip-level motion properties. We use the VMAF motion score (Li et al., 2016) as a surrogate
for global motion magnitude and retain only samples with scores in the range [2, 14]. This criterion filters
out near-static content with insufficient motion as well as clips dominated by excessively rapid or unstable
motion. We further apply heuristic constraints on camera trajectory. Specifically, we discard clips that (i)
contain multiple view reversals exceeding 150◦ within a 10-second clip, (ii) exhibit inter-frame view-angle
changes greater than 60◦ , or (iii) include abrupt camera translations whose short-term displacement exceeds
five times the mean translation magnitude computed over the entire video. Finally, we manually inspect



                                                        6
the remaining clips to remove residual failure cases that are not reliably captured by automated metrics.
This review emphasizes overall visual integrity and temporal coherence, thereby ensuring that the retained
high-quality data are suitable for mid-train and post-train.

Captioning. For the videos retained after quality curation, we apply a unified segmentation and re-
captioning pipeline. We uniformly sample all videos at 16 Hz and divide them into non-overlapping clips of
93 frames, corresponding to approximately 5.8 s per clip. Clips at the end of a video containing fewer than
50 frames are discarded to avoid generating excessively short training samples. Each resulting clip is then
passed to Qwen3.8 (Qwen Team, 2026) for automated re-captioning. We adopt a two-stage pipeline that
first produces a structured scene analysis and then converts this analysis into a dense, temporally coherent
narrative description. Given a sequence of video frames, Qwen3.8 first completes the Scene Analysis stage
and subsequently generates the Final Narrative conditioned on the structured analysis. The two outputs are
enclosed by the <scene_draft> and <final_prompt> tags, respectively.
    In the first stage, Qwen3.8 examines the clip frame by frame and populates a predefined JSON schema
describing its observable visual and temporal content. The structured representation captures the major
subjects and their appearance, pose, location, orientation, actions, and state changes, together with the
surrounding environment, lighting, aesthetics, cinematography, visual style, contextual information, textual
elements, temporal segments, transitions, and audio content. Temporal annotations use a unified M:SS-M
format, with timestamps measured relative to the beginning of the clip. The annotation follows a strict
observation-based policy: only visually or audibly observable information is recorded, and no unobserved
details are inferred or hallucinated. Fields that cannot be determined are left empty. Basic video metadata,
including resolution, aspect ratio, duration, and frame rate, is not generated by the model and is instead
injected automatically from the source video. The resulting structured representation serves as a normalized
intermediate description for the subsequent narrative generation stage.
    In the second stage, Qwen3.8 rewrites the structured scene analysis into a dense narrative caption. The
output is written as a single paragraph consisting of multiple coherent sentences, without lists or bullet points.
The narrative is required to cover the major subjects, scene setting, temporally ordered actions, relevant
background elements, lighting conditions, cinematographic characteristics, and temporal structure of the clip.
For each video clip, the re-captioning pipeline produces two complementary outputs: a structured JSON
scene analysis and a dense narrative caption. The former provides machine-readable descriptions of visual
attributes, temporal events, cinematography, and other structured information, while the latter provides a
fluent and temporally coherent natural-language description. Together, these representations provide both
structured supervision and dense textual descriptions.

Condition Preparation. Each training sample is paired with two types of conditions: a camera-view pose
pi and a world control wi . The world control has three forms: Driving layouts, Robotic layouts, and Scene
contexts. For the camera-view pose, we use the poses provided by structured datasets whenever available. For
samples without camera metadata, we estimate the pose with DA3 (Lin et al., 2026). We also include data
collected in game engines and Unreal Engine, which provide high-quality camera poses and complement the
estimated poses. Driving layouts are built from 3D object detections, lane detections, traffic-light recognition,
and traffic-sign recognition. We obtain these annotations through auto-labeling for our private MiData
collection. Public datasets generally provide the corresponding labels, which we convert to a common format.
We then render the aligned annotations with the Cosmos-Transfer tools (Ren et al., 2025). Robotic layouts are
constructed primarily from the Robotwin dataset for training and evaluation on TriWorldBench (Liu et al.,
2026b). Given the robot arm’s action sequence and configuration, we render its state from the corresponding
URDF model. Scene contexts are designed to improve consistency in driving closed-loop simulation. For an
input clip, the feed-forward reconstruction method in Sec. 3.5 first produces a dynamic 3D Gaussian Splatting
(3DGS) scene. During simulation, each new trajectory is rendered from the corresponding novel views of this
scene, and the resulting per-frame renders serve as world control so that the causal DiT can preserve the
geometry and appearance of the original scene. During training, we create input-target pairs by degrading
the ground-truth render at the original view. These degradations approximate the distribution of novel-view
renders from dynamic 3DGS and are applied to a small subset of the driving data (5%). We use three simple
degradation types: object-support degradation reduces and irregularizes the opacity of tracked objects to
mimic incomplete reconstruction, occlusion-cone holes remove parts of the static background that were hidden
in the source views, and depth degradation perturbs depths and boundaries to mimic novel-view errors.

2.5           Dataset Distribution Analysis
To support the effective mixing of data from different sources in subsequent training stages and guide the
selection of data mixture proportions and training configurations, we further analyze the final training dataset
distribution from three perspectives: sample count, caption length, and scene composition. As shown in

3     Causal World Model
To better serve Physical AI applications such as closed-loop evaluation and closed-loop training, a world
model should satisfy three requirements: interactivity, real-time performance, and controllability. Interactivity
means the model can be rolled out step-by-step in an autoregressive manner, generating the next short-horizon
future conditioned on the current state and control inputs. Real-time performance means each generation
step must finish within the time budget of the simulated step, so the closed-loop system is not blocked by
model inference. Controllability means the generated futures should faithfully follow the provided control
conditions (e.g., actions, world states, or camera poses), while remaining realistic and consistent with the
data distribution. These three requirements jointly impose a major challenge for model design: improving
quality and controllability often calls for larger models or more denoising steps, which increases latency and
can undermine both real-time performance and interactivity. To address this challenge, we make dedicated
efforts on both (i) the model architecture and condition injection, and (ii) a multi-stage progressive training
pipeline with distillation-based acceleration.
    In this section, we first introduce the overall architecture of our Causal World Model (Sec. 3.1) as shown
in Fig. 4, followed by the unified architecture adopted during pre-training (Sec. 3.2), the condition injection
strategy used in mid-training (Sec. 3.3), and distillation in post-training (Sec. 3.4).

stages involve heterogeneous conditional inputs and multi-view generation. To keep a unified architecture
throughout all stages, we incorporate the required architectural changes already during pre-training, including
the world control injection, the VTA module and the GLA module.

View Temporal Attention. VTA is designed to address the computational overhead introduced by an
increasing number of views. It reduces the growth of Attention computation while maintaining generation
quality and cross-view consistency, thereby preserving inference efficiency. Specifically, our Causal World
Model consists of L DiT Blocks, each of which contains a Cross-Attention module and a Self-Attention module.
For a sample with N views and a temporal length of T , the original Self-Attention flattens the view,
                                                                                                    temporal,
and spatial dimensions into a single sequence, and thus has an attention complexity of O N 2 T 2 . To reduce
the computational cost of multi-view modeling, VTA factorizes Self-Attention along the temporal and view
dimensions and organizes the DiT Blocks into alternating odd and even layers. Odd-numbered layers perform
only Temporal Attention, with N folded into the batch dimension. Even-numbered layers perform only View
Attention, with T folded into the batch dimension. After alternately stacking L layers, the core complexity,
ignoring the batch size and the spatial dimensions, becomes O (T N (N + T )), which maintains high inference
efficiency in both single-view and multi-view settings.
     Compared with other approaches, such as OmniDreams (Basant et al., 2026), our VTA does not introduce
additional Attention modules and computes. During pre-training, to ensure consistency with subsequent
multi-view training, the View Attention degenerates into intra-frame Self-Attention. Our experiments show
that this design does not lead to a significant performance degradation. With this design, the model establishes
a unified mechanism for multi-view correlation modeling, and our DiT Blocks require no modification when
extending the model to multi-view tasks, enabling multi-view models to inherit the generalization capabilities
learned during pre-training.

3.3    Mid-train
After pre-training, the Causal World Model already exhibits general generation capability, but it still needs to
be further extended to support conditional generation across diverse physical AI applications. The mid-train
stage has two concurrent goals: (i) enabling control conditions such as world control wi and camera view pose
pi to reliably steer the generated state, and (ii) extending the model to generate multi-view state sequences.
    For world control, we concatenate it into the latent-space channel reserved during pre-training. For camera
view-pose control and multi-view extension, we achieve this by modifying the original 3D RoPE (Wan et al.,
2025; Su et al., 2024) which also introduces no additional model parameters or computational overhead.
Overall, the model architecture remains consistent with the pre-training stage, so that it can inherit the
generalization ability and physical reasoning learned during pre-training.

Multi-view RoPE. We extend the original 3D RoPE to support positional encoding in the multi-view
setting. The original 3D RoPE partitions frequencies along the temporal, height, and width dimensions.
For multi-view scenarios, we allocate non-overlapping coordinates to different views along the width axis.
Concretely, for a sample with N views and temporal length T , we horizontally concatenate all views at the
same time step, yielding x ∈ RT ×h×(N w)×d , where w is the per-view width. A token at local coordinate
(i, u, v) in view n is assigned the absolute coordinate (i, h, nW + w).
     All views share the same temporal and height origins, while the width origin is shifted by nw. In
implementation, Temporal Attention first splits the concatenated sequence into N single-view sequences of
shape T × h × w, and then applies the original 3D RoPE with the corresponding width offset. As a result,
relative positions within each view are identical to the single-view case, while different views lie on distinct
horizontal position bands.

Fused PRoPE. Further, we condition our Causal World Model on camera view-pose with fused projective
rotary positional embeddings (Fused-PRoPE), following SolarWM (Huang et al., 2026a) and MosaicMem (Yu
et al., 2026). Each multi-view clip is converted into a geometric condition aligned with the latent video tokens.
Per-frame, per-view poses are expressed relative to a shared origin, namely the first frame of a center camera
together with its corresponding intrinsics. Within each DiT block, Multi-view RoPE represents temporal and
intra-view spatial positions, and camera view-pose control pi determines the projective matrix.

3.4    Post-train
Although the model acquires controllable generation capabilities through pre-training and mid-training, it
still cannot meet the requirements of real-time streaming inference. Moreover, during inference, the model
suffers from exposure bias. We therefore further distill the model to improve both inference efficiency and
long-term generation stability.

Streaming DMD. After the above consistency distillation, our model still suffers from exposure bias
during long-video inference. A common remedy is to apply DMD (Yin et al., 2024) with self-forcing-style
training. However, standard self-forcing is only trained with a short horizon, so the model cannot fully capture
long-range temporal dependencies, which still leads to suboptimal quality for long-video generation. Following
LongLive (Yang et al., 2025b), we extend self-forcing (Huang et al., 2026b) to a streaming DMD training
scheme, as illustrated in Fig. 4(a). Specifically, we train on long videos and split each sample into multiple
segments, where each segment length matches the training length of the bidirectional teacher. During training,
the student continuously rolls out the same sample across multiple consecutive steps; the rollout results are
then fed to the teacher model and a fake model to compute the DMD loss.
    Since a video may contain up to seven views, naively rolling out all segments and backpropagating the
DMD loss can easily trigger GPU out-of-memory. To address this, we introduce a Random Gradient Sampling
strategy. Each segment is composed of multiple chunks. Instead of recording gradients for every chunk as
in prior implementations, we only keep gradients for the last chunk and randomly select a small number
of earlier chunks to record gradients. In practice, this achieves training behavior comparable to full-chunk
backpropagation while substantially reducing memory consumption.

3.5    WorldRec 2.0
To enhance consistency and stability in long-horizon causal inference, we introduce an explicit scene context
grounded in real observations. Concretely, we propose WorldRec 2.0 to construct a persistent 3D scene
state and use it as a geometry-aware prior for generation. Given multi-camera, multi-temporal observations,
WorldRec 2.0 reconstructs a scene state composed of static and dynamic 3D Gaussians in a single forward
pass, and renders it under a target camera and timestamp. The rendered prior is then fed into the generator
through the world-control channels introduced in Sec. 3.3, serving as a stable 3D scene memory for causal
generation. In this way, WorldRec 2.0 anchors geometry, appearance, and cross-view correspondence in
observed regions, while the Causal World Model generates more realistic scenes and completes fine-grained
appearance details as well as motion details beyond the observations.

of sparse 3D queries. Each query is initialized from its 3D position, color, image feature, and Plücker ray.
Static queries stay in world coordinates. For each dynamic instance k, observed points are mapped into a
canonical coordinate system using the instance pose Tk (τ ):

This point-aligned representation helps reduce duplicate points and the ghosting artifacts introduced by

an efficient communication path. In contrast, applying full global attention to tens of thousands of queries

4     Training and Inference
4.1    Multi Stage Training
Our Causal world model training process is mainly divided into three stages. The training data and data
mixture differ across stages, but we keep the model architecture consistent throughout, allowing the weights
from the previous stage to be reused as the initialization for the next stage. The specific data usage is
summarized in Tab. 2.

Stage I: Pre-training. In Stage I, our model architecture is identical to that described in Sec. 3.2. Using
this architecture, we train two models separately: a bidirectional model and an autoregressive model. Both
models are trained with the flow-matching loss. Given a sample x1:T , we denote its corresponding clean latent
as x0 and random noise as x1 ∼ N (0, I) (we omit subscripts for simplicity). We sample a timestep t ∈ [0, 1]
from a logit-normal distribution and define the intermediate latent xt and its corresponding ground-truth
velocity v t as
                                  xt = (1 − t)x0 + tx1 ,     v t = x1 − x0 .                               (8)
   We feed xt into the model to predict the velocity uθ (xt | w, p, I0 , c). and the loss is computed using MSE.
For the bidirectional model, all chunks xti , i ∈ {1, . . . , T }, use the same timestep t, and full attention is applied
between all chunks. For the unidirectional model, we use Diffusion Forcing for training and assign a different
timestep ti to each chunk xtii . We apply a causal attention mask, so that xtii can attend only to the historical
chunks x1:i−1 . During training, we use a learning rate of 1 × 10−4 and the AdamW optimizer with β1 = 0.9,
β2 = 0.95, and a weight decay of 5 × 10−2 . To facilitate optimization, we first train the bidirectional model
and use its learned parameters to initialize the Diffusion Forcing (Chen et al., 2024a) model. We group every
two latent frames into one chunk (i.e., chunksize=2).


                                                               14
Stage II: Mid-train. In Stage II, our goal is to endow the model with controllability and the ability to
generate multi-view states, while preserving its general generation capability. We therefore adopt a progressive,
multi-step training curriculum. First, we mix single-view data with multi-view data and train with T2V
and I2V tasks, where the single-view data comes from the high-quality subset of General Data and Physical
World Data, and the multi-view data comes from driving Data and robot Data. Next, we introduce camera
view-pose conditioning with a certain probability, resulting in a hybrid training objective over T2V, I2V, and
CI2V. Finally, we incorporate world-control conditions and train with T2V, I2V, CI2V, WI2V, and WCI2V.
We design this curriculum because CI2V is more challenging and has more data than WCI2V; introducing
the harder task earlier helps the model learn complex conditional generation. In this stage, we initialize the
bidirectional model and the autoregressive model separately from their Stage I counterparts.

Stage III: Post-train. In Stage III, we optimize the model for downstream deployment by post-training
on a small, carefully curated subset from the high-quality portions of driving Data and robot Data. We
explicitly balance this subset to match the desired application-specific distribution and to ensure both high
quality and diversity. This is crucial because CD and Streaming DMD are prone to mode collapse, which
can lead to a substantial drop in sample diversity; by finely adjusting the training samples, we encourage
the model to recover both diversity and fidelity. Concretely, we run post-training separately for different
application scenarios. Before applying CD and Streaming DMD, we further fine-tune the bidirectional model
and the autoregressive model on the corresponding scenario-specific high-quality subset, using a learning rate
of 2 × 10−2 for roughly one epoch. We then perform CD and Streaming DMD to obtain scenario-specific
weights (e.g., autonomous-driving 7V and embodied 3V). Meanwhile, we reduce the training objective to
a fixed task (e.g., WCI2V) so that only a small number of optimization steps is needed to achieve strong
downstream performance. During the Streaming DMD training stage, we roll out the student model for two
steps and distill a model that performs two-step denoising with chunksize=2.

4.2    Inference.
In closed-loop evaluation and training, inference throughput and latency are critical. We speed up inference
along three dimensions: world-control generation, VAE encoding/decoding, and DiT denoising. First, world-
control signals must be rendered online based on the ego pose and dynamic scene changes. Following
FlashDreams (FlashDreams Contributors, 2026; Basant et al., 2026), we use Ludus to accelerate rendering.
Second, for VAE encoding and decoding, we combine torch.compile (Ansel et al., 2024) with CUDA
Graphs (Guide, 2020) to reduce kernel-launch overhead and improve GPU utilization. Third, for DiT
denoising, we allocate a fixed-size KV cache for the local-attention window. To keep training and inference
consistent, we also use a fixed KV-cache size during post-training for self rollouts. This keeps the computation
graph and tensor shapes constant at inference time, enabling CUDA-Graph acceleration for DiT. We further
run inference in parallel on 8 GPUs. In the 7V setting, we shard the tokens of each view across 7 GPUs, while
the remaining GPU handles VAE encoding for world control. The generated latents are then decoded on the
same 7 GPUs. We implement context parallelism via ring attention (Liu et al., 2024).

the policy network and value evaluation network corresponding to expert actions via the Proximal Policy
Optimization (PPO) algorithm. The mathematical formulation of the post-training policy loss function is

5.4.1   World Action Model for Driving
Generation and reconstruction quality alone do not establish that a world model has learned scene information
relevant to driving decisions. Closed-loop driving also requires the model to understand drivable regions,
traffic-participant motion, ego status, collision risk, and the effect of ego actions on future states. We therefore
connect spatiotemporal world latents from a causal world model to a World-Action Model (WAM) planner
and evaluate the interface with on NAVSIM (Dauner et al., 2024) using the Predictive Driver Model Score
(PDMS) and its component metrics.

System structure. The system is organized as an upstream causal world model followed by an Action
Model planner. The information flow is illustrated in Fig. 16 and the qualitative trajectory examples are shown
in Fig. 17. The causal world model receives historical multi-frame observations and encodes a spatiotemporal
world latent. It supports an RGB mode and a mode augmented with scene-understanding conditions such
as depth and segmentation. This world latent is provided as conditioning information to the Action Model,
which also receives multi-camera history from four directions, ego status, and scene conditions. The Action
Model maintains action tokens and lets them interact with the world latent to produce updated action

Figure 17 Qualitative trajectory comparison for WAM planning. Candidate trajectories produced by the Action Model
conditioned on the causal world model are visualized in the ego-centric scene, showing how the causal world model
affects trajectory selection and driving behavior.


representations. A Trajectory Decoder maps the resulting representations to a fixed number of candidate
future trajectories, and a scoring module predicts safety, drivability, progress, and comfort terms before
selecting the highest-scoring trajectory.
    The causal world model input five historical frames from a forward-facing camera. Its backbone contains a
spatiotemporal VAE and 33 intermediate DiT layers, which provide representations related to future states
and action consequences. The Action Model uses four cameras with four historical frames per camera and a
joint DINOv2 ViT-S/14 and VJEPA 2 visual backbone. Each view contains 16 learned scene tokens, and the
action branch outputs 64 action tokens. The planning horizon is 4 s with a 0.5 s interval, giving eight time
points per candidate trajectory.

Planning validation. For the controlled comparison between Base and Base + causal world model, we
keep the Action Model, Trajectory Decoder, training budget, data split, and post-processing fixed. Only
the use of the world latent and the addition of scene-understanding capabilities are changed. Here, NC
denotes no-at-fault collision avoidance, DAC denotes drivable-area compliance, TTC denotes time-to-collision,
C denotes comfort, and EP denotes ego progress. PDMS is the aggregate Predictive Driver Model Score
computed from these behavior terms. In Tab. 11, the comparison between Base and Base + causal world
model highlights the contribution of the generative world representation. Adding the causal world-model
branch increases DAC from 98.7 to 99.0, EP from 92.8 to 93.6, and PDMS from 94.9 to 95.3, while keeping

performance in the Trainval comparison setting on NAVSIM v1.1 navtest.

5.4.2   World Action Model for Robot
Concretely, we instantiate a World Action Model (WAM) for embodied control on top of our Causal World
Model, following Fast-WAM (Yuan et al., 2026). We reuse the Causal World Model DiT as the video-branch
backbone, and introduce an additional action-expert DiT that shares attention with the video DiT. During
training, the video DiT takes the clean first-frame latents together with future noisy latents, while action
tokens in the action DiT are restricted to attend only to the clean first-frame latents. At inference time, the
video DiT performs a single forward pass conditioned on the clean first-frame latents. We initialize the video
DiT with the bidirectional model weights after mid-training.
    Tab. 12 presents our LIBERO Plus (Fei et al., 2025) benchmark results, where our method reaches a 75.0%
average success rate. It surpasses Fast-WAM (Yuan et al., 2026) and Fast-WAM-Joint (Yuan et al., 2026), with
the most prominent gains observed under layout, camera, and background distribution shifts. These results
indicate that the representations learned by our Causal World Model during pre-training and mid-training
transfer effectively to downstream WAM, providing strong spatial priors for embodied manipulation planning.
