 6                                         INTRODUCTION

 7          1.     Apple set quite a trap for employees. Bright young minds thought they would work

 8 in Cupertino. Instead they were joining Hotel California: “you can check out any time you like, but

 9 you can never leave.” When employees try to leave Apple, it pulls them back in or punishes them
10 for doing so—using trapdoors Apple created through its own policies and products to accuse former

11 employees of trade secret theft.

12          2.     Indeed, Apple created a situation in which it can accuse virtually any departing
13 employee, and their new employer, of “stealing” purported Apple trade secrets.

14          3.     It’s a simple trick Apple has resorted to many times before. Apple encourages and
15 incentivizes its employees to store Apple work files in their personal iCloud accounts, and to

16 conduct substantive Apple business through iMessage using their personal iMessage accounts.

17 Work documents and communications then, by design, become inextricably tied to employees’

18 private files and messages. That tangled web then synchronizes across countless work and personal

19 devices. Combine this with Apple’s overbroad Intellectual Property Agreement and its practice of

20 designating basically every document generated by Apple employees at work as “Apple

21 confidential” information, and virtually every Apple employee will have “Apple confidential”

22 information in their personal accounts, intertwined with their personal messages—and thus

23 accessible from all of their Apple devices, both work-issued and personal—at any given time.

24          4.     If this information were truly top-secret crown jewels, it would obviously be
25 unreasonable for Apple to distribute that material so widely, or to let so many personal accounts of

26 so many employees carry that information to so many devices with so little oversight. But this is

27 what Apple has done, for years—and not by accident. This is how Apple tries to monitor and ensnare

28 departing employees.

 1          5.     Apple understands the privacy implications of its practices. It warns consumers

 2 against sharing their account information with anyone, including family, noting that doing so would

 3 expose their personal data and content. Yet Apple encourages its own employees to do the opposite:

 4 share their work and personal information in one account. Why does Apple do this? To get continued

 5 access to those accounts after an employee leaves, and to set a trap for both that employee and her

 6 future employers—a trap that can be sprung whenever Apple sees an opportunity to slow down a

 7 potential competitor.

 8          6.     Here’s how it works. If an Apple employee wants to leave Apple for a competitor,

 9 she almost certainly has documents in her personal iCloud account and communications in her
10 personal iMessages containing what Apple will call trade secrets. Since Apple syncs that personal

11 iCloud and iMessage account across all devices—work devices and personal devices—Apple’s own

12 tools make Apple information automatically accessible from or on all of her Apple devices. If this

13 employee tells her manager she’s leaving for a company Apple does not perceive as a competitor,

14 Apple could theoretically spend the time needed to sort out the mess Apple made of her personal

15 iCloud account—for example, a multi-week transition period to try to disentangle the mess and to

16 transition her work to other Apple employees. But if she is leaving for a company Apple wants to

17 punish, that multi-week transition period disappears. Rather than trying to clean up the mess Apple

18 created, Apple will walk employees out the door the same day they give notice.

19          7.     This creates lots of problems. Among them, it prevents the departing employee from
20 working with Apple to separate out Apple confidential information and messages from personal

21 information and messages in her possession. The disentanglement process would be complicated

22 under the best of circumstances, and is effectively impossible when Apple skips the offboarding

23 process and rushes an employee out the door that same day. After years of telling the employee to

24 store Apple information in her personal iCloud account, intermingled with her personal files, and to

25 conduct Apple business via personal iMessages, Apple knows the separation will take more than a

26 day. But Apple sets these employees up for failure anyway, so that it can later complain and punish

27 the employee for leaving Apple by filing lawsuits tarnishing the employee’s reputation.

 1          8.     So now the employee has left Apple but still has what Apple considers confidential

 2 and “trade secret” information in her personal iCloud and iMessage account, and across any personal

 3 devices that have synced to that account. Apple can later claim to “discover” a problem with this

 4 scenario (even though Apple created all the policies and tools that led to this predictable scenario,

 5 and was well aware of its widespread prevalence). Then Apple can come after her, and her new

 6 employer, opportunistically whenever it wants. Not only can Apple bring baseless lawsuits, but it

 7 can demand broad, unfettered, and invasive discovery into the former employee’s personal devices

 8 and materials, as it has done in this case and before.

 9          9.     At that point, the departing Apple employee has two choices: she can keep the Apple
10 materials stored in her personal accounts, in which case Apple will say she’s still improperly in

11 possession of them, or she can attempt to delete them, in which case Apple will say she’s spoliated

12 relevant evidence. And even if she deletes the materials at Apple’s request, automated iCloud and

13 iMessage backup and syncing processes (that Apple created) often return them to her account

14 anyway. For longstanding employees like Tang Tan—a widely respected, world-class innovator—

15 who mixed personal and work messages and files for decades by following Apple policies, it might

16 take years to unwind the tangled web Apple created. Those employees face an impossible choice:

17 keep decades of personal material and get blamed for it, or delete decades of personal material (and

18 maybe get blamed for that, too).

19          10.    None of this is an accident. Apple has used lawsuits complaining about former
20 employees’ accidental and unavoidable retention of Apple materials many times in recent years,

21 seeking both to punish departing employees who dare to leave for competitors (as well as the

22 competitors themselves) and to scare current Apple employees away from following others out the

23 door.

25 Apple employees who left for OpenAI, and threatened dozens more, based on Apple making files

26 available on their personal accounts and devices through iCloud, Box, and other tangled webs of

27 mixed work and personal files. After telling longtime employees like Tang Tan to blur the lines

28 between personal and work accounts, Apple now complains that the lines between personal and

19                                    NATURE OF THE ACTION
20          14.    Thanks to OpenAI’s pioneering artificial intelligence (“AI”) innovation and product

21 development over the past several years, AI has become increasingly ubiquitous in American

22 consumers’ lives, providing benefits that were not even imaginable earlier this decade. Consumers

23 are also demanding more and more innovative AI capabilities in their personal electronic devices.

24          15.    For years, Apple has dominated the market for various personal electronic devices.

25 But it has struggled to incorporate innovative AI capabilities into its previously popular products.

26 OpenAI, meanwhile, is in the process of developing new, much-anticipated AI-enabled personal

27 electronic devices—threatening to outcompete Apple on what Apple views as its own turf.

 1          16.    If Apple could compete with OpenAI on the merits, it would. But it can’t. So Apple

 2 is instead trying to leverage its size and wealth to forestall competition from OpenAI through a wide

 3 variety of unlawful, unfair, and tortious conduct.

 4          17.    That course of conduct includes the claims Apple filed in this very case. But it also

 5 includes a whole host of unlawful schemes: efforts to misappropriate OpenAI’s trade secrets and

 6 confidential information; attempts to keep OpenAI from accessing key suppliers for device

 7 development and manufacturing, including coercing and threatening those suppliers; and efforts to

 8 keep critical engineering and operations talent from choosing to work at OpenAI.

 9          18.    For example, Apple has used its size, and the resulting special relationships with
10 specialized hardware manufacturers, to impede OpenAI’s independent access to critical

11 manufacturing services and talent. OpenAI has invested in original hardware development, qualified

12 specialized vendors, and recruited talented, experienced engineers. Apple buys from some of the

13 same suppliers and employs many engineers with similar expertise. On information and belief,

14 Apple has responded to OpenAI’s anticipated entry into the hardware market by unlawfully

15 pressuring vendors not to work with OpenAI and using expansive employment restraints to limit

16 OpenAI’s access to talented engineers. Apple’s power over suppliers and engineers cannot lawfully

17 be used to exclude competing purchasers and employers from the market.

18          19.    Apple’s global supply-management personnel have behaved similarly, demanding
19 that shared vendors identify workers assigned to OpenAI and pressuring those vendors not to assign

20 resources to OpenAI projects. Suppliers have indicated that they fear losing Apple allocations if

21 they provide OpenAI the support they otherwise would offer. The economic consequences are

22 concrete. A manufacturer that had previously performed OpenAI work without issue declined to

23 continue doing so, forcing OpenAI to spend time and money finding another manufacturer for the

24 same work. Another supplier segregated OpenAI work onto a nighttime shift of an anodizing process

25 in a shared facility after Apple, for no apparent reason other than to inconvenience OpenAI, objected

26 to OpenAI work being done at the same time as Apple production. The reasons Apple has done all

27 of this are obvious: to raise OpenAI’s costs and reduce its available options. In other words, to

28 compete unfairly by any means available.

  3                                  FIRST AFFIRMATIVE DEFENSE

  4                                            (Unclean Hands)

  5                                      Asserted by: All Defendants

  6          Apple’s claims for injunctive and other equitable relief are barred, in whole or in part, by the

  7 doctrine of unclean hands, for multiple independent reasons.

  8          As one example, Apple seeks to enjoin OpenAI based on allegations that former Apple

  9 employees misappropriated Apple’s trade secrets in connection with OpenAI’s development of
 10 competing technology. Yet Apple has itself done what it alleges OpenAI did in connection with the

 11 same device-development competition at issue in this case. Indeed, on the equities, what Apple has

 12 done is far worse: Apple’s conduct cannot be attributed to any entanglement of corporate and

 13 personal devices, and instead reflects a deliberate transfer of trade secrets from one system to an

 14 entirely different system. Specifically, a long-time, senior Apple employee (“Employee X”)—who

 15 briefly joined OpenAI in late 2025 only to return, about six months later, to Apple—transferred

 16 numerous files and archives to personal accounts and devices in the weeks preceding his departure

 17 from OpenAI, including archives containing dozens of individual files and significant quantities of

 18 OpenAI’s confidential and proprietary information concerning OpenAI’s unreleased hardware,

 19 product designs, engineering specifications, manufacturing processes, and supplier operations. On

 20 information and belief, following his return to Apple, Employee X disclosed information concerning

 21 OpenAI’s confidential development activities to senior Apple personnel, including at their request.

 22 And on information and belief, Apple received, retained, used, or otherwise benefited from

 23 OpenAI’s confidential information. Apple’s conduct bears directly on the subject matter of this

 24 action and the equities underlying its requested injunction, which seeks to restrict OpenAI’s

 25 competitive device-development activities based on the very type of alleged misconduct in which

 26 Apple has itself engaged. Under these circumstances, it would be inequitable to grant Apple the
