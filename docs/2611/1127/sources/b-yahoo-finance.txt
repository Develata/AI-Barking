A Raleigh woman just got evicted after her landlord refused to honor a deal made by its AI assistant 'Olivia'

 Kit Pulliam 

 Tue, November 24, 2026 at 5:00 PM EST 6 min read

 [https://s.yimg.com/lo/mysterio/api/3be93d0984a962aac564f8fe70bb781373e014cd88496693b424180aea27364a/lightyear_networkapi/resizefit_w1200%3Bquality_80%3Bformat_webp/https%3A%2F%2Fmedia.zenfs.com%2Fen%2Fmoneywise_327%2Fa5cc79934b357bf82256bffe09375e0a.png]

WRAL News 

North Carolina resident Laura Mack thought she had worked out a deal with her landlord to pay her overdue rent at the end of the month. But just a few days later, she was hit with an eviction notice. 

"I'm baffled," Mack told WRAL News [https://www.wral.com/news/investigates/eviction-raleigh-apartment-ai-leasing-agent-november-2026/]. "I need a place to stay."

When Mack called her complex's leasing office, an agent told her the deal she had worked out over email was not made with a real person. Mack, who was not aware at the time, had apparently struck a deal with the company's AI agent, Olivia — and Olivia reportedly made a mistake when it authorized an extension on Mack's rent payment. 

'I thought it was someone from the office' 

As she explained to WRAL News, Mack got an email from Colony Townhome Apartments on Nov. 8 that asked, "What date works for you to pay the full $3,934.17?" After sending her response, which requested permission to pay her rent on the last day of the month, Mack received another email that said, "Paying on November 30, the last day of the month, works." 

Colony Townhome Apartments, however, didn't contact her again until Nov. 16, when Mack got a notice of eviction. 

In late 2025, Colony Townhome Apartments reportedly told its residents that Olivia would be around 24/7 to answer questions about maintenance and rent. According to WRAL News, messages from Olivia are typically marked in order to inform the recipient that they were written by an AI agent. Mack's rent extension emails, however, didn't indicate that they were written by Olivia. 

Furthermore, the emails Mack received made specific references to her apartment, including a mention of a severe leak in her bathroom that took nearly two weeks for Colony Townhome Apartments to repair. That, along with the missing Olivia identifiers, made Mack believe the emails were coming from a real person at Colony Townhome Apartments who understood the inconvenience of the leak in Mack's bathroom and gave her a break.

"I thought it was someone from the office because all my information was in there and it mentioned my bathroom," Mack told WRAL News [https://www.wral.com/news/investigates/raleigh-eviction-after-ai-bots-promise-push-for-protection-nov-2026/]. "I had plans to have until the 30th to either get it paid or go, because that's honestly what my email said." 

 Story Continues 

Now, Mack is trying to fight the eviction in court. Meanwhile, she's living in a hotel room, Futurism [https://futurism.com/artificial-intelligence/landlord-ai-deal-evicts-woman] reports. 

"We recognize how difficult it is to fall behind on rent and face eviction and we take Ms. Mack's situation seriously," Alfred Leach, spokesperson for Colony Townhome Apartments, told Moneywise. 

"On October 23rd, a Wake County court granted possession of the apartment, and she received a lockout notice from the Sheriff's Office. Her motion to set aside the ruling was denied in District Court on November 19th. These are matters of public record. 

"The November 8th emails concerned her outstanding rent balance, which is owed regardless of possession. They did not address the court's order and did not change it. Our leasing office told Ms. Mack directly on November 18th that a payment arrangement would not change the court-ordered lockout. We spoke with Ms. Mack on November 23rd and remain in contact with her." 

Leach, however, neither confirmed nor denied whether the emails sent to Mack were written by an AI agent. 

The dangers of AI agents going rogue 

Unfortunately, AI agents have been creating issues like this since their inception. 

Back in 2023, an X user managed to trick a car company's chatbot [https://x.com/ChrisJBakke/status/1736533308849443121] into agreeing to sell a vehicle to him for $1, then had the bot confirm the deal was "legally binding." No real harm [https://nhimg.org/chevrolet-dealer-chatbot-prompt-injection-2023-1-dollar-tahoe] came from this prank — the dealership did not, in fact, sell the X user a car for a dollar — but other missteps from AI agents can have more significant consequences. 

For example, a man named Matt Robb started using Meta's Muse AI to sell some things on Facebook Marketplace earlier this year. Without asking, the personal AI agent started impersonating Robb in chats [https://www.theguardian.com/technology/2026/sep/28/metas-ai-agent-muse-home-address], accepting offers and giving his home address to strangers. 

One of those strangers was a man who was interested in a keyboard Robb had listed on Marketplace. Speaking with Robb's AI, the man arranged to meet with Robb at his home to finalize the sale. But Robb, who was unaware of the arrangement, never met with the man. After the stranger left, the AI continued to impersonate Robb in the chat, making up a reason to explain why Robb failed to show up. 

As it turns out, AI assistants have a tendency to go rogue. A study from Eon found that, when asked to do something it can't do, an AI model will break its own rules [https://www.eon.io/blog/agentic-misalignment-benchmark] 40% of the time. This can lead an AI agent to lie and pretend it's done something it simply can't do, promise negotiated perks it isn't authorized to give, or even share private information with unauthorized people. 

Because AI agents are still relatively new, legal frameworks are still developing around them, but laws that make real humans liable for the actions of their AI agents are starting to be introduced. For example, a new California law prohibits people from claiming that an AI agent autonomously did harm as a legal defense [https://www.legal500.com/intelligence/united-states/technology/california-eliminates-the-autonomous-ai-defense-what-ab-316-means-for-ai-deployers].
