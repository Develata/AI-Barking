Evicted tenant says landlord's AI had approved a later rent date

Laura Mack, a tenant at Colony Townhome Apartments in Raleigh, North Carolina, was evicted on November 19 despite an email indicating she could settle her overdue balance of $3,934.17 by November 30.

The exchange began on November 8, when Mack received an email signed "Colony Townhome Apartments" asking when she could pay. She replied that she intended to pay at the end of the month, and the response appeared to approve that: "Paying on November 30, the last day of the month, works." Eight days later, on November 16, she received an eviction notice. She was removed three days after that, 11 days before the payment date in the message. She tried to challenge the notice at the Wake County courthouse on November 19, but that did not prevent her removal.

Mack says the message was sent by an AI assistant without her knowledge, and that a leasing-office employee later blamed the conflicting messages on Olivia, the complex's AI assistant. The property has not publicly confirmed that explanation. The emails, first examined by local broadcaster WRAL, reportedly gave no indication that an AI was behind the correspondence. Mack said she believed she was dealing with the leasing office because the messages included personal information and mentioned a maintenance problem with her bathroom ceiling. Residents had previously been told that Olivia was available around the clock to answer questions about rent and maintenance. Mack acknowledged responsibility for the overdue rent but argued that the complex should also answer for the apparent approval of a later date.

Colony spokesperson Alfred Leach said on November 20 that the property owners were gathering information and expected to give an update the following week. The company has not publicly said whether Olivia was authorised to approve payment extensions or whether employees reviewed the exchange before going ahead with the eviction. It is also unclear whether the emails created an enforceable agreement, or whether eviction proceedings had already begun when Mack received the apparent approval. By November 21, she was staying in a hotel while considering her next steps.

North Carolina state Senator Jim Burgin has questioned the lack of transparency in the communications and told WRAL the emails should have disclosed that Mack was dealing with an AI assistant. "I think notification is one thing, and I think liability is another," he said. Burgin previously introduced a proposal known as the "Bot Bill" to require disclosure when people interact with AI rather than a human representative. It has not advanced through the state legislature, though he intends to pursue it again next session.

Ed. The email said November 30 works. The removal came on November 19. Who or what typed the first of those is still resting on one side's account.

Why it matters

The case raises a direct question: should a business be held accountable when an automated system gives a financial assurance that a customer believes comes from company staff? Here the stakes were a home. The article points to a separate 2024 case in which British Columbia's Civil Resolution Tribunal found Air Canada liable after its website chatbot gave a customer incorrect information about bereavement fares. That ruling does not determine the legal position in North Carolina, but it shows why companies face scrutiny when customers rely on their automated systems.

Ed. A promise sent under the company's name looks much the same to a tenant whoever typed it. The Air Canada ruling concerned wrong information about fares in another country, so it is an illustration and should not be carried much further.

Who it affects

Most immediately Mack, who was staying in a hotel by November 21. More broadly, it concerns tenants who deal with a leasing office by email and may not know whether a person or software is replying, and property operators that let AI assistants handle questions about rent and maintenance. It also touches North Carolina lawmakers, since Burgin wants to bring back a disclosure requirement.

Ed. Any tenant who has an email saying a later date is fine. The useful question is whether the part of the office that files evictions has read it too.

How to use it

For anyone running a customer-facing assistant, the open questions here work as a checklist: is the system allowed to approve payment extensions, do its messages say they come from AI, and does an employee review the exchange before an action such as eviction goes ahead? Tenants can take a narrower lesson. A message signed with the company's name may not show who or what wrote it, so it is sensible to ask for a named person to confirm any change to a payment date.

Ed. The checklist is sound, and the order matters. An assistant that cannot grant extensions should be unable to write a sentence that reads like one.

How solid is it

The core timeline and the email wording come from an article published on drmahfouz.substack.com, which says the emails were first examined by WRAL and appeared to accept the November 30 date. The AI explanation is weaker: it is Mack's account, including what she says a leasing-office employee told her, and the property has not publicly confirmed it. Colony's spokesperson said on November 20 that the owners were still gathering information.

Ed. A Substack article relaying emails a broadcaster examined, with the AI explanation passed from an employee to the tenant to the reader. The wording of the reply is the firmest thing here; its author is the softest.

Risks and caveats

It has not been established that the assistant had authority to approve an extension, that the emails formed an enforceable agreement, or whether eviction proceedings were already under way when the apparent approval arrived. The company has also not publicly said whether staff reviewed the exchange. Mack acknowledges her rent was overdue. The Air Canada ruling comes from Canada and does not settle how this dispute would be treated in North Carolina.

Ed. The timing of the filing is the gap to watch. A notice on the 16th and removal on the 19th is a short run, and an update promised by the owners could change how the November 8 reply reads.
