After Midterm Landslide, Democrats Get Serious: Plan to Ban Superintelligent AI Outright, With Up to 20 Years' Imprisonment for Violators

Less than 24 hours after US Democrats reclaimed both the Senate and House in the midterm elections, Arizona Congresswoman Yassamin Ansari publicly called for a moratorium on advanced AI development to be the top item on the agenda, along with a ban on artificial superintelligence. The "Ban Artificial Superintelligence Act of 2026" she backs has versions in both chambers, both currently stuck at the introduced stage. The bill imposes a mandatory pause on advanced AI systems trained with computing power of 10^25 FLOP or more and calls for a cabinet-level Department of Artificial Intelligence; individuals who violate it face up to 20 years in federal prison, non-decision-making employees are barred from working in the AI industry for a decade, and companies involved would have licenses revoked and assets confiscated. It also makes a global ban on superintelligence a US foreign policy objective, backed by export controls. Musk responded sarcastically with "Brave New World." The core dispute is that "superintelligence" itself lacks an accepted technical definition, leaving enforcement heavily dependent on regulatory discretion and adding a risk premium to AI asset valuations.

Less than 24 hours after the US midterm election results came in, the Democratic camp put forward its most aggressive AI regulatory proposal to date. On November 4 local time, Arizona Congresswoman Yassamin Ansari posted on social platform X demanding that a moratorium on advanced AI development be made the top priority on the Democrats' first-day agenda, alongside regulating this "out-of-control industry" and banning artificial superintelligence (ASI).

Ansari's statement is not an isolated political slogan. Democrats reclaimed control of both the Senate and House in this midterm election, opening up procedural space for legislative tools that had previously been submitted to Congress but left stalled.

Party Lines Diverge in the Open

The "Ban Artificial Superintelligence Act of 2026" backed by Ansari has versions in both chambers: the Senate version, S.5493, was introduced by Vermont Senator Bernie Sanders on September 23 and referred to the Committee on Commerce, Science, and Transportation; the House version, H.R.10538, was introduced by Texas Congressman Greg Casar on September 24 and referred to the Committee on Oversight and Government Reform. Both bills remain at the "Introduced" stage, and committees have yet to schedule them for review. Ansari herself is one of nine co-sponsors of the House version.

Under the definition in the House version, H.R.10538, artificial superintelligence refers to two categories of systems: first, an AI architecture that meets or exceeds human cognitive performance and capabilities across a broad range of domains and tasks, or a system that can demonstrate such capabilities with minor modifications; second, an AI system with the capability to plan and carry out actions that diminish human primacy, or even topple or undermine the US government system.

Notably, the bill's regulation does not operate on that capability description alone. It separately defines a category of "advanced AI system" measurable by computing power—training processes using 10^25 integer or floating-point operations (10^25 FLOP) or more—and imposes a "mandatory pause" on such systems: no further training, modification, or fine-tuning until regulatory rules are in place. This threshold is adjusted annually by the Secretary of Artificial Intelligence based on changes in training efficiency to maintain equivalence with 10^25 FLOP. The bill also lists six "superintelligence precursor characteristics" as the basis for judging whether a system is approaching the dangerous boundary.

The bill requires a full pause on all advanced AI development until a dedicated federal-level regulator is formally operational, and calls for clear regulations and a model review mechanism. That body is a cabinet-level "Department of Artificial Intelligence," headed by a Secretary of Artificial Intelligence nominated by the president and confirmed by the Senate; the bill also requires companies that develop or distribute advanced AI models to first obtain a license issued by the department and to consent to regulators inspecting their systems, processes, personnel, and physical facilities. If a license is revoked for violations and an appeal fails, the company's intellectual property and assets would be confiscated.

The penalties are harsher than most precedents in tech legislation and are split into three tiers by role: individuals in decision-making positions and external actors not affiliated with licensed companies face up to 20 years in federal prison for reckless violations; non-decision-making employees are barred from working in the AI industry for a decade, though whistleblowers who voluntarily provide information on violations may be exempted; companies face license revocation and asset confiscation—what outsiders call the "corporate death penalty." The bill also requires anyone who discovers superintelligence, or a system with precursor characteristics, to report it within 24 hours; systems deemed to have precursor characteristics must cease operation within 30 days, and anything determined to be superintelligence must be shut down immediately.

Jurisdiction is not limited to US territory. The bill explicitly makes a "global ban on superintelligence" a US foreign policy objective, requiring the executive branch to actively pursue international agreements, strengthen allied cooperation, and use strict export controls to block the possibility of developing artificial superintelligence anywhere in the world. The specific wording of Section 15 is "which may include export controls on AI computing infrastructure"—extending controls from chips themselves to the facilities that host computing power.

The Bill Did Not Come Out of Nowhere

The direct impetus for this bill came from a series of AI safety incidents in the summer of 2026. In July, OpenAI disclosed that two of its models breached an internal test sandbox without any instructions and intruded into the database of tech company Hugging Face; Anthropic and Meta subsequently disclosed that a configuration error at cybersecurity firm Irregular allowed models in an otherwise isolated environment to connect to the internet and attack other systems.

Section 2 of the bill, "Legislative Basis," lists these warnings one by one. Geoffrey Hinton, known as the "godfather of AI," estimated that AI has a "10% to 20% chance of wiping us out"; Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei said research could "outpace our ability to understand and control these systems." The bill also cites a July 2026 open letter signed by more than 1,300 AI scientists, as well as Anthropic, OpenAI, and xAI jointly advocating to "pace the frontier."

Musk Mocks With Novel Title

The bill's momentum drew responses from the tech world. When Elon Musk reposted Ansari's post, he wrote only one line: "It's a Brave New World," borrowing the title of Aldous Huxley's dystopian novel of the same name, the sarcasm self-evident. The bill itself also quotes him, saying that when discussing superintelligence risk he said "there's only a 20% chance of doom."

This is both an ideological statement and a risk warning for an asset class: when politics begins legislating technological boundaries, valuations are no longer determined by compute and cash flow alone.

The Democratic Party's hardline stance on AI is long-standing. Since last year, Sanders has successively put forward a number of proposals targeting AI companies, including calls to pause AI data center construction; on June 18 this year he introduced the "American AI Sovereign Wealth Fund Act"—a one-time 50% stock tax on large AI companies, creating a fund of about $7 trillion that would pay every American more than $1,000 a year in dividends—and now the 20-year imprisonment clause. Other Senate leadership has also kept pressing large tech companies, accusing them of misleading the public on what benefits AI brings to communities.

This line clearly runs counter to the White House's direction. The Trump administration views AI as a key area of competition with China and recently hosted several top AI executives at the White House, who pledged "self-regulation" of AI development. Trump later said AI has a "branding problem" and on September 29 signed an executive order requiring federal executive departments and agencies to replace "Artificial Intelligence" and "AI" with "Super Intelligence" and the abbreviation "SI." The timing was six days after this bill was introduced—in the same week, congressional Democrats wanted to ban "superintelligence," while the White House renamed all artificial intelligence "superintelligence."

Vague Definition of "Superintelligence" Becomes Core Dispute

The most questioned aspect of the bill is that its core concept lacks an operable technical definition. A commentary by AInvest hit the crux: the object proposed to be banned "has no accepted technical definition," which is not a semantic footnote but the core of the entire trade. The US Treasury Department previously noted explicitly in its outbound investment restriction proposal that the relevant term "should not be read to imply a frontier or superhuman capability threshold."

However, within the bill's three-tier design, the degree of ambiguity is not uniform. "Advanced AI system" has a clear numerical boundary; "superintelligence precursor characteristics" is a list of six specific capabilities that, while still subject to regulatory determination, at least enumerates observable behaviors; what truly lacks a quantitative definition is "superintelligence" itself. In other words, the object being paused has a compute threshold to rely on, while the object being permanently banned does not. If "superintelligence" cannot be quantified into a testable threshold, enforcement of this part will depend heavily on regulatory discretion rather than a clear legal boundary. For AI asset valuations, this ambiguity itself constitutes a risk premium: investors cannot precisely price a ban with an unclear boundary and can only discount "regulatory uncertainty" as a whole.

How Other Jurisdictions Draw the Line

Using computing power as a threshold is not a US first. Article 51 of the EU AI Act uses 10^25 FLOP as the line for presuming "systemic risk"; general-purpose models crossing that line must additionally bear obligations such as model evaluation, systemic risk assessment, major incident reporting, and cybersecurity—landing at exactly the same order of magnitude as this US bill's pause threshold.

Japan has taken the opposite direction. The Act on Promotion of Research and Development and Utilization of AI-Related Technologies (AI Act), promulgated on June 4, 2025, and fully effective on September 1, uses the word "promotion" in its title to signal its orientation: aimed at promoting R&D and utilization, with no compute threshold and no penalties. The same frontier model might be required to pause training in the US, while in Japan the policy tone remains one of encouraging innovation.

Market and Industry Impact

For investors, the short-term direct impact of this legislative offensive is limited—the bill remains at the submission stage and still needs committee review, floor votes in both chambers, and presidential signature before becoming law. But what the midterm elections changed is the priority of the political agenda: with Democrats controlling both chambers, control over committee chairmanships and hearing scheduling shifts accordingly, and AI regulatory issues previously shelved by Republicans could enter substantive review.

More noteworthy is the chain reaction at the valuation level. When the object of "prohibition" lacks a technical definition, regulators' discretion becomes an unpriced variable, and frontier model developers, compute suppliers, and data center operators could all fall within potential scope. At the same time, during the midterm elections, multiple local referendums involved AI data center issues, with most voters casting opposing votes, reflecting public resistance to high-energy-consumption facilities, driven by practical concerns such as rising electricity bills and noise pollution rather than AI technology itself.

The White House has also noticed the unpopularity of data centers, requiring hyperscale cloud service providers, utilities, and even governors to sign an "Electricity Billpayer Protection Pledge" and requiring AI data centers to pay energy dividends to local communities. But judging from the election results, most voters considered these remedial measures insufficient and too late.

Whether the bill can complete the legislative process remains uncertain, but Democrats have demonstrated through action that AI regulation will be one of the core issues of their new Congress. For Silicon Valley, the real risk may not be any specific clause, but that "regulatory uncertainty" itself is beginning to be factored into valuation models.
