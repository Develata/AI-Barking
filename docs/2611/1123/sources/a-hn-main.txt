> VP=VNP implies P=NP, but this result doesn't imply P != NP.
For those unfamiliar with the subject, a constructive proof of P=NP would be a very weird universe, because it would essentially allow fully automated discovery of proofs for any theorem that has a polynomial-time checkable proof. (And almost anything you'd call a "proof" would be checkable in polynomial time. So basically, fully automated mathematical discovery.)
See the discussion of P and NP by Scott Aaronson part way down this page : https://www.scottaaronson.com/democritus/lec9.html [https://www.scottaaronson.com/democritus/lec9.html]
So if your AI proves P!=NP, your AI is very clever. (There several different proofs that rule out common proof techniques being able to prove P!=NP.) But if your AI instead proves P=NP, the future just got weird on a level that's hard to even describe. Cracking cryptography wide open is the least weird thing you should expect.

Here's some speculative writing:
If P=NP, then once you know what the desired solution looks like, your solution isn't far away.
"Ask, and it shall be given you; seek, and ye shall find; knock, and it shall be opened unto you" -- Matthew 7:7
In fact, once you realize the "trouble" with NP problems is that they're discrete and combinatorial, meaning that there's no general way to "get closer" to a solution, unlike more continuous problems where you can simply look at the slopes and find a (local) minima easily -- these days we call this gradient descent.
AI breakthroughs in the past decade were in a large part due to the realization that gradient descent goes a very long way if you get enough data and compute.
If P=NP then perhaps even combinatoric problems would get a similar boost.

cwillu [user?id=cwillu] 16 hours ago [item?id=50620540] | root [#50620360] | parent [#50620442] | next [#50620702] [–] [javascript:void(0)]

There are significant no-go theorems about what a proof of P≠NP can look like, such that we didn't (at least as of a few years ago) know what a proof would even look like: all the known techniques were proven to be insufficient; generally in forms like “if this technique worked to prove P≠NP, then the technique could also be used as part of an algorithm to solve NP-hard instances in P time.” See Relativization and Algebrization.
As such, any proof would necessarily involve new breakthroughs rather than merely the exhaustive application of existing techniques.

The interesting thing about both the halting problem and the continuum hypothesis is that they involve infinity.
The halting problem is trivially (but not necessarily efficiently) computable if you constrain the evaluated program to any specific finite amount of memory, because then a failure to halt would have to repeat a previous state. To solve it the evaluating program needs to keep more state than the evaluated program, which makes it incomputable when the evaluated program is allowed infinite state.
Which is kind of weird, because that makes it computable for any finite amount of state no matter how large, but not for an infinite amount.
Meanwhile the speed of light and Planck units seem to imply that physical reality can't deal with infinity either.

Interestingly, the independence of the continuum hypothesis from ZFC is provable in a weak fragment of arithmetic (as an implication of the consistency of ZFC). PRA suffices, and PV_1 probably works too.
(To see why, you're basically asking if a computer program that searches all proofs of ZFC for a proof of either CH or ¬CH and halts in either case ever indeed actually halts. The non-halting proof ultimately requires a fairly weak arithmetic theory even if the standard proof is typically presented at a higher level of abstraction using forcing).
We've gotten lucky so far that many independence theorems are themselves provable in relatively weak theories. This is by no means guaranteed however, and it's entirely possible we stumble upon a problem at some point for which the only justification of its independence from known theories is a new principle that hopefully appears intuitively obvious enough to accept.
Not saying P vs NP falls in this category (that would be really surprising) but it's certainly not impossible.

This is imo a bad analogy because it also doesn't understand the question at all, and instead just autocompletes "How many Rs does strawberry have?" to follow up with a random number, because the pattern "How many <X> does <Y> have?" occurs thousands to millions of times on the internet, but probably very rarely with exactly strawberry & R.
If it had any "understanding" of the question, it would simply write a python script to answer it, because someone who only sees Chinese characters, could solve the problem in this way. And as soon as reasoning models came around, they started doing exactly that.
I also think it's incorrect that people started assuming that tokens are the (only) deciding factor here. I mean, yes, if you have a network that tokenizes words at the letter-level, there is a good chance it can learn a "kernel" that allows it to count letters in sentences to some degree, but early LLMs were extremely bad at counting too. It would get enough training examples to get the strawberry, but very likely fail at longer letter counts all the same (say a word with 82 Rs), because it wouldn't actually be adding letter counts together, it would just be hardcoding the counts for small numbers. Proper counting is something that came with reasoning models.
So tokens are definitely one of the issues, but lack of counting capabilities + a self-referential question are just as large factors.

Chess is a very narrowly-bounded problem, and mathematics is vast. But you're right: We really have no idea.
Thing is, even if AI starts solving the hardest problems and determines for itself what the right problems to solve are... math, like chess, is still a human activity.
Humans watch other humans play chess, and using stockfish in competition is the lowest form of cheating. Chess is more popular than ever, because it's more a human sport than a problem to be solved.
In an analogous way, I'd suggest that math is, traditionally, a form of pedagogical activity. The future will always need people who interpret and explain the work of LLMs in human terms, as a form of mental training, and as a means of furthering human understanding -- just as the study of philosophy is a form of mental training.
Industrial math (generating useful results for engineering or cryptography) is absolutely certain to become AI-dominated in the very near future, but math as a discipline for building human mental models and advancing our understanding of the world isn't going away any time soon.

If you believe that the only worth of studying math is to solve problems which can be stated inside a formal proof system such as Lean, then maybe? Even if that were the case, coming up with proofs and formally verifying them is still limited by compute.
There is definitely value in knowing which problems to pursue, which is also what people who are steering these agents are doing (and they probably studied math).
Even if LLMs know every tool and are faster at using it than humans, you still require humans to communicate the task they are trying to solve (at least for the moment). Studying math allows you to know which tools make sense to use or how to properly frame a problem you are trying to solve. This is particularly relevant for applying math in real world contexts. Real world problems are generally not as well defined and you can only simulate up to a certain precision.
Math knowledge and skills transfer well to other/related domains, including (maybe ironically) the advancement of AI models. I don't claim I can predict a math degree's economic "value" in the future, but studying is about much more than getting a degree. I would say there is a lot of uncertainty for research in this field at the moment (see Masullo rushing out his draft for the RH). Anyone can use agents to work on a problem, but how far you get might solely depend on how many resources you have.

Its only an intermediate result if it proves to be so. This is more characterizing complexity of permanent (#P) relative to fields of characteristic != 2. Maybe useful? Maybe not. After all, the Riemann Hypothesis over finite fields was proved in the 1940s. You don't got it till you got it. And what people want is NP, not circuit complexity #P (permanent).
As someone who previously worked in this field, I think this is the kind of paper that would be published in STOC or FOCS, and ultimately be read by < 10 people. Most of them grad students in CS complexity looking for a thesis topic; and I suspect that there are few of those left or going forward now.
It would have to be a pretty brave/foolhardy grad student who would pick this up thinking "hey, I'll take the next step here that unreleased-advanced Claude couldn't take". Seems like it kinda deadends the approach, except for those who have already invested decades in this direction ... the very few.
So, unless the next step is obvious to a long-time researcher, this has probably placed a stop on future progress in this direction for the near term. Except maybe by increments, like with multiplications or other recent minor tweaks to exponents.
But that's probably cool. We've struggled along with Shoenhage-Strassen multiplication for decades and still nobody ever uses it because its impractical. So there's that silver lining: that this is more for the understanding than for any practical progress. And for the understanding, one needs understandable proofs/documents. Cuz who is ever really computing permanents, eh?
