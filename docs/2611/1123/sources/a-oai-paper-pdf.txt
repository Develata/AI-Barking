                            VP and VNP are diﬀerent
                                             OpenAI


                                             Abstract
        We prove that VP diﬀers from VNP over every ﬁeld. The lower bound holds for border
    circuits and is superpolynomial, of the form exp(Ω((log n)5/4 )). In particular it applies to
    the permanent outside characteristic two. The proof trades two kinds of hardness against
    one another: the rigidity of tensor networks priced in Euclidean space, and the ability to
    diagonalize even against degenerating nonuniform circuits by replaying their computations
    inside a wire budget.

1    Introduction
A polynomial can have an enormous number of terms and still be easy to compute. But
suppose that the only compression we know is the following one: the terms (with repetitions
allowed) can be described locally, or checked quickly given an index. Does this suﬃce?
    Valiant’s completeness theory [76, 78] gave us a formal version of this question and some
of the most arresting concrete versions as well. VP over a ﬁeld consists of families of mul-
tivariate polynomials of polynomial degree in polynomially many variables, computable by
polynomial size arithmetic circuits; VNP allows, on top, summing over all settings of polyno-
mially many auxiliary inputs to zero and one. Here and throughout, size and degree bounds for
classes are polynomial in the family index, circuits are nonuniform, computing with free ﬁeld
constants and binary additions, subtractions and multiplications, and the computation is of
formal polynomials. No assumptions are made about degrees along the way. For background
and formulations see e.g. [13, 10, 12, 71, 15].
    We give a negative answer. In fact we prove the geometric strengthening (cf. [11]): VNP
is not contained even in border VP. By this we mean that we may take coeﬃcient-wise limits
of circuits, in the sense of Zariski closure over the algebraic closure. To give the result a
quantitative form, take the family

Proof. When only the degree in question is approximated, ﬁrst homogenize to obtain exactly
that degree, at polynomial cost in the size and degree. Polynomial projections preserve border
upper bounds by continuity. Bounds at polynomially sampled dimensions give bounds of this
form at all large dimensions by standard padding (to force extra HC vertices subdivide a vertex
through a mandatory chain). Determinant has polynomial size over every ﬁeld [50]. For orbit
closure (aﬃne cone, allowing extra ambient variables if needed), use that the orbit consists of
determinants of linear matrices and that padding by a power of a new variable can be undone by
specialization. Even padding by an existing variable works: split into components by degree in
that variable, discard too-low degrees and undo the shift, a linear operation costing polynomial
overhead.

Where this sits. We mean separation at the polynomial scale; there are harder quasipoly-
nomial questions (notice the formulation already in [76]; see also [54]), closely connected to
formula complexity by depth reduction [35, 79]. Nor is this a separation for decision prob-
lems in the Boolean model. But even the polynomial determinantal projection question by
itself has long been a benchmark open problem (e.g. [80]), and in orbit-closure form central
to geometric complexity theory [53, 54, 15, 14]. We give no explicit module of equations or
representation-theoretic obstruction; occurrence obstructions are known not to work for stan-
dard GCT with large polynomial padding [14]. Previous advances on determinantal lower
bounds include quadratic bounds [52, 16, 46] and, in recent preprints, cubic border bounds
[59, 69] (see also the polar methods of [70] and [45]). For unrestricted circuits think of the
n log d scale: gradient and degree give such lower bounds even on simple degree-d polynomials
in n variables [4, 73]; see also the recent treatment and permanent results in [61].
    There is a magniﬁcent history of large bounds after changing the problem slightly. Mono-
tonicity can force exponential bounds [77, 36]; symmetry too can come at a steep price [22].
The multilinear world has superpolynomial bounds on formulas even for determinant [66].
Ranks of partial derivative matrices and relatives [55, 56, 19] are ubiquitous; but circuits give
us low ranks across varying cuts, which is quite diﬀerent from a single matrix (cf. the discrep-
ancy and partition issues in [65, 68, 2, 23, 18]). There is the depth-four program [1, 43, 74]
with formidable progress via shifted partials and beyond, attaining even the square-root scale
in the exponent for homogeneous depth four, but with constants critical for unrestricted cir-
cuit implications [38, 29, 40, 39, 44]; see [60] for recent progress at depth ﬁve. And there are
now even superpolynomial bounds at arbitrary constant depth [49, 6, 24]. For more perspec-
tives, including the power and possible limitations of proofs by low-complexity equations, see
[71, 41, 26, 27, 25]. Here the approach will be diﬀerent, although varying cuts play an essential
role.

Strategy. Call a binary index in a tensor contraction a wire. Imagine placing the tensors in
Euclidean space, and charging length for each wire. We isolate a strict shortening hypothesis.
Informally: is there some dimension and some ﬁxed local tensor identity that communicates
along bundles of orthogonal diameters more cheaply than literally laying the wires? We can
allow alternatives (send a selector from the center to the terminals and sum).

  • Without strict shortening, there is a direct, short proof. On a graph with independent
    bundles along the edges, any balanced cut saving enough rank would allow wire to be
    saved: reroute through a hub displaced a little in the direction of the resultant of the cut


                                               3
       wires. Even cuts chosen diﬀerently in diﬀerent terms would work. This leads quickly to
       Theorem 1.1.

    • With strict shortening, we can save wire in computations. The point of saving is to diag-
      onalize. Think of a coeﬃcient of fn as a programmable processor. Against a description
      of a circuit, compute this very coeﬃcient, plus one. Now that sounds like diagonalization
      against a linear bound at best. Instead take a contraction with very many programmed
      processors in it; in its live execution it must be able to replay this entire contraction with
      the hypothetical small circuit substituted for every processor. It gets some important
      economies. First, the same circuit services all the queries: it is possible to batch, charging
      essentially only degree per query since we compute by counting and may follow just one
      parse tree. (For other kinds of batching see e.g. [75, 81].) And second, this computation
      can itself be performed as a contraction, with RAM transcripts guessed and checked by
      sorting. So we can keep shortening its wires so as to ﬁt the processors’ communication
      budget.

    • There are real diﬃculties with this program. Wire saving in big networks must be made
      to iterate (compare gap ampliﬁcation in undirected network coding [8], though here our
      alphabet may be contracted by summation and we even sum over wirings). We build
      ribbon-like switching hosts: locally Euclidean along many axes, with vast routing room;
      the cost accounting will pay even for concentration of traﬃc. We will also need a ﬁnal
      constant-factor squeeze, adapting the grid method of the recent [20] on counting SAT
      (cf. also [42, 62]). And how can one learn, let alone distribute, a circuit using arbitrary
      constants, possibly only working in a limit? We avoid that: after collecting powers of all
      circuit parameters, insert certain universal weights. We realize the limit as an eigenvector
      law for operators whose entries can be replayed by counting with very modest advice.
      Cartier operators in positive characteristic are key to this step, even if we began in
      characteristic zero.

    Thus the proof is not just a rigidity argument and not just an algorithmic lower bound
(for a diﬀerent use of algorithms and diagonalization in lower bounds see [82]): if geometry
fails to prove hardness directly, its failure powers a hierarchy theorem. We organize the proof
around that hinge. After making shortening precise and giving the direct bound, we state and
reduce the contrasting stretch theorem (§3)—stretch because a single ﬁxed saving will boost
the lower bound to stretched exponential. Its reductions go to a boundary lemma (§4) and
a replay lemma; the rest of the core argument constructs the replay. One can also proceed
directly to that construction (using the one-dimensional, exact version of the boundary lemma
for motivation), returning to §4 at the end.


2     The hinge
A bit tensor network has arbitrary local tables. Joining ports sums over a shared bit; multiply
all local entries and sum over assignments (external ports left as arguments). A width-b cable
joins b pairs of ports. Place nodes in a metric space: charge width times distance for each cable,
including any run up to an external terminal. Thus local reblocking or other computation at
the same point costs nothing.

    Here is how we actually use one. For j juxtaposed crossings, sum over proﬁles (j1 , . . . , jA )
of multiplicities, one layout for each. Within a layout, send a word of j selectors (constrained

with widths proportional to ja , not needing to see the selectors. This realizes the tensor exactly,
each layout saving a ﬁxed fraction of length, and with a bounded number of cable descriptions
(widths O(j)). Local operations are word parsing, reblocking, and applying constant tables.

4.1   A prime of modest size
We recall three geometric degree bounds, using the degree of the projective closure in the aﬃne
case (see [30, Ch. I, §7] and [31, Lemma 2 and Proposition 3]). A linear image of an irreducible
variety has closure of degree at most the degree of the variety. More generally, the closure of
an image of an open subset of Ah under a rational map with a common denominator and all
degrees at most e ≥ 1 has degree at most eh . Indeed a general linear section of complementary
dimension meets the image closure in its attained locus in as many distinct points as its degree.
The pullbacks must have at least one component per such point. Bézout bounds this number,
including when a pullback intersection has components of several dimensions: in a cut of degree


                                                7
at most e the sum of (deg V )edim V over components cannot increase. In particular, successive
linear sections never increase the total degree.
    Finally, if a point is outside an irreducible closed set of degree D and dimension b, an
equation of degree at most D separates them. Indeed, project the projective closure to Pb+1
from a linear center disjoint from its join with the point. The image is a hypersurface (a
positive-dimensional projective ﬁber would meet the center), and misses the projected point.
Its equation pulls back to one as asserted. In codimension one use the hypersurface equation
itself. These arguments work over any algebraically closed ﬁeld. For a reducible set one can
multiply the separating equations of its components.

6    Architecture of replay
We turn to Lemma 3.2. This section describes the two executions that will give the two sides
of the replay identity. The subsequent sections meet the budget.

The big network. The layout deﬁning the row F will have teams of r processors each (a
processor has n bit ports and is to have table cx ). There are R teams, a very small power of
n. Each processor has literal ﬁelds hardwired, and we can plug a pin string in, distributed
over the ﬁrst team. We use all processors, with unused ports forced. Every two teams have
a predetermined bundle of exterior links, evenly distributed among processors (ports equal at
opposite ends). In its live execution the network runs fn programs, i.e. local counting programs
with their codes literally in the x ﬁelds. Together one team can perform long nondeterministic
random access computations (RAM), taking the literals, pin ﬁelds and exterior links as initial
data: we spend some interior ports within a team on the RAM simulation.
    Formally, for the coordinate of the row with a given pin, put that pin in the board. F is
just the contraction with symbolic table (slots indexed by x, other coeﬃcient slots ignored) at
every processor. Regardless of what we will program live, there is also the primed execution:
instead of cx put the same coordinate of Y (X) at every processor. Now the literals are just
data specifying slots. This execution gives F (Y (X)). Thus for instance the primed network
sums over interior RAM wires with no demand that they be legal RAM transcripts.

Low degree application. To indicate the ﬂexibility of the witness, even clique polynomials
can show such bounds with only polylogarithmic degree. In each ﬁxed characteristic take
ordinary clique (monomial is product of edge variables) on N vertices, clique size dlogA N e for
a suﬃciently large constant A. This is in VNP, and has border complexity exp(Ω((log N )5/4 )).
To see this, recall the elementary projection: encode a sum over compatible choices at k
sites (pairwise constraints, unary weights) by one part of vertices per site plus a singleton for
attaching all unary weights by edges. Force selection of one per part by zero within parts;
extra singleton parts pad the clique size. Use constants for compatibility. In the no-shortening
proof apply this directly to the partition polynomial g with n taken a small ﬁxed power of
N (the same balanced-cut proof bounds g itself). With shortening, take n a power of log N
large enough for the stretch theorem to suﬃce; encode the tableau sum for fn . For example,
sites represent Boolean values and also satisfying tuples for individual local constraints, edges
matching constraint arguments to their values; attach variable weights at the x sites. This
uses only polynomially many sites and vertices in n, so ﬁts with A large. As before projection
after homogenization suﬃces even for degree-component border lower bounds. This growing-
parameter conclusion is to be distinguished from hardness in parameterized models (cf. [7]).
