Claude Claims Proof That VP ≠ VNP, the Algebraic Cousin of P vs NP. Here’s What It Means

 November 24, 2026November 24, 2026 [https://officechai.com/ai/claude-vp-vnp-proof-valiant-conjecture-p-vs-np/] OfficeChai Team [https://officechai.com/author/admin/]

Anthropic might have just been getting started with its Riemann Hypothesis proof [https://officechai.com/ai/claimed-riemann-hypothesis-proof-made-with-claude-appears-on-anthropic-mathematician-levent-alpoges-website/].

Anthropic has published what may be the most significant result in theoretical computer science yet credited to an AI. In a new folder of its formal-math repository on GitHub [https://github.com/anthropics/formal-math/tree/main/vp-vnp], the company has posted a 70-page paper titled “VP ≠ VNP over every field”. The paper claims to settle Valiant’s conjecture, a 47-year-old problem often described as the algebraic version of P vs NP.

The paper lists one author: “Claude (Anthropic)”. According to the repository, an internal research Claude model found the result. AI models wrote both the paper and a machine-checkable proof in the Lean programming language, which runs to roughly 73,000 lines across 324 files. Anthropic is also upfront that no human mathematician has checked the proof yet.

VP vs VNP, in plain language

Picture a calculator with only two operation buttons, add and multiply. A sequence of button presses that turns some input numbers into a final answer is called an arithmetic circuit, and its size is the number of steps it takes. There is one important condition: the circuit has to produce the exact formula and work for any numbers you feed it, not just for convenient ones.

Now take a square grid of numbers and two classic formulas built from it. The determinant, familiar from school linear algebra, picks one entry from each row and column in every possible way, multiplies each selection together, gives each product a plus or minus sign, and adds everything up. The permanent does exactly the same thing, except that every sign is a plus. Both formulas add up an astronomical number of terms: about 2.4 quintillion for a 20 × 20 grid. Yet the determinant has a clever shortcut that needs only around m³ steps for an m × m grid, while the best known methods for the permanent still need exponentially many.

In 1979, Harvard computer scientist Leslie Valiant turned this puzzle into two classes. VP contains formulas that are easy to compute with a small circuit, such as the determinant. VNP contains formulas that are easy to describe term by term, even when there are exponentially many terms, such as the permanent. Valiant showed that the permanent is the hardest formula in VNP. His conjecture, VP ≠ VNP, therefore asks a simple question: is the permanent truly harder than the determinant, or has nobody found the trick yet?

Claude’s paper says the trick does not exist. It proves that any such circuit for an m × m permanent needs more than m^(c·(log m)^(1/3)) steps once m is large. That number eventually outgrows every fixed power of m. The bound holds over every field except those of characteristic 2, where the permanent and determinant turn out to be the same formula. For the closely related Hamiltonian cycle polynomial, it holds over every field. The best previous lower bound for unrestricted circuits, dating to work by Volker Strassen and Walter Baur in the 1970s and 80s, was barely more than quadratic. Going from there to a superpolynomial bound is a leap the field had not managed in four decades.

How it connects to P vs NP

P vs NP is one of the seven Millennium Prize Problems and carries a $1 million reward. It asks whether every problem whose solution is quick to check, like a filled-in Sudoku, is also quick to solve. Valiant designed VP and VNP to mirror P and NP: VP plays the role of “easy to solve”, and VNP plays the role of “easy to verify”.

The repository is explicit that the new result “does not settle P vs NP,” and the reason is subtle. The chips in ordinary computers only need to give the right answer when every input is 0 or 1, and over those two values many different formulas look identical. A Boolean circuit can therefore get away with computing some easier formula that happens to agree with the permanent on 0s and 1s. An arithmetic circuit cannot take that shortcut. The paper identifies the two steps of its argument that break once circuits are allowed this freedom.

The known logical links also run in only one direction. Proving VP = VNP would have implied a version of P = NP. A strong proof of P ≠ NP would very likely have implied VP ≠ VNP, which is why researchers long treated Valiant’s conjecture as the easier sibling and a natural stepping stone. Settling it is real progress, but nobody yet knows a path from here to the Clay prize.

The timing adds an irony. One month ago, OpenAI said its model had shown the Exponential Time Hypothesis to be false [https://officechai.com/ai/new-openai-research-says-exponential-time-hypothesis-eth-is-false/], and hours later Anthropic published Claude’s own independent refutation [https://officechai.com/ai/anthropic-says-claude-too-independently-disproved-exponential-time-hypothesis-on-the-same-day-as-openai/]. That result was a sign that a classic hard Boolean problem may be slightly less hard than assumed. VP ≠ VNP points the other way for algebra, confirming that the hard problem there really is hard.

Can it be trusted?

Lean guarantees that every logical step has been checked by a computer. In this case the proof was checked twice, by Lean’s own kernel and by an independently written checker called nanoda, using only Lean’s standard axioms. What Lean cannot guarantee is that the formal statement says what mathematicians mean. If the 98-line file that defines circuits, VP and VNP differed subtly from the standard definitions, Lean would happily certify the wrong theorem. The paper admits that this match was judged only by AI readers so far. That leaves experts a far smaller job than refereeing 70 pages, but until they have done it, this is best described as a claimed, machine-checked proof rather than an accepted theorem.

What it means

For businesses, nothing changes directly. No encryption scheme depends on VP ≠ VNP, and computing permanents remains as hard in practice as it was before. The significance lies elsewhere: in what this says about how quickly AI research capability is advancing.

The past five months show the pattern. In July an Anthropic mathematician said Claude Fable 5 helped him disprove the Jacobian conjecture [https://officechai.com/ai/an-anthropic-researcher-says-fable-just-helped-him-disprove-the-85-year-old-jacobian-conjecture/], open since 1939. In September Claude spent 11 days autonomously producing the first computer-checked proof of Fermat’s Last Theorem [https://officechai.com/ai/claude-works-autonomously-for-11-days-to-generate-first-computer-checked-proof-of-fermats-last-theorem/]. OpenAI has released a large batch of results that includes proofs for 23 Erdős problems [https://officechai.com/ai/openai-has-also-included-proofs-for-23-erdos-problems-in-its-latest-math-proof-drop/], and it leads trackers of AI-solved problems, with Anthropic second [https://officechai.com/ai/openai-has-solved-234-math-problems-anthropic-follows-with-57/]. Last week Anthropic mathematician Levent Alpöge posted a claimed proof of the Riemann Hypothesis made with Claude [https://officechai.com/stories/levent-alpoge-riemann-hypothesis/], prompting even Sundar Pichai to react [https://officechai.com/ai/somewhere-euler-gauss-and-riemann-in-awe-today-google-ceo-sundar-pichai-reacts-to-riemann-hypothesis-proof/]. Mathematics has become the arena where frontier labs compete, and the contest is now about how important the results are, not just how many.

The bigger lesson may be about verification. AI now produces results faster than human experts can absorb them; one mathematician argued that no single living mathematician may be able to follow all of the Riemann proof [https://officechai.com/ai/theres-likely-no-single-mathematician-with-the-breadth-of-expertise-needed-to-understand-all-of-alpoges-riemann-proof-mathematician-abhishek-saha/]. Formal checking changes the question from “did someone follow every step?” to “does the short statement say the right thing?”. Companies deploying AI-written code and analysis face the same problem of output outgrowing review capacity, and machine-checked guarantees are one of the few approaches that scale with it.

There is also the question of credit. The Riemann and Jacobian results came with a named human mathematician, but this paper names no human author at all. Journals, prize committees and universities have yet to decide how to handle that. For now, the next step belongs to people: specialists in algebraic complexity must confirm that the statement is correct and then work out why the proof works.
