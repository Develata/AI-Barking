Why read proofs anymore? [https://blog.computationalcomplexity.org/2026/11/why-read-proofs-anymore.html]

Loosely based on an actual dinner conversation between Lance and Bill in DC ten days ago and updated with recent announcements because the math world moves too quickly.

LANCE: We have all these wonderful new theorems assisted or proven by AI, but I just can't get myself excited to read the proofs. 

BILL: You haven't read the proof of the Riemann hypothesis [https://www.nytimes.com/2026/11/20/science/anthropic-ai-math-riemann-hypothesis.html?unlocked_article_code=1.SFE.pbig.hZOCsdx8f2vM&smid=url-share] yet? 

LANCE: Do you mean Levent Alpöge's 17-million-line Lean proof [https://math.mit.edu/~drew/grh/], or Alessandro Masullo's not-ready-for-prime-time paper [https://math216.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/the-music-of-the-primes.pdf]? No. Did you?

BILL: Much like the last Harry Potter book, I'll wait for the movie. I am more impressed with Alpöge's proof that primes are infinite that uses Ramsey Theory [https://doi.org/10.4169/amer.math.monthly.122.8.784] than his contribution to TWO millennium problems.

LANCE: Of more interest to me, on Friday William Hoza dropped a new paper [https://eccc.weizmann.ac.il/report/2026/323/] showing NL = UL. He showed how, in log-space, to compute the advice used by Reinhardt and Allender [https://doi.org/10.1137/S0097539798339041] in their NL in UL/poly paper to isolate a path in a directed graph.

BILL: So you read that proof?

LANCE: I skimmed it.

BILL: Don't you want to know if the theorems are correct?

LANCE: They are mostly Lean-verified. And if they aren't, I'm not the one to thoroughly check all the details.

BILL: So why does anyone read proofs?

LANCE: Mostly people read proofs to learn techniques to prove new theorems. Maybe it's a theorem that I once worked on and I'd like to see how it was proved. Or sometimes the proof is just so beautiful, a "proof from the Book", it's like a piece of art. AI might make a good mathematician but a great artist it is not.

BILL: So don't you want to read it to prove new theorems? 

LANCE: If you have a conjecture for a related result, you could just ask ChatGPT to prove it for you based on the old proof. 

BILL: To help me come up with questions and projects for students.

LANCE: You don't need the proofs for that. Just the statements of the results. And AI may soon be able to come up with questions and projects for you.

BILL: For entertainment. Like the opera you came to see [https://washnatopera.org/nixon-in-china].

LANCE: Would you find reading the proof of the Unique Games Conjecture entertaining?

BILL: Probably not.

LANCE: I'll wait for Unique Games: The Opera. Or you can watch the movie [https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=de-CszI9YXc]. So again, why do you read proofs?

BILL: For my own personal enlightenment. Here is an analog: I recently read Distant Friends and Intimate Enemies: A History of American-Russian Relations [https://www.amazon.com/dp/0521111056?lv=shuf&channelId=500&plpRedirect=mhFallback&linkCode=ll2&tag=computation09-20&linkId=cd69084a173c8fb6d6ab03a356c94deb&language=en_US&gaOptInStatus=true&ref_=as_li_ss_tl]. 

LANCE: Sounds like fun.

BILL: It was enlightening and makes me better informed on history and politics even though I am not going to get a paper out on the topic.

LANCE: But you can discuss history and politics with a lot of people. I don't think you can discuss the 3SUM algorithm [https://blog.computationalcomplexity.org/2026/11/3sum-and-apsp-and-eth-oh-my.html] with a lot of people.

BILL: I tried telling Darling about it but she laughed when I told her that \(n^{1.9992}\) is a lot better than \(n^2\).

LANCE: Did you tell her that we can stop trying to prove it requires \(\Omega(n^2)\) time?

BILL: She was not impressed.

LANCE: So should we still read proofs? Or will it go the way of the slide rule?

BILL: Children should still know their times table up through eight even though they can use calculators. Similarly, we should still know how to prove simple things.

LANCE: Agreed. And maybe a few tougher things as well.

BILL: Take us out, Lance.

LANCE: And remember, in a complex world, best to keep it simple.

How Will We Judge? [https://blog.computationalcomplexity.org/2026/11/how-will-we-judge.html]

I hate that we have to judge theorists but departments need to make decisions about PhD admission, faculty hiring, tenure and promotion. Artificial intelligence adds new wrinkles to the process. AI makes it easier to prove theorems and write up papers, certainly leading to more papers. And how do we judge the role that an author plays in an AI-assisted or AI-generated paper? And are we rewarding good prompting and access to the pricier AI models?

These issues go back before the AI age. As our field has become more metric driven, we've seen a significant increase in the number of research papers from undergrads, grad students and young professors, often at the least publishable unit, just good enough to get accepted at various conferences. Bill told me about a quote he heard, "Deans can't read but they can count." Too often even our CS colleagues will count papers as well.

When I was department chair, I had faculty candidates upload a single paper with their submission. That gave us a single paper to read, though I'm not sure it always got read.

Since our papers list authors alphabetically instead of by contribution, we already had challenges sussing out the role an individual author played in the paper. With AI papers we often get an AI disclosure, but once we use that for judging a theorist, people will write those disclosures carefully, maybe in a misleading way or worse.

Here we could rely on recommendation letters. In computer science, letter writers tend to write the most positive letter without lying, so to read a letter properly you need to reverse that process. But now that many of these letters seem to be AI generated themselves, that muddles the information we get.

How do real companies deal with hiring? A company might wait until it gets a better feel for just what roles AI will play in research and teaching, but academic departments tend to fill slots because they may not have them next year.

Many companies use AI to at least give a first pass through the candidates, some even doing the first interview. We could do the same. AI would certainly do a more thorough job reading candidates' statements, papers and recommendation letters than we do, especially for those applying from universities we know less about. An AI interview could check how well a candidate understands their research and the role they played. But I suspect CS departments would feel queasy about offloading these processes.

A group of theory faculty, responding to a Simons Institute working group report, put out a statement [https://njbergam.github.io/tcs-hiring-statement/] about hiring for the AI age. You could do worse than following these guidelines.

Emphasize quality over quantity. In the evaluations of candidates, consider at most three works from July 2026 onwards (of the candidates’ choosing), in addition to any number of prior works before July 2026.

Actively assess thorough understanding. Evaluate the thoroughness of the candidates’ understanding of the aforementioned works more than was done normally until now. This evaluation could take the form of a “chalk talk,” more mathematically substantial interview slots, and/or other methods of the department’s choosing.

Why did it take the AI era for us to finally put quality over quantity?

A More Impressive Use of AI than Proving the Unique Game Conjecture [https://blog.computationalcomplexity.org/2026/11/a-more-impressive-use-of-ai-then.html]

 I have a website devoted to parodies of the song

 I am the very model of a modern major general 

here [https://www.cs.umd.edu/~gasarch/FUN/modmajgen.html]. So I sometimes look around the web for more. I recently came across

 We are the very models of the throwback starfleet criminals

here [https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=j-tEHZDVLo8]. The tune isn't really the tune of I am...general but it's very impressive, especially to someone over 60 who both knows Star Trek and does not know what technology can do. 

a) It is NOT sung by fans in costume. (More on that later.) It's actually sung by the actors who played those roles, in the costumes they wore for the show. What's really impressive is that all of the actors are dead.

b) The images looked just like the characters, and the voices sounded just like the characters.

c) I assume AI was involved. How much? Did AI write the song? Do I care so long as it's funny?

d) My main point: this would have been impossible X years ago for some value of X (readers--what do you think X is?) and hence I am impressed. How old do you have to be to keep being impressed by these things? And when will it stop being impressive? Lance had a blog post about impressive visual effects in movies becoming standard and hence not impressive, see here [https://blog.computationalcomplexity.org/2024/06/the-godzilla-moment.html].

e) It was really very good! Of course, as a fan of novelty songs, Gilbert and Sullivan, and Star Trek, I am predisposed to like it.

f) To understand the entire song you need to have watched three specific episodes recently. This reminds me of a prior post see here [https://blog.computationalcomplexity.org/search?q=song+about+bill] about technology making it possible to produce good songs about niche topics. 

After seeing that video, I looked around for more Star Trek songs. I found quite a few, here are my favorites:

------------------------------

A song about Dr. McCoy to the tune of The Devil went down to Georgia.The song's title is I'm a doctor not a ... It's here [//www.youtube.com/watch?v=AsX7dPktG88]

A song about Miles O'Brien (ST:DSN), to the tune of I'm Gonna Be (500 miles). The song's title is I would fix 500 faults. It's here [https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vb1pfLPBkRM]. I'm impressed with the video and with anyone who can identify every episode it refers to. 

A song about Picard trying to talk to a Tamarian, a species that only talks in metaphor, to the tune of Walk like an Egyptian. The song's title is Talk like a Tamarian. It's here [https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jXIgyaYy7BI]. It's based on one episode, Darmok. That's niche!

A song about the episode of Shore Leave from ST-TOS, to the tune of Life in Wartime. The song's title is Life During Shore Team. It's here [https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fOECJM_jGLw]

----------------------------------

So, AI is producing really good star trek music videos with the actors from the show singing and playing instruments (I know it's not really them but it the images are just like them.) Is there a downside? For me, as a fan, this is great. 

But there are also YouTube videos made by fans (I presume they are fans) playing the roles. So they don't look like the characters, the songs aren't as clever as the AI-generated material (there are probably exceptions). I am sure it's still FUN for them. But will they stop doing it and have AI do it for them? If so then have we, as a culture, lost something? More generally, if writers depend too much on AI we may lose the ability as a society to produce original material. And past some point AI might run out of material to use.

Fall Jobs Post 2026 [https://blog.computationalcomplexity.org/2026/11/fall-jobs-post-2026.html]

Each fall I try to predict the upcoming job market for theoretical computer science faculty and give suggestions to those pursuing these positions. Last year I wrote [https://blog.computationalcomplexity.org/2025/10/fall-jobs-post-2025.html] that we should get set for a rocky ride, with AI’s disruption of computer science, fiscal stress at universities, and new U.S. policies affecting visas and funding.

Rocky indeed. The job market for computer science graduates continues to decline and with it undergraduate enrollment in the discipline. That should crater demand for CS professors, but anecdotally I'm seeing quite a few departments advertising multiple slots. Perhaps as more computer science departments create Artificial Intelligence programs, deans see the need for continued growth in computing.

Mostly though we're seeing hiring at schools with reasonably good finances, those with large endowments and state flagship universities. Many other schools are struggling and having hiring cutbacks or outright freezes. So I expect the strongest theory students will still find good positions but the second tier might be more challenging than in the past. Oddly enough, a candidate may have an easier time landing a job at a top university than at a mid-tier one.

I'm pessimistic that the recent elections will change much, at least for the next couple of years. Democrats won't make higher education a priority issue. At best they will slow the development of AI but it will continue to disrupt not just computer science but all of higher ed. Trump will remain president for two more years so I don't expect we'll see major changes in visa policy, at least not in a positive direction. Other causes of financial stress, including Baumol's cost disease [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baumol_effect], the tough job market for new computer science graduates, the public perception of higher education, and the demographic cliff will not go away. Perhaps universities will learn how to use artificial intelligence to cut costs but I'm not holding my breath.

On the positive side, Congress could use the appropriations process to reopen the NSF spigot, though that could take years to play out.

On to specific advice for students on the academic job market. 

CATCS maintains a Theoretical Computer Science Jobs posting site [https://cstheory-jobs.org/]. More generally in computer science, there is the CRA Database of Job Candidates [https://cra.org/cv-database/] and the CRA [https://cra.org/ads/] and ACM [https://jobs.acm.org/] job postings. 

Don't shy away from artificial intelligence. Make sure you talk about AI in both your research and teaching statements. Departments will need instructors who can teach the courses in all the new AI majors popping up.

Have a well-designed website with all your job materials and links to your papers (AI can help). Make sure your Google Scholar, LinkedIn and GitHub (if relevant) sites are accurate and up to date. Make a short video describing your research to a general computer science crowd. Take advantage of all the links above. Reach out directly to professors you know at schools you are interested in. And start now.

Demaine-Gasarch-Hajiaghayi have written a book that is a sequel to Garey and Johnson [https://blog.computationalcomplexity.org/2026/11/demaine-gasarch-hajiaghayi-have-written.html]

LANCE: You should take a break from all the AI stuff and talk about your book!

BILL: I will! But it's not my book its co-authored with Erik Demaine and Mohammad Hajiaghayi. The title is

Computational Intractability: A Guide to Algorithmic Lower Bounds [https://link.amazon/B01XfbJSl]

Computers and Intractability: A Guide to the Theory of NP-Completeness [https://www.amazon.com/Computers-intractability-NP-completeness-mathematical-sciences/dp/0716710447?crid=OJNHE226GT2M&dib=eyJ2IjoiMSJ9.flEDbX3_z2d5i8DEyucrBFxM6Yw_cEzLgJOnhcx_y7a2eulZelEEIvHAH1M-bNI8R-Cd99RIGGZkLM_le9IAMwe5fFEvyu-Ji6JMKubx9WWFPey_2dyRQUBV5Z72YSY8DJq6uS3YqRwe63-NNNJZmJHBM7ZmjdsJuroNmBLPSgDTTeIsz3xRSwa1Rsz8HlQ4OMmMbRK3D6MTCxftwM6ivwQ8QIxAk2HMl0UkzBoCqgM.RVIizxKA0aCridMTW6YLeoMqsAxopNjiz8OLxnJcfXA&dib_tag=se&keywords=gary+johnson+np-complete&qid=1793629257&sprefix=gary+johnson+np-complet%2Caps%2C190&sr=8-6&linkCode=ll2&tag=computation09-20&linkId=76b19d99faed7e9cd52aff304aa55049&language=en_US&gaOptInStatus=true&ref_=as_li_ss_tl]

BILL: Indeed. DGH is a sequel to Garey and Johnson.

LANCE: But so much has happened since 1979.

BILL: Yes. That's why the book is over 500 pages and still does not cover everything since 1979.

LANCE: So what IS in the book?

BILL: The main question that drives complexity theory (and many other fields) is the question:

 Given a problem, how hard is it?

LANCE: Oh, You mean like P and NP!

BILL: Yes, but there is more! We cover P and NP: Many problems are proven NP-complete. Some are on Puzzles and Games. 

Examples of NP-Complete Problems that you might not have heard of

Disk Packing: Given a set of disks and a target square, can you place the disks so that they do not overlap and all of their centers lie within the square? This is Strongly NP-hard. 

Mate-in-1: Given a position in Settlers of Catan, can the player who is about to go win? This is NP-complete

Losing Checkers: Given a position in Checkers, is there a move that forces your opponent to win? This is NP-complete. This is an important problem in case you play Checkers with a Wookie, see here [https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ElZfE1AVDPQ].

Super Mario Brothers and Many other Nintendo Games: Given a position in the game, and a level, can the player get to that level? This is NP-complete. (There are versions that are PSPACE-complete and versions that are undecidable)

Some use the machinery developed since G & J to show lower bounds on approximation:

VC with vertices of degree \(\le 3\): If there is a polytime \((\frac{145}{144}-\epsilon)\)-approx then P=NP.

We have a chapter on the Unique Games Conj--uh, uh, Theorem. 

We look BELOW NP: 3SUM-hardness, APSP-hardness (these are both a bit outdated), streaming algorithms, online algorithms, others.

We look ABOVE NP: #P, ETR, 

LANCE: What's ETR?

BILL: Existential Theory of the Reals is the following problem: Given a sentence of the form 

\((\exists x_1)(\exists x_2)\cdots(\exists x_n)[ C_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge C_k]\)

where each \(C_i\) is a polynomial equality or inequality in \(x_1,\ldots,x_n\)

is this sentence, with the quantifiers going over the reals, true?

Tarski proved ETR is decidable. ETR is known to be NP-hard and is thought to be harder than NP. 

A problem is ETR-complete if it is equivalent to ETR. This is a way to classify some problems that involve reals that were hard to classify with more traditional classes. For example, the Art Gallery Problem (how many guards do you need) is ETR-complete

LANCE: What else do you have that's above NP? 

BILL: Poly Hierarchy, PSPACE, and even undecidability.

LANCE: Are there really NEW results about undecidability?

BILL: There are some video games where questions about winning are undecidable. I doubt Turing knew that. One of them is called braid (see here [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Braid_(video_game)]).

LANCE: Don't you have whole chapters devoted to the now-defunct Exponential Time Hypothesis and the 3-SUM conjecture?

Bill: Also the APSP conjecture. Are these now-defunct conjectures still worth studying? I did a blog post on that here [https://blog.computationalcomplexity.org/2026/11/3sum-and-apsp-and-eth-oh-my.html]. Do you read my blog posts?

LANCE: Do you read mine?

BILL: Fair point.

LANCE: Despite having three chapters obsolete, the rest of the book sounds great. Where can I get it?

BILL: You can buy it on Amazon, see here [https://www.amazon.com/Computational-Intractability-Guide-Algorithmic-Bounds/dp/0262550776]. You can buy it at MIT press here [https://mitpress.mit.edu/9780262550772/computational-intractability/]. You can download a prior draft here [https://hardness.mit.edu/].

Another Step Towards Optiland [https://blog.computationalcomplexity.org/2026/11/another-step-towards-optiland.html]

When the Clay Mathematics Institute announced the Millennium Prize Problems [https://www.claymath.org/millennium-problems/] back in 2000, we joked that if you proved P = NP, you wouldn't only get the $1 million prize for solving P v NP, but you would get $7 million since you could use P = NP to solve the rest.

In theory, if P = NP, then you could efficiently find the proof of any theorem, provided its formal proof was not too long. In practice, we don't have P = NP, and back then we didn't even have a good way to formalize a proof. Fast forward to today and we have a computer generated formalized proof of Navier-Stokes [https://github.com/openai/NavierStokesAndEuler/blob/main/README.md] and a number of other important problems.

In my 2022 CACM article [https://cacm.acm.org/research/fifty-years-of-p-vs-np-and-the-possibility-of-the-impossible/], I argued that we were heading towards Optiland, where we got all of the goodies of P = NP, without losing cryptography. But some challenges remained.

Machine learning seems to fail learning simple arithmetic—for example, summing up a large collection of numbers or multiplying large numbers. One could imagine combining machine learning with symbolic mathematical tools. While we’ve seen some impressive advances in theorem provers, we sit a long way from my dream task of taking one of my research papers, with its informal proofs, and having an AI system fill in the details and verify the proof.

I failed to imagine that in less than five years AI models would not just fill in the details of my proofs but find the proofs themselves.

Still plenty of math problems that AI hasn't solved, including P v NP itself. I've given up predicting what AI will prove next, though I still believe P v NP requires new techniques that AI won't come up with on its own.

I watch discussions on whether we could find some version of the ETH-refutation algorithm [https://blog.computationalcomplexity.org/2026/10/the-exponential-time-refutation.html] that would help solve SAT problems in practice. Spoiler alert: It will never beat CDCL [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conflict-driven_clause_learning].

We are running out of examples of real-world problems we still need P = NP to tackle. Outside of breaking codes, what could you do with P = NP that you couldn't do today using other means?

3SUM and APSP and ETH, Oh My! [https://blog.computationalcomplexity.org/2026/11/3sum-and-apsp-and-eth-oh-my.html]

(Throughout this post I will assume that the recent results by OpenAI and Anthropic contain correct proofs of what they claim.)

BEGIN DOCUMENT FROM TWO YEARS AGO

Why do we think \(P\ne NP\)?

a) Lots of people have worked on SAT and other NP-complete problems, without getting them in P. This is not mathematical evidence but it is worth noting.

b) If SAT is in P then all problems in NP are in P. This implication is one reason I think \(P\ne NP\) is true. I find this more compelling than (a), though it's still not a proof.

c) Just my opinion: I think SAT is not in P.

-------------------------

3SUM is the following problem: Given integers \(a_1,\ldots,a_n\) is there a triple of indices \(i< j < k \) such that \(a_i + a_j + a_k = 0\)? There is a clever algorithm for this problem that takes \(O(n^2)\) time. It is conjectured that 3SUM has no subquadratic algorithm (that is, for every fixed \(\epsilon>0\) there is no \(O(n^{2-\epsilon})\) algorithm). (My spellcheck doesn't think subquadratic is a word. Are they right?) 

This conjecture is widely believed. There is a notion of 3SUM-hardness and 3SUM-completeness. \( A \le B \) if there is a linear time function \(f\) such that \(x\in A\) iff \(f(x)\in B\). A problem \(A\) is 3SUM-complete if both \(A\le 3SUM\) and \(3SUM \le A\).

There are around 15-20 3SUM-complete problems. Either they are all subquadratic (which includes 3SUM being subquadratic) or none are subquadratic. Given the conjecture, none of them are subquadratic. That is the common belief.

Why do we think 3SUM has no subquadratic algorithm?

a) Lots of people have worked on 3SUM without getting it into subquadratic time. This is not mathematical evidence but it is worth noting.

b) Darn! There is no Cook-Levin-type theorem for 3SUM.

c) Just my opinion: I think 3SUM is not subquadratic. I only wish there were a Cook-Levin-type theorem for this belief. I doubt this is possible.

--------------------------

APSP (All Pairs Shortest Paths) is the following problem: Given a weighted graph \(G\), find, for all pairs, the cost of the shortest path. There is an \(O(n^3\)) algorithm for APSP. It is widely believed that there is no subcubic algorithm for APSP (that is, for every fixed \(\epsilon>0\) there is no \(O(n^{3-\epsilon})\) algorithm). This conjecture is widely believed. There is a notion of APSP-hardness and APSP-completeness. \( A \le B \) if there is a linear time function \(f\) such that \(x\in A\) iff \(f(x) \in B\). A problem \(A\) is APSP-complete if both \(A\le APSP\) and \(APSP \le A\). (My spellcheck doesn't think subcubic is a word. Are they right?)

There are around 15-20 problems that are APSP-complete. Either they are all subcubic (which includes APSP being subcubic) or none are subcubic. Given the conjecture, none of them are subcubic. That is the common belief.

Why do we think APSP has no subcubic algorithm?

c) Just my opinion: I think APSP is not subcubic. I only wish there were a Cook-Levin-type theorem for this belief. I doubt this is possible.

ETH (Exponential Time Hypothesis) Not only is 3SAT not in P but 3SAT on \(n\) variables requires \(2^{\Omega(n)}\) time. ETH is widely believed.

Informally, if ETH is true, and \(A\) is NP-complete by a linear reduction, then \(A\) requires exponential time. There are many such NP-complete problems.

A writeup of this by J. Alman and V.V. Williams is here [https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.06783]. 

2) Anthropic has obtained an \(O(n^{2.9995})\) algorithm for APSP with integer weights (with no negative cycles). Darling, thinks this result is hilarious. (Spell check thinks Antrhopic is a word. Are they right?)

OpenAI's announcement is here [https://openai.com/index/building-scientific-understanding-with-academic-communities/].

Assuming the conjecture, for every fixed \(\epsilon\), for every 3SUM-complete problem \(A\) there is no \(O(n^{1.5-\epsilon})\) algorithm for \(A\).

At least until AI gives an \(O(n^{1.499999})\) algorithm for 3SUM.

2) Similar for APSP and, say, \(O(n^{2.5})\).

3) ETH. Replace with the following: there exists \(0<c<1\) such that there is no \(O(2^{n^c})\) algorithm for \(3SAT\). Or something else like it.

The Exponential Time Refutation [https://blog.computationalcomplexity.org/2026/10/the-exponential-time-refutation.html]

In this week's big news (though it's only Wednesday), OpenAI announced [https://openai.com/index/building-scientific-understanding-with-academic-communities/] a proof that the exponential-time hypothesis is false [https://github.com/openai/math/blob/main/preprints/The-Exponential-Time-Hypothesis-is-False-October-25-2026/paper.pdf], with a Lean proof [https://github.com/openai/math/blob/main/lean/docs/378.md]. Later Anthropic released their own paper [https://github.com/anthropics/formal-math/blob/16a5ce41304fcdaea4e0be7c132e1c361dc26da5/eth/paper.pdf]. Ryan O'Donnell [https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~odonnell/some_eth_false_exposition.pdf] and Shiva Kintali [https://shivakintali.github.io/papers/ETH.pdf] posted follow-up proofs, smartly focusing on simplicity rather than getting the best bounds. OpenAI will partner [https://docs.google.com/document/d/1adB0IhJgLzsmbfCgoLrtoJTjnlPf74UnyjMo35P5JHo/edit?tab=t.0] with three of my favorite complexity theorists, Russell Impagliazzo, Mohan Paturi and Ryan Williams to verify and study the proof. I have not verified the proof myself, but it is safe to assume now that the exponential-time hypothesis is toast. 

Update (10/29): Impagliazzo, Paturi and Williams set up a site to discuss the ETH Refutation [https://ethdiscussion.blog/2026/10/26/eth-refutation-examination-and-discussion/] with a discussion blog [https://ethdiscussion.blog/] and paper repository [https://drive.google.com/drive/folders/1SAqEO1kt6wnA0NXuZecyPhdBgzW3Z0y1].

Recall 3-SAT, the problem of determining whether a Boolean formula that is an AND of ORs of three variables or their negations has a satisfying assignment. 3-SAT is the prototypical NP-complete problem from which we derive all the others. The exponential-time hypothesis (ETH), introduced by Impagliazzo and Paturi in 1999 [https://doi.org/10.1109/CCC.1999.766282], building on their work with Francis Zane, states that there is a \(c > 0\) such that every algorithm solving 3-SAT requires time \(2^{cn}\), where \(n\) is the number of variables. There's also a strong exponential-time hypothesis (SETH) which says that for every \(\epsilon > 0\) there is a \(k\) such that \(k\)-SAT cannot be solved in time \(2^{(1-\epsilon)n}\). The OpenAI paper gives an algorithm that, for every fixed \(k\), determines whether a \(k\)-CNF formula with \(n\) variables and \(m\) clauses is satisfiable, and even counts the number of solutions, in time \((n+m)^{O(1)}\,2^{O(n\log\log n/\sqrt{\log n})}\) (their Lean proof gives a slightly weaker bound). This refutes ETH, and hence SETH, which implies ETH.

If you want to understand the proof, I suggest starting with Ryan O'Donnell's paper [https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~odonnell/some_eth_false_exposition.pdf]. Ryan also mentions Bernardo Subercaseaux's observation [https://x.com/b_subercaseaux/status/2114865520055046442] that we get a nontrivial simulation of nondeterministic time on multitape Turing machines, 

\[\mathrm{NTIME}(T) \subseteq \mathrm{DTIME}\left(2^{O(T(\log\log T)^3/\sqrt{\log T})}\right).\]

Researchers studied ETH and SETH because they implied many other hardness results, for example

If ETH is true then Clique requires time \(2^{\Omega(n)}\) on \(n\)-vertex graphs, or \(2^{\Omega(\sqrt{m})}\) on graphs with \(m\) edges.

If ETH is true then finding a clique of size \(k\) cannot be done in time \(f(k)\,n^{o(k)}\) for any computable function \(f\), so the trivial \(n^{O(k)}\) algorithm is essentially optimal [https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jcss.2006.04.007].

If SETH is true then the orthogonal vectors problem [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthogonal_vectors_problem] on \(n\) vectors of dimension \(\omega(\log n)\) cannot be solved in time \(n^{2-\epsilon}\) for any \(\epsilon > 0\).

Bill's new (badly timed) book [https://www.amazon.com/Computational-Intractability-Guide-Algorithmic-Bounds-ebook/dp/B0GC1VRLDB?crid=2TA3RU9KVMCAU&dib=eyJ2IjoiMSJ9.aAhGi9rIgk4KZf76JdkUZQ5aeuHh-4a0IzqFES5ZwbwEWQ8EGK_0jbiOHPTdbu8CINIsCscRYwue7gBarinOOKjwuhsYLgkS_4942wmwbIdfrGeMO5kkN0D75Kom1IDgWcDRpicm6GOWFE2jhwXV6O95FGy-reiL3QMd2vw64ygpNHNOV_Hb0alI6YtLc86QoMWWGM-lWkE_jfne2hKj9T9TYvOquQB_LEJo4TKWZpk.vY-kFsp4lOanGt5j7BNsUrtgKqhq-s0zdeuy6MayZQw&dib_tag=se&keywords=demaine+gasarch+haj+book&qid=1793212892&sprefix=demaine+gasarch+haj+book%2Caps%2C762&sr=8-1&linkCode=ll2&tag=computation09-20&linkId=e9adc435f7a1166411594b4d595b4f7d&language=en_US&gaOptInStatus=true&ref_=as_li_ss_tl] with Erik Demaine and Mohammad Hajiaghayi has details. The fact that ETH and SETH fail doesn't mean their implications are false.

Keep in mind that the OpenAI algorithm applies to \(k\)-SAT but not to NP problems in general. It gives nothing new for Subset Sum or Traveling Salesman, whose best algorithms still take roughly \(2^{n/2}\) and \(2^n\) time, because the reductions from those problems to 3-SAT blow up the number of variables too much. Getting subexponential algorithms for all of NP would require solving 3-SAT in time \(2^{n^{o(1)}}\mathrm{,}\) which the OpenAI result doesn't get close to achieving.
