Finding faster quantum algorithms with agents and formal verification

Sergey Shirobokov, Ben Jaderberg and Ryan Babbush

How using agent teams and formal verification can improve quantum algorithms for modelling fusion energy, chemistry and enzymes

Quantum computers could soon solve problems beyond the reach of classical supercomputers. However, there is a catch: even when using the best quantum algorithms, some important applications still require more resources than early quantum hardware will be able to support.

In a new paper [https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.09822], we used teams of Gemini 4 agents to improve well-known quantum algorithms, creating highly efficient quantum circuits. These optimized circuits, which are instructions for how a quantum computer should carry out an algorithm, could one day help us run simulations that solve complex scientific challenges, from fusion energy to drug design. To help the community build on this work, we are open-sourcing our Lean library [http://github.com/google-deepmind/formal-quantum-computation], problem specifications, and verified solutions on GitHub.

Because we cannot test resource-intensive quantum circuits on classical computers or today's small quantum processors, we have to prove that they work mathematically. Our agent teams proved each result using Lean, a formal theorem prover.

Improving quantum algorithms using agentic teams For each problem, we tasked separate, independent agent teams with finding algorithmic improvements. These ideas were then verified with Lean, before entering a shared notebook. Every four hours, agents from each team met at a virtual conference to trade, debate and compare ideas. As teams produced more effective ideas over time, the computational cost gradually fell until the experiment finished.

We chose to focus on applications that could one day impact some of humanity’s greatest challenges, such as clean energy and drug design, but our method is not limited to these problem areas.

We found a quantum circuit that helps calculate an important property of inertial confinement fusion, a fusion reaction method that uses lasers to compress hydrogen fuel until ignition. Our agent teams cut the required computing cost (a metric that combines required qubits and operations) by 11.8×, and lowered required memory from 5,650 to 4,338 error-corrected qubits [https://research.google/blog/making-quantum-error-correction-work].

The agents also reduced costs for simulating molecules in drug metabolism and certain photochemical reactions, which are complex reactions that are difficult for classical computers to simulate. For a common light-sensitive molecule, the agent teams made quantum simulation 8× cheaper, simplifying how the algorithm tracks particle positions and electric forces. For Cytochrome P450, an enzyme that breaks down most human medicines and is important for drug design, they found a 2.5× improvement and lowered memory from 481 to 333 qubits, by replacing bulky memory lookups.

We also improved a fundamental quantum algorithm and a widely used arithmetic building block. We found a 2.57x speedup for a specific implementation of Grover's algorithm, a foundational quantum operation that searches through an unsorted list, and a 290x speedup for arcsin, a common routine that is used by many other quantum algorithms to load numbers into quantum states.

Running these algorithms in scientific simulations would still demand more computational resources than the first error-corrected quantum computers are likely to provide, but these improvements are significant.

We are also excited by the way in which we found them. We built a new, open-source library [http://github.com/google-deepmind/formal-quantum-computation], in Lean for quantum algorithms which could be used by teams of collaborating agents, so their ideas were backed by mathematical correctness guarantees. We could then set the research direction by specifying the problems for our agent teams and supervise their progress. Our knowledge of quantum algorithms, combined with the way we set up our agent teams, led to real scientific results.

The same approach could also accelerate other aspects of quantum computing research, and scientific discovery more broadly. Proof-checked AI search compresses work that once took years of manual design, empowering researchers to focus on choosing problems and interpreting results.
