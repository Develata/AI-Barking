                                                                                         Abstract

                                                     Long-horizon agentic tasks require a model to call tools over many steps at contexts
                                                     of 100K tokens or more, yet many low-bit quantization methods are calibrated
                                                     on short web text and judged on perplexity. This mismatch is costly: existing
                                                     quantizations of Qwen3.8-27B at or below 3 bits per weight match BF16 on shorter
                                                     tasks like AIME, yet in published results solve at most 72% as many tasks as
                                                     BF16 on DeepSWE, a long-horizon agentic benchmark. We propose NariQ, a
                                                     trellis-coded post-training quantization method that calibrates on tokens generated
                                                     by the BF16 model in agent sessions. On Qwen3.8-27B, NariQ at 3.0 and 2.5
                                                     bits per weight stays level with BF16 on two long-horizon agentic benchmarks,
                                                     DeepSWE and Terminal Bench, as well as on AIME and RealWorldQA. Our
                                                     checkpoint at 2.0 bits per weight trails this performance. To our knowledge,
                                                     these are the first quantizations at or below 3 bits per weight shown to match
                                                     their full-precision version on long-horizon agentic tasks. We release the files as
                                                     Qwen3.8-27B-NariQ-GGUF, with inference available through our llama.cpp fork
                                                     at https://github.com/nari-labs/nari-llama.cpp.


                                         1    Introduction

                                         Language models are increasingly used as agents working on long-horizon tasks. For example, a
                                         single task in DeepSWE [8], a popular software engineering benchmark, takes an agent on average
                                         about 150 tool calls and 50 minutes, with a median context of 92K tokens.
                                         Near 4 bits per weight, quantization already preserves such agentic performance. NVIDIA’s NVFP4
                                         version of Qwen3.8-27B [16] stays within 1.6 points of BF16 on Terminal Bench [13] and solves at
                                         least as many DeepSWE tasks as BF16 [9].
                                         Below 4 bits, existing quantized models still do well on short tasks, but not on long-horizon ones. For
                                         example, the 3-bit GSQ-RCO quantization of Qwen3.8-27B [10] stays within a point of BF16 on
                                         AIME (96.9% against 97.7%). Yet in Unsloth’s reported results it fully solves only 63% as many
                                         DeepSWE tasks as BF16 (Table 1).
                                         We therefore ask whether we can quantize a model to 3 bits per weight or fewer without losing its
                                         long-horizon agent capability. The answer decides whether capable agents can run on consumer
                                         GPUs, where the weights must fit into 8–32 GB.
                                         A better weight format alone is unlikely to close this gap. Random rotations make weights close to
                                         Gaussian [1, 21], and on Gaussians, trellis codes are already within about 10% of the rate-distortion
                                         bound at 2 bits (mean squared error 0.069 against 0.063) [22]. We instead ask which tokens a
                                         quantized agent must get right. Many low-bit methods are calibrated on short chunks of web text [5]
                                         and judged on perplexity and short benchmarks, which say little about agents. They weight every
                                         calibration token equally, whereas an agent’s quality depends on the tokens it writes.


                                         Preprint.
We introduce NariQ, a post-training quantization recipe for long-horizon agentic use at 2.0, 2.5 and
3.0 bits per weight. NariQ keeps the standard trellis code and changes which tokens the loss counts,
which modules get which widths, and what data the scales are tuned on. It sets each module’s width
from an estimate of that module’s own loss, which stays informative for trellis codes. It codes the
output layer in bands by how likely the model is to predict each token, keeping every band at 2 bits or
more to protect rare tokens.
We evaluate agentic work, math, visual understanding and multilingual tasks. NariQ at 3.0 and
2.5 bpw is level with BF16 within noise on all benchmarks we ran; at 2.0 bpw, it trails BF16.

2   Method
A file at B bpw uses B bits per text-model parameter, including codes, scales, labels, embeddings
and the output layer. To build a file θ̂ whose next-token distribution stays close to BF16’s, we (1)
quantize modules at several integer widths, (2) estimate their loss from gradients, (3) allocate widths
within the budget, and (4) tune the scales.
We trellis-quantize each rotated matrix [22], using input- and output-side statistics to compensate
rounding error as in YAQA [23]. We retain input-only rounding unless two-sided rounding lowers
held-out curvature-weighted error by at least 5%; almost every module uses both sides. NariQ adds
four changes; Figure 1 shows their development sequence at 3.0 bpw.
First, the end-to-end loss counts only model-written tokens. Agent traces contain long tool outputs
and file contents that can dominate an all-token loss. For a calibration trace x (Section 3.1), let M
index the tokens generated by BF16. Our objective is

Both models receive the same prefix x<t , including tool outputs. The mask selects prediction targets;
it does not remove context tokens.
Second, widths are allocated by each module’s own loss term. Since the KL is minimized at BF16,
we use a second-order expansion around its weights and neglect interactions between modules’ errors,
so the total loss is a sum of per-module terms. Take one module and flatten its weights into a vector:
W at BF16 and Wk = W + Ek at width k. With H the module’s Hessian block at BF16, its loss
term at width k is ℓk = 12 ⟨Ek , HEk ⟩. We take gradients of L on a file with every module at some
width. Let c be the module’s width in that file and G its weight gradient; under the approximation,
G ≈ HEc . RCO [7] scores a move to another width k ̸= c by the first-order change
                             ⟨G, Wk − Wc ⟩ ≈ ⟨Ec , HEk ⟩ − ⟨Ec , HEc ⟩.                             (2)
The first term measures how much the errors at the two widths align. They barely do: in held-out
curvature estimates for four probe matrices, the curvature-weighted cosine between the 3-bit error
and the 2-, 4- and 5-bit errors is 0.035, 0.009 and 0.004. We therefore neglect this term. The score is
then about −2ℓc for every k ̸= c, whereas the quadratic loss change is ℓk − ℓc , so the score misses
the loss at the destination width.
NariQ instead uses the score to estimate the current loss: averaging 12 ⟨G, Wc − Wk ⟩ over k ̸= c gives
an estimate of ℓc . The gradient estimates ℓc only at the current width. To predict the other widths,
we assume a module’s loss scales with the code’s error: with rk the trellis code’s measured mean
squared error at width k, which falls about 4× per added bit, we predict ℓk ≈ ℓc rk /rc . For example,
a module’s loss at 2 bits is predicted as about 3.9 times its loss at 3 bits.
Scaling a noisy gradient estimate to a distant width amplifies its noise. We combine estimates from
several width assignments, rescaling to a common width and weighting by inverse variance. We use
the mean plus two standard errors and apply a small positive floor.

Choosing one width per module makes this a multiple-choice knapsack problem. We use dynamic
programming over a discretized budget, favoring the fullest feasible allocation. An exact-cost check
enforces the storage limit, but global optimality is not guaranteed. The loss estimate fails at 1 bit: at
2.0 bpw, moving the 12 modules the allocator had put at 1 bit up to 2 bits lowered KL by 7.2% for
only 0.021 bpw, far more than predicted. Every linear layer therefore stays at 2 bits or more.
Third, the output layer gets more bits for likely tokens. Only about 13% of its 248,320 tokens appear
as generated tokens in our calibration traces, although every row contributes to the softmax. For a
token with BF16 probability p, the KL curvature with respect to its logit is p(1 − p), which is close
to p for all but the most likely tokens. We therefore rank rows by their mean predicted probability,
as a proxy for sensitivity, and sort them into 16 bands of about 15,500 rows. We code each band at
its own width, as a separate module in the allocation. Rare tokens still need some precision: in our
tests, bands at 1 bit lowered KL on most traces but raised it on traces that use rare tokens, such as
multilingual text, so every band keeps at least 2 bits. At 3.0 bpw the bands lower the mean per-trial
KL by 12.1%. At 2.0 bpw the input embedding is also banded. It has no predicted probability, so its
rows are ranked by how often each token appears in the input, and unlike output bands, its rare-token
bands may use 1 bit.
Fourth, scale tuning uses general data as well as coding traces. With codes and widths fixed, we
tune every coded matrix’s scales and every norm weight to minimize L on coding agent traces and
BF16 replies to general prompts (math, science, chat, code and long documents), weighted 70:30.
An earlier 2.0 bpw file tuned on coding traces alone scored 77.3% on AIME against BF16’s 97.7%;
adding the general data raised it to 86.0%.
Format. We release the files as NariQ GGUFs [6]. The format rotates every matrix with randomized
Hadamard blocks [21] of power-of-two size. In attention and DeltaNet layers, one side of most
matrices is first shuffled by a seeded permutation. Because every block has power-of-two size, the
format needs no Hadamard sizes tied to a particular model. The codebook is computed from the
trellis state, as in EXL3 [24], so kernels decode the codes inside the matrix multiply.

4     Results
4.1   Agent benchmarks

At 3.0 and 2.5 bpw, our files are level with BF16 on DeepSWE (Table 1). They solve 47 and 46 tasks
against BF16’s 44, and their DeepSWE scores are 84.9% and 84.5% against BF16’s 83.0% (paired by
task, +1.9 and +1.5 points, s.e. 3.2 and 3.6). At 2.0 bpw, our file solves 33 tasks and scores 75.7%,
7.3 points under BF16 (s.e. 3.3). In Unsloth’s reported results, every file at or below 3.0 bpw solves at
most 72% as many tasks as its BF16.
On Terminal Bench 2.1, our 3.0 and 2.5 bpw files score 102% and 96% of our BF16, within noise
of it (paired by task, +1.2 and −2.4 points, s.e. 3.5 and 3.2), and our 2.0 bpw file scores 84%, 10.2
points under (s.e. 3.5). The reported files at or below 3.0 bpw score 64–90% of Unsloth’s BF16.

4.2   Beyond coding

General ability holds (Table 2): on AIME, RealWorldQA and MGSM, our 3.0 bpw file is within a
point of BF16 and our 2.5 bpw file within 2.2 points. At 2.0 bpw, the largest drop is on AIME, 11.7
points.

4.3   Size and speed

NariQ’s text-only GGUFs, including embeddings and output layers, occupy 10.1, 8.4 and 6.8 GB
at 3.0, 2.5 and 2.0 bpw. Vision adds a shared 0.9 GB BF16 projector; the original 55.6 GB BF16
checkpoint includes vision and MTP. At these three budgets, our llama.cpp fork on one RTX PRO
6000 generates 89, 99 and 112 tokens/s at a 4K-token context, and 71, 77 and 85 at 64K, for a single
session.

5     Related Works
Codes and rounding. Low-bit formats include scalar and grouped codes [5, 11], and vector and
trellis codes [4, 21, 22], often applied after random rotations [1, 21]. EXL3 [24] ships the trellis
family for local inference. GPTQ [5] and LDLQ [1] round with the input Hessian, and YAQA [23]
adds the output side. We use QTIP’s bitshift trellis with a computed codebook.
Bit allocation and recovery. Mixed-precision allocation by sensitivity is established [29], and
search methods such as EvoPress [18] and RCO [7] optimize widths against the end-to-end loss;
GSQ-RCO [10] publishes files of this model built that way. Variable bit-width quantization [14]
learns widths per group, down to about 1 bit on the output layer. Fine-tuning scales and other
continuous parameters after quantization is common [2, 22]. Our allocator differs in reading the
end-to-end gradient as each module’s own loss term, which remains informative for codes whose
errors at different widths are uncorrelated.
Generated calibration data. FAQ [28] regenerates calibration data with a larger model from the
same family. OnQ [30] recovers low-bit reasoning through distillation on the quantized student’s
own trajectories. NariQ instead uses fixed BF16-generated tool-use traces, restricts its loss to
model-written positions, and allocates bits against that objective. We evaluate the resulting files on
long-horizon agentic tasks.

6   Limitations and Future Work

While our 3.0 and 2.5 bpw files are level with BF16 on all benchmarks we ran, evaluations on more
models, more agent harnesses and more agentic benchmarks remain future work. At 2.0 bpw, our file
trails BF16 by 7.3 points on DeepSWE and 11.7 on AIME, and we hypothesize that training beyond
scales and norms can recover it.
The multi-token prediction module is not yet quantized, and our llama.cpp fork is tuned for one GPU
generation (SM120); other GPUs run it slower, and GPUs older than Ampere fall back to the CPU.
We also plan to quantize the KV cache and activations, so long contexts fit on 16 GB consumer GPUs,
and extend the study to more models and more budgets at or below 3 bits.


7   Conclusion

NariQ calibrates and allocates bits around model-written tokens in agent sessions. On Qwen3.8-27B,
its 3.0 and 2.5 bpw files are level with BF16 on long-horizon agentic benchmarks. We release
the files as Qwen3.8-27B-NariQ-GGUF, with inference available through our llama.cpp fork at
https://github.com/nari-labs/nari-llama.cpp.
