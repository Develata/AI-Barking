Fan Chang, Hong Liu, and Maio Liu Proved Chvátal’s 1972 conjecture [https://gilkalai.wordpress.com/2026/11/20/fan-chang-hong-liu-and-maio-liu-proved-chvatals-1972-conjecture/]

 Posted on November 20, 2026 [https://gilkalai.wordpress.com/2026/11/20/fan-chang-hong-liu-and-maio-liu-proved-chvatals-1972-conjecture/] by Gil Kalai [https://gilkalai.wordpress.com/author/gilkalai/] 

 [https://gilkalai.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/chvatal2.png]

Two unrelated updates: Tristan Buckmaster and Levent Alpöge announced today [https://mastodon.social/@tristanbuckmaster/117649035783718539] a counterexample for the regularity of the unforced Euler equation and Navier Stokes equation; Levent Alpöge also announced [https://alpo.ge/remark.pdf] a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis. Good day for Levent! 🙂

And now for the main news of today’s post about Chvátal’s conjecture.

Chvátal’s conjecture

In 1972 Václav (Vašek) Chvátal made the following beautiful conjecture

For a family of sets and an element let . A family of sets is called an ideal (or a down-family or a simplicial complex) if whenever and then .

Chvátal’s conjecture: Let be an ideal of sets and let be an intersecting subfamily, (i.e., for every , ). Then there is an element so that .

We discussed Chvátal’s conjecture in this 2008 post [https://gilkalai.wordpress.com/2008/09/28/extremal-combinatorics-iii-some-basic-theorems/] about extremal combinatorics. (I also raised it in 2021 [https://gilkalai.wordpress.com/2021/01/29/possible-future-polymath-projects-2009-2021/] as a possible polymath project.)

Chang-Liu-Liu’s solution

A few weeks ago Fan Chang, Hong Liu, and Maio Liu (CLL) proved Chvátal’s conjecture. [https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.19123]This is a remarkable breakthrough! Congratulations to Fan, Hong, and Maio.

The proof relies on the connections with the analysis of Boolean functions and with correlation inequalities that were proposed in a 2018 paper [https://arxiv.org/abs/1608.08954] by Friedgut–Kahn–Kalai–Keller. CLL had an earlier result [https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.32024] proving another correlation inequality from FKKK18.

A few days ago I had a zoom meeting with Fan, Hong, and Maio, who told me about the path to the proof, about their further work on correlation inequalities which is an important area in its own right, about analysis of Boolean functions, about combinatorics in the far east, especially in China and South Korea, and about AI in mathematics.

Fan is a PhD student at Nankai University, and Maio is a PhD student at Shandong University. They were both visiting students in Hong’s ECOPRO group [https://www.ibs.re.kr/ecopro/members/#:~:text=Members%20*%20Hong%20Liu%20%E5%88%98%E9%B8%BF%20(2022.4%2D)%20Email%3A%20hongliu%40ibs.re.kr.] at the Institute for Basic Science (IBS) in South Korea. They also told me that Vašek Chvátal offered ten dollars for the solution of his conjecture and on Nov. 2 he deposited the current equivalent – $80.61. (See the picture above.)

Some background

Our 2018 paper relied on two earlier lines of research. One line of research is related to Chvatal’s conjecture itself. Danny Kleitman proposed a sharp form of Chvatal’s conjecture and Jeff Kahn proposed an even sharper form that could be expressed in Fourier language. Another line of research is about correlation inequalities. In combinatorics this was pioneered by the Harris-Kleitman inequality (a special case of FKG, see, again, this post [https://gilkalai.wordpress.com/2008/09/28/extremal-combinatorics-iii-some-basic-theorems/], also for some applications to extremal combinatorics). The connection to analysis of Boolean functions was studied by Talagrand who, among other things, relied on influences and KKL. (See this picture [https://gilkalai.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/02/talabb.png] from this 2020 post [https://gilkalai.wordpress.com/2020/02/28/remarkable-new-stochastic-methods-in-abf-ronen-eldan-and-renan-gross-found-a-new-proof-for-kkl-and-settled-a-conjecture-by-talagrand/].)

Subsequent developments regarding Chvatal’s conjecture:

Shortly after CLL’s proof, Peter Keevash extended it [https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.23595] to prove the full generality of Kahn’s original conjecture. Ellis, Friedgut and Filmus found a simplified version “from the book” [https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.28404] of the proof of Chvatal’s conjecture. (Both these developments primarily rely on AI.)

A few other things about related correlation inequalities.

Correlation inequalities is a wide area with applications in various areas of mathematics. Talagrands 1996 “first-order” refinement [https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF01844850] of Harris-Kleitman was a starting point for much research in the area. around the same time FKKK was written I wrote an additional paper [https://arxiv.org/abs/1511.04600] with Keller and Mossel on correlation between monotone families. Both papers proposed several conjectures and over the years some of them were settled. Both of our papers also relied on a 2014 paper [https://arxiv.org/abs/0911.1601] by Keller, Mossel and Sen. Eldan proved in 2022 an interesting second order correlation relation, [https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.11641] and recently Chang, Li, Wang, and Wang [https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08289] found an interesting refinement.

Test Your Intuition: Double Edition [https://gilkalai.wordpress.com/2026/11/17/test-your-intuition-double-edition/]

 Posted on November 17, 2026 [https://gilkalai.wordpress.com/2026/11/17/test-your-intuition-double-edition/] by Gil Kalai [https://gilkalai.wordpress.com/author/gilkalai/] 

On The Way from Yale to NYC

I was driving from Yale to NYC and I saw a very puzzling view from the window: to my right there were 6 highway lanes with cars in the opposite direction to me. To the right of these lanes there were additional 6 highway lanes with cars moving in the same direction as me. How is this possible, I thought. I realized that while the view from the window was crystal clear there was one mistake in my worldview which explains the unusual sight from the window.

TYI: What was the explanation?

On the way to an AMS meeting in upstate New York

Frank Sottile [https://franksottile.github.io/] is a well-known mathematician with many mathematical interests and wonderful results. I still thankfully and fondly remember a party in his house when I visited Texas A&M, many years ago.

I went to a very exciting AMS meeting in upstate New York. I was looking forward to meeting old friends and new mathematics, and I noticed that Frank Sottile is giving two invited lectures in two separate special sessions on quite different topics. I was looking forward to hearing both Frank’s lectures. As I was driving, when I was very near to the location of the conference, to my surprise, I saw a street named “Frank Sottile”.

TYI: What was the explanation?

a) Looking forward to Frank’s lecture I day dreamed regarding the street name

b) I misread a similar name

c) (humorous) The city was also impressed by Frank’s two lectures and decided to name a street honoring him

d) It was a street named “Frank Sottile” after a different Frank Sottile.

Diaries for My Nostalgic 2026 US Tour: Princeton, Oct. 12–11. [https://gilkalai.wordpress.com/2026/11/07/diaries-for-my-nostalgic-2026-us-tour-princeton-oct-12-11/]

 Posted on November 7, 2026 [https://gilkalai.wordpress.com/2026/11/07/diaries-for-my-nostalgic-2026-us-tour-princeton-oct-12-11/] by Gil Kalai [https://gilkalai.wordpress.com/author/gilkalai/] 

 [https://gilkalai.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/assaf-gil-creature.jpg]

with Assaf Naor and a creature

“I am going now to sacrifice my rook”, said Assaf’s older son… six moves later I encountered a sudden, humiliating checkmate.”

Preface

This blog serves both as a place to share my ideas with readers interested in combinatorics, mathematics, TCS, quantum computing, academia, and the other matters I write about, and as a personal record. I often return to old posts for personal and technical details and useful links. (There is now another audience as well: AI tools, which are most welcome to read and use everything written here.)

On my recent US tour, I visited Providence, Boston, New Haven, Princeton, and New Brunswick (and, very briefly, NYC), gave six lectures on four different topics, met many people, and exchanged many ideas. (I even have some incomprehensible notes.) These were two unusually hectic weeks, which I quite enjoyed. Starting today, I will describe this adventure (largely for myself) in a few parts, in reverse chronological order.

Monday Oct 12, Princeton

I have fond memories from Princeton. I was a member at IAS in the falls of 1995 and 2000, and visited both the institute and the university several other times. In my visits to Princeton I often stay with Edna and Avi Wigderson.

17:00 My last meeting, over dinner, was with Peter Sarnak, whom I first met in the 80s. Naturally, AI and Math occupied part of the conversation and Peter is close to some leading players: Jacob Tsimerman is a former PhD student of his and he recently established an independent institute for AI safety. Levent Alpöge, who solved the Jacobian conjecture over the Mondial with Claude and moved on to prove many other major results, was also close to Peter in his Ph D days. Of course, Peter’s research is close to the amazing solution of the quasi Rieman Hypothesis [https://www.quantamagazine.org/ai-proved-the-quasi-riemann-hypothesis-what-happens-now-20261019/] and Peter told me a little about it. Peter sounded rather critical of OpenAI’s practices.

We talked about various other things and fondly discussed a polymath debate [https://polymathprojects.org/2011/03/09/polymath-discussion-at-ias/] we both took part in. I did not get to discuss MOC (my most outrageous conjecture [https://gilkalai.wordpress.com/2026/10/14/the-most-outrageous-conjecture-in-the-world/]) with Peter, but years ago he told me that he does not see as a barrier for analytic number theory methods. Quite amazingly, Peter’s wife Helen Nissenbaum, a Professor of Information Science at Cornell Tech, has studied and written about the threats from computers to privacy and to society in general for three decades!

15:00 In the afternoon I met Maria Chudnovsky, who, besides her main position at Princeton, is a distinguished visiting scholar at the Hebrew University. For the past decade Maria has visited and given a short course at HUJI every summer. I first met Maria when she was a candidate for MSc studies.

We talked about various mathematical projects that we started in the past few years, and agreed to delegate some of the work to an AI tool. For example, we wrote a paper [https://arxiv.org/abs/2111.03228] about attempts to extend the notion of perfect graphs to hypergraphs, and had various ideas on how to find a topological characterization of perfectness in graphs. There is a nice topological characterization of chordality in graphs and we would like to find a similar characterization for the class of perfect graphs or for other related classes of graphs defined by properties of their induced subgraphs.

Overall Maria is rather optimistic about AI and Math. Naturally we also talked about Israel, New York City, and about antisemitism.

13:00 Over lunch I met my old friend Bernard Chazelle. We set a meeting for 1 on Oct 12, and I, not untypically, confused the date and the time and rushed to the place at 12:00. Bernard and I talked about fond (separate) memories of Yale, about the amazing emergence of computational geometry as a field in the early 80s (which I merely observed), and the connections with discrete geometry (in which I took part). Of course, AI+math and the situation in Israel occupied some of the conversation.

What we really planned to talk about was chaos, and the philosophical problem of free will [https://gilkalai.wordpress.com/2021/08/18/to-cheer-you-up-in-difficult-times-29-free-will-predictability-and-quantum-computers/]. When it comes to chaos, a major challenge is to connect the notion of chaos in nature with mathematics. Bernard once wrote to me: “the butterfly effect and strange attractors made the word chaos sexy but mathematically it’s always been a mess.” This time he told me a little about the related notion of robustness in biological systems.

09:30 In the morning, following a granola breakfast with Avi, Edna and Nati Linial, I met Nati Seiberg. Nati told me that he regards the problem of finding a rigorous mathematical framework for quantum field theory as very important, not only for mathematics but also for physics. (I found this view rather flattering for mathematics, and I don’t know to what extent it is shared by many physicists.) Nati referred me to a recent Simon collaboration [https://www.simonsfoundation.org/event/simons-collaboration-on-probabilistic-paths-to-quantum-field-theory-annual-meeting-2026/] on the topic and to his lecture [https://youtu.be/pTAQH501_2g?si=kOaYtcs4mBwlvp84] there.

We also talked (in admiration) about our common friend Eliezer Rabinovici, about AI and math/physics, about dualities, and a little about quantum computation.

Update: A major recent AI-assisted claim about the mathematical foundations of QFT.

A few days ago, OpenAI announced [https://openai.com/index/building-scientific-understanding-with-academic-communities/?fbclid=IwY2xjawVOh1VleHRuA2FlbQIxMQBwZG9mAWJyaWQRMTR6OU1oSlRiZUhneU1LOWpzcnRjBmFwcF9pZBAyMjIwMzkxNzg4MjAwODkyAAEemn6Onc6DwvjyL52EQiePsZriHfUyUIiQeGiZs_yubHQNgBtHW4ALcp0apxs_aem_Rgrh4SSBOOq9Yn-Z4CRQoQ] a major advance in putting quantum field theory (and specifically QCD) on rigorous mathematical foundations. This holds much promise, directly and indirectly, for physics.

Physicists in an Israeli Facebook community [https://www.facebook.com/groups/606544961404260/posts/1462274452497969?locale=he_IL] are not enthusiastic about the potential benefits for physics; also on FB mathematician Lior Silberman regards [https://www.facebook.com/LiorSilberman/posts/pfbid0f7qWbz3WK4QN56nQQcq3TieSLCFGk4XG3Vova5a1KoEBKKtFZyyhmiiz5tXqJ1GZl?locale=he_IL] it as the most important problem in mathematics, but other mathematicians strongly disagree.

I personally regard the new development as amazing, for mathematics, physics, and science, and in my view an important test for such a new mathematical theory is whether it allows quantum field theory computations to be performed on an ideal quantum computer.

Sunday Oct 11, New Brunswick – Princeton

18:30 A wonderful dinner with my beloved friends Avi and Edna, Noga, Nati, Elon and Abigail, and Irit and Hadar.

15:00 After lunch, I did what I regarded as the most deeply nostalgic thing of my entire trip. “Let’s go to visit Peter”, I suggested to Assaf Naor after lunch. Peter Sarnak lived two houses away, and this suggestion evoked nostalgia not for the 80s or 70s but for the 60s, when you would just knock on people’s doors without any prior arrangement. We tried, but Peter was not at home so I left a note, also something from the past.

12:00 It was great to meet Assaf Naor and his wonderful family for lunch. A memorable moment after lunch: “I am going now to sacrifice my rook”, said Assaf’s older son. This did not look like a good chess move to me but six moves later I encountered a sudden, humiliating checkmate.

On the hot matter of AI and Math, Assaf does not worry about AI capabilities; his long-term research agendas (some with Manor Mendel) are not harmed by the new AI papers, but he is rather pessimistic about the impact of AI on the mathematical community.

Assaf also told me that, in view of my always-positive style of writing, the net effect of my blog is that of hyping some mathematics (or mathematicians) that I like. (I really love criticism, and this criticism certainly has some truth to it.)

11:00 After a little morning walk in downtown New Brunswick (Semicolon Cafe), and further polishing my post on MOC, I took an Uber from New Brunswick to Assaf Naor’s house in Princeton. The driver (about ten years younger than me) had immigrated from Egypt decades ago, and arrived in the US as a young musician.

I expressed my admiration for music and he told me that he regards music, sex, and money as three elements of the human experience that you can enjoy even without the need for language. I opined that music does not even have the rather serious problems associated with sex and money. Of course, we talked a little about the Middle East situation and about Jewish and Moslem beliefs. He was unhappy about extremal voices by young members of his congregation. (We did not talk about AI and mathematics.)

Ran Raz’s 60th Birthday: Lower Bounds, Randomness, Space Complexity, and Quantum Computation [https://gilkalai.wordpress.com/2026/11/06/ran-razs-60th-birthday-lower-bounds-randomness-space-complexity-and-quantum-computation/]

Celebrating 100 Years: Avi 70 + CSDM 30 [https://gilkalai.wordpress.com/2026/11/02/celebrating-100-years-avi-70-csdm-30/]

 Posted on November 2, 2026 [https://gilkalai.wordpress.com/2026/11/02/celebrating-100-years-avi-70-csdm-30/] by Gil Kalai [https://gilkalai.wordpress.com/author/gilkalai/] 

Greetings everybody from Tel Aviv!

Irit Dinur and Amir Shpilka asked me to spread the word through my blog about the celebration for Avi Wigderson and for computer science and discrete mathematics at IAS.

Avi Fest @ IAS: Dates: June 14-18, 2027

 [https://gilkalai.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/100-poster.png]

The Institute for Advanced Study’s School of Mathematics is pleased to announce a conference celebrating Avi Wigderson’s 70th birthday and retirement, together with 30 years of the Computer Science and Discrete Mathematics program (CSDM) at IAS. The conference will honor both Avi’s extraordinary scientific legacy and his transformative role in shaping the intellectual community around theoretical computer science and discrete mathematics at IAS.

Progress on Borsuk’s Problem and Answer to TYI 60. [https://gilkalai.wordpress.com/2026/10/21/progress-on-borsuks-problem-and-answer-to-tyi-60/]

 Posted on October 21, 2026 [https://gilkalai.wordpress.com/2026/10/21/progress-on-borsuks-problem-and-answer-to-tyi-60/] by Gil Kalai [https://gilkalai.wordpress.com/author/gilkalai/] 

The Finite and the Infinite Borsuk problem

TYI(60) The question

Let f(d) be the smallest integer so that every set of diameter one in can be covered by f(d) sets of smaller diameter.

Let g(d) be the smallest integer so that every finite set of diameter one in can be covered by g(d) sets of smaller diameter.

Clearly g(d) ≤ f(d)

Test your intuition: Is g(d)=f(d) ?

TYI (60): my intuition

The answer is not known, but my own intuition is that g(d)<f(d) for every large d. In my lecture around the Borsuk Conjecture at Jeff Kahn’s fest I raised the conjecture that this is indeed the case. I also mentioned an interesting psychological fact: Over the years, both before and after I wrote my 2015 survey article on problems around Borsuk’s conjecture [https://arxiv.org/abs/1505.04952], I have encountered several incorrect proofs that g(d) = f(d). Often these proofs involve the De Bruijn–Erdős theorem [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_Bruijn%E2%80%93Erd%C5%91s_theorem_(graph_theory)], which asserts that an infinite graph with finite chromatic number has a finite subgraph with the same chromatic number. (This result is also an easy consequence of compactness in mathematical logic.) Some proofs also involve the claim that there is a constant c < 1 such that every set of diameter one in can be covered by g(d) sets of diameter at most c.

In my lecture at Jeff Kahn’s conference I mentioned that I am usually convinced by such proofs for a while, until the prover (and sometimes I) finds the mistake. This gave me extra motivation to firmly conjecture that for some dimensions (or even for almost all dimensions) g(d)<f(d). Sure enough, this happened again after my talk, once again I was convinced for a short while by proofs presented to me that g(d)=f(d).

Open AI’s proof that f(9)>10

One of the 700 or so papers released by Open AI on October 6, 2026 asserts that f(9)>10. This is a major progress on the Borsuk’s problem. (Of course, the proof needs to be carefully checked. The main theorem has also been formalized in Lean.)

The construction itself is one of the constructions Jeff and I studied—the set of matrices of the form where is a unit vector in . For , these are symmetric matrices of trace one, and therefore they lie in a nine-dimensional affine space. The distance between two such matrices is maximal precisely when the corresponding vectors are orthogonal.

What is new is the proof that this set cannot be covered by ten sets of smaller diameter. The proof is topological. You can regard it as related to the topological proof of the Borsuk–Ulam theorem, [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borsuk%E2%80%93Ulam_theorem]which implies that when you cover the unit sphere in with closed sets, one of these sets must contain a pair of antipodal points. The topology here looks more complicated and the proof looks very beautiful!

One of the reasons for my (shaky) intuition regarding g(d) < f(d) is that I do not see, for example, why the Borsuk–Ulam theorem or its proof implies (the easy fact) that there is a finite set of diameter one that cannot be covered by d+1 sets of smaller diameter.

 [https://gilkalai.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/borsukai.png]

A figure from the AI paper

Can transversality save Borsuk’s conjecture? And Gábor Damásdi’s characterization of spatial diameter graphs

In my lecture at Jeff Kahn’s conference, I mentioned several new developments around Borsuk’s conjecture. I will mention here one development from Gábor Damásdi’s October 2026 paper [https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.06340], which characterizes finite diameter graphs in three-dimensional space.

This work fits in a nice way with my attempt to save Borsuk’s conjecture using transversality. My conjecture applies to both finite and infinite sets, but for finite sets it has a simple formulation: If the diameter graph of a finite set in $\mathbb R^d$ is stress-free, then it is (d+1)-colorable. Here stress-free means that there is no nonzero assignment of equilibrium stresses to the edges of the diameter graph.

Gábor Damásdi proved that extremal diameter frameworks in three-dimensional space are stress-free. He also achieved a complete characterization of finite diameter graphs in three-dimensional space: they are precisely the subgraphs of non-bipartite quadrangulations of the projective plane (with at least one edge). Earlier, Yaakov Kupitz, Horst Martini, and Micha Perles showed that [//arxiv.org/abs/0905.1528] the graphs associated with extremal configurations in three dimensions (with (2n-2) diameter pairs) have a remarkable strong self-duality property, and they conjectured a converse that Damásdi has now proved and strengthened.

The Most Outrageous Conjecture in the World [https://gilkalai.wordpress.com/2026/10/14/the-most-outrageous-conjecture-in-the-world/]

 Posted on October 14, 2026 [https://gilkalai.wordpress.com/2026/10/14/the-most-outrageous-conjecture-in-the-world/] by Gil Kalai [https://gilkalai.wordpress.com/author/gilkalai/] 

 [https://gilkalai.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/kbr.png]

Donald Knuth, Enrico Bombieri, and Julia Robinson.

Ten years ago I asked a question on MathOverflow about The most outrageous (or ridiculous) conjectures in mathematics [https://mathoverflow.net/questions/259844/the-most-outrageous-or-ridiculous-conjectures-in-mathematics]. It led to a nice list of many outrageous conjectures. Here I would like to present what I regard as a serious contender for the most outrageous (ridiculous) conjecture. It is originated from the following MathOverflow quetion I asked in 2013 Infinitely many primes, and Mobius randomness in sparse sets [https://mathoverflow.net/questions/135948/infinitely-many-primes-and-mobius-randomness-in-sparse-sets], and it is closely related to some aspects of polymath4 [https://polymathprojects.org/2009/08/09/research-thread-ii-deterministic-way-to-find-primes/] (2009).

The most outragous conjecture (informal form): There is an inherent difficulty to prove infinitude of primes, and related number theoretic results (like Möbius randomness) for explicit, sparse sets of integers.

The terms “sparse,” “explicit,” and “inherent difficulty” are negotiable and we will give them the most outrageous interpretations.

By “sparse sets” we mean sets of integers that has less than elements smaller than , for every . For example, (i) integers with more than 90% ones in their binary expansion and (ii) integers of the form , where are integers. (iii) Unions of intervals of the form , where, say is a power of 2; here indeed the best known unconditional results is when . RH allows and it looks that for this question the is a viable barrier.

Several number theorists that I talked with do not regard the as a barrier for general problems and expect that for some problems (like items (i) and (ii)), current techniques could be pushed for density below .

Let me move now to a precise formulation. Before that, let me thank Lior Silberman for helpful comments and corrections and Andrew Granville and several other colleagues for helpful discussions over the years.

The most outrageous conjecture

We say that a sequence of integers is explicit if there is a deterministic polynomial time algorithm in to compute . For example, the sequences , and are explicit. The sequence of primes is for all we know not explicit.

The Most Outrageous Conjecture (MOC):

Let be an explicit set of integers. Suppose that for some constant ,

(1) It cannot be proved in ZFC that contains infinitely many primes.

(2) Möbius randomness for cannot be proved in ZFC.

(3) The assertion of the PNT for cannot be proved in ZFC.

Short further explanation

A priory, there could be three possibilities for the assertion that contains infinitely many primes: It could be proved in ZFC; it could be disproved in ZFC and it could be undecidable in ZFC. The conjecture asserts that the first possibility cannot occur for explicit sparse sequences. ZFC is the standard set of axioms [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zermelo%E2%80%93Fraenkel_set_theory] for mathematics.

Möbius randomness asserts that . The assertion of the Prime Number Theorem (PNT) is that the number of primes among behaves like [corrected]. (This is a stronger assertion compared to infinitude of primes.)

Claims b) – d), conjectured here to be undecidable, are not that far from established theorems:

b’) MOC asserts that proving PNT and MR for integers with more than 90% ones in their binary expansion is undecidable. Eric Naslund proved [https://arxiv.org/abs/1211.2455] Möbius randomness and infinitude of primes for the set of integers with at lease twice as many ones as zeros in their binary expansion. As far as I can speculate, the MOC holds even if the validity of GRH is assumed.

Computational complexity

MOC has also outrageous consequences in computational complexity. It implies that strong forms of the derandomization conjecture are undecidable. (I am comfortable with these conclusions but you can avoid these consequences by adopting a stricter form of “explicit”.)

A weak form

The main challenge is, of course, for number theorists to disprove MOC. This challenge may not be so hard because we have a large variety of sets to choose from and a large variety of number theoretic methods to choose from. On the other hand, proving MOC goes beyond known techniques for proving undecidability. It would be very interesting (but still beyond reach in present methods) to prove MOC just for a specific (non-artificial) sequence. Lior Silberman pointed out we can use Godel’s theorem to artificially create a sequence of integers for which, say, the infinitude of primes is undecidable.

Further Discussion

0. (Added, Oct. 17) Saharon Shelah raised related ideas in his 2002 paper Logical dreams [https://arxiv.org/abs/math/0211398]. (H/t to Harald Helfgott who told me about it.)

The conjecture can be regarded as a variation with a twist of an outrageous conjecture (or intuition) of Knuth discussed for example here: Knuth’s intuition that Goldbach might be unprovable [https://mathoverflow.net/questions/27755/knuths-intuition-that-goldbach-might-be-unprovable]. The twist is that refuting Knuth’s intuition would require proving Goldbach conjecture. Here number theorists can choose between a variety of sets and a variety of proof techniques and they can tailor the explicit set for this purpose. Similar assertions about undecidability of strong number theoretic conjectures were raised by several other researchers including Enrico Bombieri. In his interesting paper [https://mxphi.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/07/Granville.pdf] that we discussed in this post [https://gilkalai.wordpress.com/2024/06/07/andrew-granville-accepted-proofs-objective-truth-or-culturally-robust/], the number theorist Andrew Granville regards such conjectures as manifesting human hubris.

 As we already mentioned MOC has some consequences for computational complexity. It implies that strong forms of the derandomization conjecture are undecidable. These consequences can be avoided by pausing positing a stricter form of the term “explicit” that would still include all number theoretical statements that occurs “in nature”. (Our notion of “explicit” adds to the courageousness of the conjecture, but it will probably not make it easier to disprove.)

This conjecture (and the previous comment) is related to the interesting 2009 discussion in polymath4: a polymath project for finding a deterministic polynomial time algorithm (in logn) to find a prime number with n digits.

 As mentioned above, the question, in more moderate terms is raised in my MO question: Infinitely many primes, and Mobius randomness in sparse sets. [https://mathoverflow.net/questions/135948/infinitely-many-primes-and-mobius-randomness-in-sparse-sets] The question is related to other MO questions (such as Why so difficult to prove infinitely many restricted primes? [https://mathoverflow.net/questions/79113/why-so-difficult-to-prove-infinitely-many-restricted-primes] ). I first raised MOC in my lecture at Gowers’ 60th birthday conference (Here are somewhat edited slides [https://gilkalai.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/gowers60.pptx], and here is the recording [https://www.newton.ac.uk/seminar/42100/].)

There is a result [https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF01388985] by Noam Elkies about the infinitude of supersingular primes [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supersingular_prime_(algebraic_number_theory)]. Those primes are conjectured to be of density among the first numbers but provably it is only known that they are of density less or equal . I don’t know if there are Elkies-like results that can lead (provably or conjecturally) to sparser sets of primes.

There are results about primes and Möbius randomness in short and even very short intervals. Kaisa Matomäki and Maksym Radziwiłł wrote in 2015, a breakthrough paper Multiplicative functions in short intervals and initially I thought it can lead to a refutation of MOC. However, as far as I can see these startling results do not violate MOC because they are statistical: they talk about the expected behavior over many short intervals.

The philosophical point of view of MOC is that as far as ZFC is concerned the primes are “really” random.

The four problems in Landau’s famous list [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landau%27s_problems] of number theory conjectures, are (barely) above the barrier of MOC.

I did not try to use AI to find a counterexample.

Are the primes random?

There is a the famous 1936 Cramér model for predicting the behavior of primes based on a probabilistic model and his model led to the famous Cramér conjecture [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cram%C3%A9r%27s_conjecture] that asserts that . (It is undecidable by MOC.) MOC asserts that when it comes to ZFC the axioms are too weak to give very precise information about primes so with respect to various different models of ZFC the primes do behave as “random” objects.

Cramér model gives good coarse predictions but for precise predictions it is believed to be just a good starting point. The paper An uncertainty principle for arithmetic sequences [http://annals.math.princeton.edu/wp-content/uploads/annals-v165-n2-p06.pdf] by Andrew Granville and Kannan Soundararajan established an uncertainty principle which more-or-less shows that there is no formulation of Cramér type heuristics that can give completely correct predictions!

Why undecidability?

Beside undecidability, there are various mathematical ways to express the idea that proving certain mathematical statements is inherently beyond reach.

a) The proof is huge, there is no way a human (or AI) will be able to find and present such a proof.

b) There is a short proof but finding it is computationally intractable. There is no way a human (or AI) will find such a proof.

I am not aware mathematical methods for proving (a) or (b) for specific mathematical statements. For showing independence from ZFC (or other axioms) there are at least some available methods.

Of course it is quite possible that there is no inherent barrier at density whatsoever, and that to pass this barrier require more effort using known techniques or perhaps some new ideas.

Let me also mention that David Kazhdan referred to the question “why is mathematics possible [https://gilkalai.wordpress.com/2013/05/23/why-is-mathematics-possible/]” (in view of Gödel-type and NP-hard type obstacles) in a lovely 1999 essay Reflection on the development of mathematics in the twentieth century [https://gilkalai.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2013/05/kazhdan-math.pdf] .

As posed here the conjecture also implies that various claims in computational complexity are undecidable. (And this is closely related to the themes we discussed in polymath 4.) Readers who do not like this feature can replace “explicit” by a more modest definition that would still include all number theoretical statement that occurs “in nature”. But I actually like this feature and it implies that proving also several (albeit very strong) computational complexity claims are undecidable. (This adds to the outrageousness of the conjecture, but it will probably not make it easier to disprove.)

Equations in primes

There are far reaching theorems and conjectures regarding the number of solutions of equations in primes. The Hardy-Littlewood conjecture [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Hardy%E2%80%93Littlewood_conjecture] (and several generalizations) provides asymptotic estimates for the number of prime solutions for n linear equations in m variables. For such questions there seem to be a barrier in the available mathematical methods between m linear equations in n variables (for the primes) when n=m+1 and when n>m+1; in the later case the Hardy-Littlewood conjecture (even in a greater generality) was settled by Green, Tao, and Ziegler.
