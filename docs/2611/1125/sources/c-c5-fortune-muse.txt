Exclusive: Meta and Amazon discuss ending Muse shopping bot blockade, as ads, search emerge as key issues

By 
Sebastian Herrera [/author/sebastian-herrera/]
Sebastian Herrera
Tech Correspondent

By 
Sebastian Herrera [/author/sebastian-herrera/]

Amazon [https://fortune.com/company/amazon-com/] and Meta [https://fortune.com/company/facebook/] Platforms are in ongoing discussions over a potential deal to connect Meta’s popular Muse AI agent to the e-commerce giant’s online store and end a blockade of the shopping bot [https://fortune.com/2026/09/22/metas-muse-ai-is-exploding-in-popularity-and-drawing-heated-backlash/] imposed by Amazon two months ago.

Recommended Video

The two tech giants have held talks on multiple occasions, and two major factors being negotiated are how the companies will handle search results and advertising in any deal, according to people familiar with the matter. Discussions are fluid, and it’s unclear if or how soon an agreement will be struck. There are numerous issues, ranging from Amazon’s own AI shopping ambitions to how, exactly, Muse would connect to Amazon. A major threat Muse poses to Amazon is that, in theory, it would bypass advertisements on Amazon’s website, which are a large source of revenue for the retailer.

Amazon is worried about losing advertising revenue and about the quality of the product search results Muse would surface, and executives are also concerned that shopping agents like Muse will weaken the relationship Amazon has with consumers, people familiar with the matter said. Any deal between Amazon and Meta is expected to require sign-off from the CEOs of both companies, they noted.

Meta and Amazon declined to comment. The negotiations are being closely followed because they have major implications for the future of online shopping and represent an important test case for how large corporations respond to the advent of AI agents. As agents like Muse become more widely used, companies like Amazon are scrambling to understand how the bots alter typical shopping patterns as well as the valued connections [https://fortune.com/2026/11/16/the-next-customer-brands-need-to-win-over-isnt-human-and-it-doesnt-care-how-big-your-company-is/] that brands and retailers have with consumers.

The talks are ongoing as Amazon prepares for another Black Friday and Cyber Monday, one of its most critical shopping periods of the year. This year, roughly half of holiday shoppers plan to use AI in some way to help make purchases, a National Retail Federation survey this month showed.

AI agents promise to bring major changes to many industries. Agentic shopping [https://fortune.com/2026/06/12/ai-shopping-agents-are-coming-no-one-is-ready-for-them/] could total $190 billion to $385 billion in U.S. e-commerce spending by 2030, representing 10% to 20% of the total market, according to a 2025 report by Morgan Stanley [https://fortune.com/company/morgan-stanley/]. Analysts at JPMorgan have praised Muse as a powerful agent, recently writing that tens of millions of businesses could eventually use the tool. 

Amazon blocked Meta’s Muse app from accessing its store in September, saying at the time that Muse’s access would violate its user agreements. Muse, which Meta released in the U.S. that month, is a personalized AI agent that users can name and direct to autonomously carry out tasks on their behalf, such as checking email, managing calendars, and booking reservations. As of October, more than 10 million people [https://fortune.com/2026/10/22/mark-zuckerberg-meta-muse-stripe-credit-limit-shopping/] in the U.S. and Canada had set up the app, making it Meta’s biggest consumer AI hit to date. Chief executive Mark Zuckerberg has said shopping is Muse’s top use case.

“We think that billions of Muses in a network at scale will be able to get more done for people and businesses by working together,” Zuckerberg said last month [https://fortune.com/company/facebook/earnings/q3-2026/] on a call with analysts. “We’d like to create a large-scale Muse network as quickly as possible.”

One of Amazon’s main concerns is how shopping agents such as Muse will affect its advertising business. Advertising has become a $68.6 billion business for Amazon, representing nearly 10% of its total yearly revenue. In October, Amazon chief executive Andy Jassy, while not naming Muse, tried to quell investor concern by saying few customers are buying with outside shopping agents [https://fortune.com/2026/10/29/amazon-ceo-says-shopping-agents-like-metas-muse-not-great/] because the experience is “still not great, nor more convenient than coming to a retailer directly.”

Still, Jassy said this month at a Semafor conference that Amazon hadn’t ruled out letting outside shopping agents into its store, but that they would have to provide a “fair exchange of value.”

Aside from Meta, OpenAI launched its Dots [https://fortune.com/2026/09/29/openai-takes-on-metas-muse-with-new-dot-agents-and-unveils-a-potential-google-workspace-competitor/]personal AI agents in September, and startup Instinct’s personal assistant has also become a mainstream AI agent. Amazon has its own shopping agent for its website, dubbed “Alexa for shopping,” which Jassy said has been used by more than 400 million customers in the past year.

Meta and Amazon have partnered on e-commerce before. In 2023, the companies struck a deal that allowed Facebook and Instagram users to purchase Amazon products without leaving the apps. Meta makes money from ads it serves users on its apps, including for Amazon products, and Amazon earns revenue through the actual purchases. The partnership was made as TikTok expanded into U.S. e-commerce.

Meta signed on other partners early in its Muse efforts, including Amazon rivals such as Shopify, Walmart [https://fortune.com/company/walmart/], and Best Buy [https://fortune.com/company/best-buy/]. Thus, retailers have gained another channel for selling products as Meta tries to grow Muse, and according to Zuckerberg, the company eventually plans to profit by taking a “small fee from transactions.”
