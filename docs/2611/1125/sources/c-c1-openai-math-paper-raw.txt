           Superpolynomial graph diameter
                 of simple polytopes
                                             OpenAI

                                            Abstract

     We disprove the polynomial Hirsch conjecture. For every sufficiently large n, we
     construct a bounded simple rational polytope with exactly n facets whose graph
     diameter grows faster than every fixed power of n. Its dimension grows more slowly
     than every positive power of n. For any prescribed subpolynomial s(n), two vertices
     can remain superpolynomially far apart even when a single step may cross any face
     of dimension at most s(n).


1    Introduction
How many edges must one traverse to get from one vertex of a polytope to another? In 1957
Hirsch proposed that a d-dimensional polyhedron with n facets should require at most n − d
steps. Santos disproved this bound for bounded polytopes by constructing an example of
dimension 43 with 86 facets and two vertices at least 44 steps apart [12]. That example left open
the possibility that some polynomial in n bounds all such distances. We construct bounded
polytopes whose diameter grows faster than every fixed power of their facet count.
    The diameter is the maximum, over all pairs of vertices, of the length of a shortest path in
the undirected vertex–edge graph. Our examples are simple, meaning that every vertex lies on
exactly d facets. We can even allow a move directly between any two vertices of a common
face of dimension at most s. Write dists (v, w) for the minimum number of these moves, so that
dist1 is the usual graph distance. For a positive function of n, the notations no(1) and nω(1)
mean that the ratio of its logarithm to log n tends to zero and to infinity, respectively.

Theorem 1.1 (Superpolynomial diameter). For each positive integer-valued s(n) = no(1) there
is a family of bounded simple rational polytopes Pn , one for every sufficiently large n, with
exactly n facets, dimension no(1) , and s(n)-move diameter nω(1) .
    Moreover, there is a linear functional x on each Pn , of positive range ∆x = maxPn x −
minPn x, such that for every fixed 0 < α < 1,

    Taking s(n) = 1 disproves the polynomial Hirsch conjecture. The objective estimate explains
where the long distances occur. On the same polytope, every pair of vertices separated by even
a fraction n−α of the objective range requires a long walk. Intermediate vertices may take any
objective values.
    There is also a bound with both the dimension d and the facet excess E = n − d prescribed.
This includes the classical d-step case E = d, as well as regimes in which the two parameters
grow at very different rates.

1.1    Diameter and linear programming
The simplex method gives Hirsch’s question an algorithmic interpretation. Starting from a
feasible vertex, a pivot can move to an adjacent vertex. Even complete knowledge of the
destination cannot shorten a walk below the graph distance. General LP algorithms have other
choices. In the companion paper [10], a strongly polynomial algorithm finds an optimum by
recovering its primal and dual certificates from the inequalities. Corollary 14.3 makes this
distinction concrete. It gives packing linear programs in which the origin and the all-ones vector
are the unique minimizer and maximizer of the sum of the coordinates, yet every edge path
between these explicit endpoints has superpolynomial length.
    Dantzig recorded Hirsch’s original question in his treatment of linear programming [1,
p. 160]. Klee and Walkup reduced the linear conjecture to the case n = 2d and gave an
unbounded counterexample [5]. For bounded polytopes, Matschke, Santos and Weibel brought
the dimension of Santos’s counterexample down to 20 [8]. Davies and coauthors constructed
five-dimensional prismatoids whose width grows linearly in their number of vertices [2]. The
polynomial conjecture asks for a single polynomial valid in all dimensions [12, Conjecture 1.3].
    Larman’s upper bound n 2O(d) shows that a superpolynomial diameter forces d/ log n to tend
to infinity [6]. Theorem 1.1 places the examples within this range while keeping d smaller than
every positive power of n. The Kalai–Kleitman bound and its refinements give quasipolynomial
upper bounds as d varies [4, 14, 13]. Liu, Ge and Ye also obtain a quasipolynomial upper bound
in the facet excess alone [7]. Our construction concerns actual convex polytopes. It complements
work on combinatorial models of diameter, including connected layer families [3]. By polarity,
the examples also give rational simplicial polytopes whose facets have superpolynomial diameter
under adjacency across ridges [15].

1.2    Why distances multiply
The construction uses a linear coordinate x to measure progress through a polyhedron. We call
x time, although a path can move in either direction in x. The elementary way to force a bend
is to give J + 1 independent coordinates zi the lower bounds zi ≥ |x − a|. At a fixed x, a point
in a face of the slice of dimension at most J must attain at least one bound. In particular, some
zi = 0 when the path reaches x = a. If an affine segment crossed that level in its interior, its zi
coordinate would give a line through the corner lying above both branches of |x − a|. Figure 1
shows why this is impossible. A convex piecewise affine lower bound with many corners forces
a separate bend at each one.

    To make the cost grow faster than the number of inequalities, we place a copy of an expensive
crossing inside each of many disjoint intervals of x. Within one such interval, called an episode,
a pair of auxiliary coordinates records progress by its difference w. A pair can be chosen for
which w crosses from near zero to near one. Several disjoint triples of smaller intervals are
marked on the w-axis. On a traversal of a triple, either part of the path describes an entire
inner crossing, with w as its time, or the path crosses an overlap of two phases in x. We put
another copy of the test in each such overlap. Figure 2 shows the two time coordinates. The
last overlaps in this recursion use the piecewise affine lower bounds above.
    The lower bound for the inner crossing is counted repeatedly. For a sufficiently large crossing
cost D, one level with N episodes and q triples per episode forces at least N qD/4 segments. To
count them on an undirected walk, for each interval stop at the first hit of its far endpoint and
look back to the last hit of its near endpoint. The selected subpaths occupy disjoint intervals
of the path parameter. Even two visits to the same physical overlap can force different bends
(Figure 3).
    The cost in facets is a separate matter. Giving every inner crossing its own variables
and inequalities would spend the very factor the construction gained in distance. The copies
instead use common clock readings. Different copies on the same bank of clocks can contribute
tight inequalities only in disjoint reading intervals. Eliminating the readings therefore requires
combinations containing at most one row from an inner copy. When a coordinate needed for
that copy is missing, a prescribed group of slacks can take its place. The scheduler is the system
of inequalities that makes the required slacks available at the appropriate times. It must do
this for many episodes using few coordinates.
    Section 2 converts a finite crossing bound into Theorems 1.1 and 1.2, including the passage
to bounded simple rational polytopes with an exact facet count. Sections 3–6 establish the
crossing bound using the scheduler. Its numerical input is Theorem 7.1, which assigns states
to successive times subject to a coordinatewise inequality between neighbors. Sections 7–10
construct these states and prove the inequality. Sections 11–13 turn them into the scheduler’s
linear constraints. Section 14 gives the consequences for complementary vertices, Minkowski
sums and packing linear programs.

2     From crossings to diameter
A move in a face can be drawn as a straight chord. We force a path of such chords to change
direction many times as it crosses the level sets of a linear functional. The construction
starts with a polyhedron Q in coordinates (x, z) ∈ R × Rp . We call x the time and write
Q(x) = {z : (x, z) ∈ Q}. A crossing of a closed interval is a polygonal path in Q that joins
its two endpoint slices and stays between them. Its time coordinate may reverse direction
any number of times. A segmentation partitions the parameter interval into affine pieces. Its
interior partition points are the breaks.

Theorem 2.1 (Finite-scale crossing bound). Fix an integer K ≥ 2 and 0 < η < 1. There are
an integer L ≥ 2 and a constant γ > 0 with the following property. For every sufficiently large
power N = Lh , every integer R ≥ 1, and every choice of N pairwise disjoint nondegenerate
closed intervals in (0, 1), there is a polyhedron Q defined by F inequalities of the form c · z ≥ ℓ(x),
with c ≥ 0 nonzero and ℓ affine. Each coordinate of z has an individual lower bound among
these rows, and
                                   p + 1 ≤ N η,     F + 3 ≤ RN 1+η .
Set k = ⌊N γ ⌋. Suppose that along a crossing, at every point (x, z), the smallest face of Q(x)
containing z has dimension at most k + 1. Every segmentation of a crossing of [0, 1] has at
least RN K pieces. Every segmentation of a crossing of one of the prescribed intervals has at
least RN K−1 interior breaks. Both bounds hold in either orientation.

    Section 6 proves Theorem 2.1 using the scheduler of Section 3. The distinction between
pieces and interior breaks matters when we add the costs of separate intervals. A break inside
a trimmed crossing is still a break of the original path.

3     Crossings and the scheduler
Fix a linear coordinate x, which we call time. We want to force many changes of direction on a
path from one time level to another. The basic observation is that a point on a face of dimension
at most J admits at most J independent two-sided directions. The recursive construction uses
this observation twice. It first constrains a path in an outer polyhedron, then reads a path in
an inner polyhedron from slacks of the outer inequalities. Only some inner coordinates can be
varied independently through these slacks. We record which sets of coordinates suffice for the
next crossing.

3.1    Blocking inequalities and crossings
A blocking system Q consists of finitely many inequalities

                                  c · z ≥ ℓ(x),     c ≥ 0,   c ̸= 0,                              (1)

with affine right-hand sides. It contains an individual lower bound for each coordinate of z. A
convex piecewise affine right-hand side stands for all the inequalities given by its affine pieces.
Rows are indexed, so that equal inequalities with different indices retain their identities. The
support of a row is {i : ci > 0}, and its slack at (x, z) is c · z − ℓ(x).
    Write Q(x) for the slice at time x. For a set I of coordinates, restrict the system by keeping
only coordinates in I and rows supported on I. The restricted slice is exactly the projection of
Q(x) onto I. Indeed, a feasible vector on I extends to Q(x) by taking every omitted coordinate
equal to a sufficiently large number. Each discarded row has a positive coefficient on at least
one such coordinate, and there are only finitely many rows. The same argument gives a strictly
feasible point in every restricted slice. Its individual lower bounds exclude lines.
    A crossing of [a, b] is a continuous polygonal path in Q whose time coordinate starts at a,
ends at b, and stays in [a, b]. It may reverse direction in time. A segmentation is a finite partition
of the path’s parameter interval on each part of which the whole path is affine. Constant pieces
are allowed. A break is an interior partition point. On a subpath we count only breaks strictly
inside its parameter interval, so that neighboring traversals cannot charge a common endpoint
twice.

3.2    Which coordinates suffice?
Consider a coordinate i whose role in the crossing argument can also be fulfilled by two other
coordinates j, k together. The usable sets are those containing i or containing both j and k.
More generally a coordinate may have several alternatives, each with further alternatives of its
own.
     A signature T is a finite rooted tree. Its height is the largest number of edges on a root-to-leaf
path. Each edge γ out of the root is an option, with signature Tγ given by the subtree rooted
at its child. A bank is an occurrence of a signature. A program on a bank of signature T makes
a finite list of direct requests. For each such request i and each option γ, it specifies a program
on a fresh bank of signature Tγ , the upgrade of i along γ. Lists may be empty. Each request
has its own identity and indexes one coordinate.
     Call a set I of requests viable if for every direct request i, either i ∈ I or some upgrade
of i is itself viable for I. At a bank with no options all direct requests must belong to I. An
empty program is viable for every I. In the example above i has a single upgrade, the terminal
list j, k. Adding coordinates preserves viability. We may combine programs at one bank by
concatenating disjoint direct lists and keeping each request’s upgrades. The combined program
is viable precisely when each component program is viable.
     One useful program makes one direct request at the root and repeats this prescription in
every upgrade, down to the leaves of T . It has one request per node. A set I is viable precisely
when it contains at least one of these requests. Thus J + 1 disjoint copies at the same bank
require J + 1 distinct usable coordinates. If these coordinates occur only in individual lower
bounds and all those bounds are slack, their J + 1 coordinate directions are independent two-
sided motions. This is how the construction in Section 6 forces an equality on a low-dimensional
face.

Definition 3.1 (Admissibility). Let J ≥ 0 be an integer and let Q be a blocking system on
the requests of a program. At a feasible point (x, z), form the matrix C whose rows are the
coordinate normals c of the tight inequalities. The point is admissible if there exists a set I of
usable coordinates such that
   1. I is viable for the program,
   2. every tight row is supported on I, and
   3. dim ker(C|I ) ≤ J, where C|I retains the columns in I.
A crossing is admissible if each point is admissible, with I allowed to vary from point to point.

   At fixed x a direction is feasible for small moves in both signs precisely when it annihilates
every tight coordinate normal. The two-sided directions therefore form the translation space of
the smallest face of Q(x) containing z. A point of a slice face of dimension at most J meets
Definition 3.1 with I equal to the full coordinate set. For the inner path we can instead use a
smaller viable set. All tight rows are still tested in the full system. The nullity bound then
counts the two-sided directions supported on I.
   To count the cost of substituting slacks, also consider the forest whose vertices are requests.
The children of a request are all the direct requests in all of its upgrades. The span of a set of
requests is the cardinality of its union with all its ancestors in this forest. The span of a row is
that of its support. When several supports have common ancestors, their union counts those
ancestors only once.

3.3    Forcing equality at selected times
The scheduler is a blocking system for a prescribed list of time intervals. Its input includes
a program π, called the client. For each index P ∈ {1, . . . , B}, called a bucket, the scheduler
supplies a group of distinguished red rows for each client request i. A tight row in the group
(P, i) certifies that i is usable for bucket P .
    Each interval has two endpoints, its anchors, and one interior probe per bucket. At either
anchor, only a few buckets can have any nearly tight red row. If the number of buckets exceeds
twice this per-anchor bound, then for any two feasible points at the anchors there is a bucket
with every red row slack at both points. Near its probe, the tight red rows at a vertex certify
a viable set of client requests. This probe conclusion survives restriction to a viable set of
scheduler coordinates and small perturbations of the right-hand sides.

4     Coupling the clocks to the scheduler
An episode has two time coordinates. The physical time x locates the anchors and probes of
the scheduler. Within one bucket the difference w of two clock readings will serve as time for
an inner crossing. We build a single blocking system, the driver, in which that inner crossing
can be read from either clock vector or from the red-row slacks. The readings themselves will
be determined by the clock vectors. Eliminating the readings preserves the rows tight on a
path, the data needed to transfer a crossing in Section 5.

5     Transferring a crossing to an inner system
Fix a bucket of a driver and restrict physical time to 0 ≤ x ≤ 1. Its canonical readings s, u give
three feasible inner points with the same time w = s − u, one for each of QA , QC , QR in (3).
To use an inner crossing bound, we must also transfer admissibility and control the number
of segments in the resulting path. The two clock vectors use coordinates of the driver itself.
The resource vector uses minima of scheduler slacks, so its admissibility requires a separate
dimension argument.

5.1    Transferring admissibility
Lemma 5.1 (Clock readings). At an admissible driver point, inactivity of the bucket implies
                                    |w − (a(x) − c(x))| ≤ 2C∗ ρ.
If every coordinate of A exceeds a(x), the bucket is active and (w, zA ) is admissible for QA . The
corresponding assertion holds for C, c(x), QC . For 1/4 ≤ w ≤ 3/4, the A assertion applies in
the early phase and the C assertion in the late phase.
Proof. Fix a usable set certifying admissibility in Definition 3.1. Its coordinates in each clock
copy form a viable set for that copy, of size greater than J. If the bucket were inactive and
every A floor were slack, no tight driver row would involve A. The usable A coordinates alone
would then supply more than J independent kernel directions. Thus in an inactive bucket some
A floor is attained, and likewise some C floor is attained. The two minima equal a(x) and
c(x), respectively. These are below the cap T∗ , so subtracting the two estimates in (4) gives the
reading bound.
    Now suppose that every A floor is slack. The bucket is active. Every tight QA row occurs in
a tight coupling row by Lemma 4.2. The multiplier of its A normal is positive and its support
is therefore usable. Take a variation on the usable A coordinates in the common kernel of these
inner normals and extend it by zero on all other coordinates. It annihilates each tight driver
row. Indeed, the only tight rows that can involve A are coupling rows of this bucket, and their
A parts are zero or positive multiples of tight QA normals. The inner kernel thus has dimension
at most J, as required. The proof for C is the same with the two clocks interchanged.
    In the early phase a − c = 0. If an A floor is attained, s ≤ a + C∗ ρ, while u ≥ c − C∗ ρ
always. Hence w ≤ 2C∗ ρ < 1/4. In the late phase a − c = 1. Attainment of a C floor would
give w ≥ 1 − 2C∗ ρ > 3/4.

The three transfers are continuous. Even on a straight segment of the driver, the red row

Lemma 5.3 (Two-chord straightening). Fix a transfer which applies throughout an affine
segment of admissible driver points with the bucket active. Its inner endpoints can be joined by
an admissible path preserving the reading at every parameter. One segment suffices for a clock
transfer and at most two for a resource transfer.

Proof. Choose a coupling row tight at the midpoint. Its affine slack on the driver segment is
nonnegative and vanishes there, hence vanishes on the entire segment. Its two upper equalities
determine s, u affinely by Lemma 4.2. Thus w and both clock images are affine. The assumed
clock transfer certifies admissibility pointwise, so a clock image itself is the desired segment.
    For the resource transfer each coordinate of µ is a minimum of affine functions along the
segment. Therefore each coordinate of zR is concave. Every resource row has nonnegative
coordinate normal and its right-hand side is affine in w, so its slack along the resource image is
a nonnegative concave function. Such a function vanishing at any interior parameter vanishes
everywhere. Thus all interior points of the image have precisely the tight rows of its midpoint,
and both endpoints satisfy these rows with equality.
    Replace the image by two chords, one joining each endpoint to the midpoint. They are
feasible because the inner system is convex in the joint variables (w, zR ). On the open part of
each chord an affine nonnegative slack is zero exactly when it is zero at both ends of that chord.
These common equalities are precisely the midpoint’s tight rows. Its usable set certifies the two
open chords, and the three retained points keep their original certificates. Since w is affine, the
chords preserve it pointwise.

   One chord can introduce additional tight rows. For example, take a, b, c ≥ 0, a + b ≥ 1,
J = 0, and a program for which any one of the three coordinates is viable (Section 3). On the
broken line
                            (1, 0, 0) −→ (1, 1, 0) −→ (0, 1, 0)
the endpoints are vertices and every interior point has only the equality c = 0. With the bend
halfway along the parameter interval, the image is coordinatewise concave. The direct chord
between the endpoints would impose a + b = 1 as well as c = 0. Their supports require all three
coordinates to be usable, yet the two normals have nullity one on these coordinates. Retaining
the middle point avoids this additional equality.

The cost of a work window. Suppose a driver subpath meets both reading endpoints of a
work window and its transfer applies over the portion used to cross. Orient the subpath so it
visits the near endpoint first. Stop at the first far endpoint visit after that, and begin at the
last near endpoint visit before the stopping time. By continuity the trimmed reading remains
in the closed window. Restrict the given segmentation to this subpath and apply Lemma 5.3 to
each resulting segment. The replacements agree at their endpoints. Throughout the window,
Lemma 4.1 identifies the overlay with the input by an affine translation and time change. This
preserves the coordinate normals of the tight rows, their supports, and the request program, so
the resulting polygonal path is an admissible input crossing. It has at most as many segments
as the trimmed driver path for a clock transfer, and at most twice that number for a resource
transfer. The input requires D∗ segments. In either case at least D∗ /2 − 1 breaks of the given
segmentation lie strictly inside the trimmed path. Cutting at its two endpoints contributes no
breaks to this count.

7.1    Binary terms and a one-scale marking lemma

8    Directional coverage and small cuts
Lemma 7.2 requires frequent occurrences on the micro layers and, from each blank term, a
path along which all occurrences lie at a single other site. To see how the two requirements fit
together, assign each micro term a color and associate it with one blank term on each shore of
its site. These two blank terms are its keys. An outcome selects a color and opens certain keys
in that color. An occurrence requires a term of the selected color with both keys open.
    Follow the outward spine from a blank term. If it encounters an occurrence, then at that
site it also encounters an open key in its direction of travel. It suffices, from the first such key,
to find a continuation to a leaf whose moves charge no open key outside that site. The rules
below encode this condition in a graph that compresses each straight run of parent steps into
one move. A move charges its direction key if the run could contain a term of the selected
color. Thus every occurrence encountered on the run forces a charge to an open key in the
occurrence’s site.
    The proof builds the rules on a nested sequence of intervals. Within one interval and for
a fixed color, a collection of successful keys covers a test if it is charged on every short path
towards one end of the interval. Low probability of coverage supplies the desired continuations.
This section bounds the weight of the small sets of keys that can cover such a test and orders
8. Directional coverage and small cuts                                                            26


typical pairs of those sets. In Section 9 that order will allow the preferred direction to change
as the interval grows. Section 10 realizes the rules on terms using a finite probability space.

8.1    Moves, colors and path tests
The sites are the integers from 1 to L0 , in their usual order. We work in a directed acyclic
graph of bounded outdegree. The graph may be infinite. Write m(v) for the site of a node v. A
node is either a micro node or a blank node on the west or east shore of its site. The blank
nodes are also called keys. We say that u reaches v if there is a finite directed path from u to v,
allowing the empty path. We call every node reachable from u an ancestor of u.
    A blank leaf has no moves. From any other blank node there are two basic moves. The
outward move leads to a blank on the facing shore of the adjacent site, and the inward move
leads to a micro node in the same site. Thus a blank node with no neighboring site across its
shore is a leaf. Each micro node has a basic move in either direction, leading to a key on the
corresponding shore of its own site, its direction key. It may also have jumps in that direction to
other micro nodes in its site. Every such jump preserves the direction key. A spine follows basic
moves in one geographical direction until it reaches a leaf or leaves the interval being tested. At
most three of its moves originate at a given site, and it visits at most one micro node there.
    The graph is revealed in rounds. All micro nodes have the same color in round zero. At each
later round, a deterministic rule assigns a shore to each old color. A micro node appends to its
old color a datum computed at its key on that shore. Once these colors have been calculated,
the round supplies flags and witness jumps subject to the following rules.
   1. For each micro node, direction and color there is a flag. When the flag is true, every move
      from that node in that direction charges its direction key in the specified color. All other
      moves carry no charge in that color. In particular a blank node never charges a key.
   2. A true flag in a refined color requires a true flag in the same direction for its projection to
      the preceding round.
   3. Each true flag has a witness of the specified color, either the micro node itself or a micro
      node reached by one jump in that direction. The round adds at most one such jump per
      node, direction and color. All earlier moves remain available.
All flags, witnesses and calculated colors are independent of the randomness used for the gates.
In the application to free terms, a basic move from a micro node compresses travel through
several micro layers. Its flags record the colors encountered there, and the witness jumps recover
individual occurrences. A refinement can add paths and can remove charges when an old path
is read in the refined color. At a charging move we may always use the basic move to the
charged key instead, with the same charge. Alternatively we may reach the witness from the
source of the charging move in at most one move.
    For a cluster (an interval of sites), a west test at v to depth e consists of all paths from v
inside the cluster to a leaf or through their first west exit. The number of moves originating
at any one site is at most e, including the exiting move. We also use depth vectors, with a
separate bound for each site. East tests are defined by reflection. A set of keys covers a test in
a color if every tested path charges some key in the set in that color. Random coverage means
coverage by the keys whose gates in that color succeed. Each test of finite depth is finite, since
path length is bounded by the sum of the depths and branching is bounded.
    Two observations account for the depth bounds below. A depth is charged to the source
of a move. Thus replacing a charging jump by the basic move to its direction key preserves
8. Directional coverage and small cuts                                                         27


both the charge and the depth spent at each site. Also, suppose a path prefix from v to u
stays in the cluster, avoids charges to a set B, and spends at most bx moves at each site x. If
B covers the west test at v to depth vector f ≥ b, then B covers the west test at u to depth
f − b. Prepending the prefix to a missed path at u would give a missed path at v. This applies
whatever directions the prefix follows. Enlarging a depth only enlarges the set of test paths.
Thus a covering set also covers every shallower test, and a path witnessing failure of coverage
persists in every deeper test.
    A rule at a key is local if it reads a bounded outgoing unfolding at that key, with the types
of the nodes, their sites, flags, labeled move slots and the equality relation on nodes in the
unfolding. The radius may depend on the parameters of the construction. A rule refining a
color sees only flags and moves already supplied. The independent random bits read by a gate
are indexed by keys or bounded nonempty sets of keys in the unfolding, with auxiliary indices
such as stages and colors. Different appearances of the same index refer to the same bit. Both
the radius and the number of slots are uniformly bounded once the parameters are fixed.

Lemma 8.2 (Joint screened successes). If k ≤ K distinct keys can all have screened success in

9     Changing the direction of a test
Fix one stage and orient the preceding color toward the west as in Section 8. To extend the
coverage bound t to the larger cluster, we must choose a direction for each refined color. The
choice depends on the cuts covering the west test. If their total weight on a suitable list is
small, we keep testing west. If the weight is large, we switch east.
    Two facts make the switch possible. First, only a small weight of west cuts can also be
charged along the east spine. Most cuts can therefore be carried forward along that spine.
Second, among the cuts that persist, one can select a cut D and many newer cuts whose keys
all strictly reach those of D (Lemma 8.5). An east test that succeeds must pass barrier bits
shared by the newer cuts. Except on a rare event, it must also succeed from a key of D, where
none of those bits can be read. Their independence gives a contraction for east coverage.

10     One finite probability space for all layer lengths
Lemma 9.5 assigns random bits to keys and to sets of keys in a directed acyclic graph. A term
of Section 7 can have arbitrarily many distinct ancestors. Assigning a fresh bit to each ancestor
would make the probability space depend on the term. We instead index the bits by finite
descriptions of keys, called names. Two keys with the same name receive the same bits. We
choose the depth of the descriptions at random. At each fixed key, with high probability the
equalities between names in its neighborhood are exactly the equalities of keys in some legal
graph. This local guarantee will suffice both for the occurrence probability and for the path in
Lemma 7.2.

10.1     Recognizing a legal neighborhood
Fix the parameters of the gate construction. The unfolding at a key to radius R is the rooted
tree of its move sequences of length at most R. A named unfolding records the node data, the
available labeled move slots and an equivalence relation on the occurrences, namely equality of
names. At each fixed depth there are only finitely many such patterns up to renaming, since
branching, slots and node data are bounded.
    A color refinement is a function of a finite pattern from earlier rounds. Give the function
an arbitrary default on patterns where it was undefined, proceeding through the rounds in
order. Extend the local prescriptions of raw, barrier and screen tests to all patterns in the same
manner. These extensions use only equality of names and preserve the bit indices on realizable
patterns.

Lemma 10.1 (Local realizability). For every R there exists R′ ≥ R such that the radius-R
restriction of a named unfolding to radius R′ is realized in the graph model of Section 8 if it
passes the following checks.
   1. Equal names have the same node data. Whenever a move slot is exposed at two occurrences
      of one name, it has the same target name.
   2. The basic move rules and the prefix and witness rules for flags hold wherever the neigh-
      borhoods needed to test them are exposed. In these checks the colors are calculated by the
      extended refinement rules.
   3. Identifying equal names in the graph of exposed moves creates no directed cycle.

Proof. If no such radius exists, there are counterexamples of arbitrarily large radii. By successive
extraction, stabilize their patterns at each finite depth. In the limiting tree identify equivalent
occurrences. The first check makes each move well defined on the quotient. A directed cycle
would use finitely many exposed moves and name equalities, contradicting the third check at a
finite depth.
    The second check gives the basic moves. Suppose the first rounds of colors and flags
already satisfy the rules of the graph model. These rounds admit a legal completion by
making all subsequent flags false. The next refinement reads only the completed earlier rounds,
so its extended value is its specified value on the graph. The second check now gives the
prefix and witness conditions for the next flags. Induction produces a legal graph, possibly
infinite, whose root realizes the stabilized radius-R pattern. This contradicts the choice of the
counterexamples.

10.2     Giving names to the keys of a term
The two blank layers of each site are its shores. A nonleaf blank term has its two parent steps
as basic moves. Attach two temporary blank leaves to each micro leaf, one on each shore of its
site. From a micro term, a straight run of parent steps in either direction now reaches a first
blank term, its direction key. Compress this straight run, or leg, into one basic move to its key.
A witness jump in the same direction will end at a strictly older micro term on the leg, with
the same direction key. When we return to the original terms we stop a path at the micro leaf
preceding a temporary blank.
    Choose a radius j uniformly among the integers N∗ , . . . , 2N∗ − 1. In round zero this range
is a single bin. In each later round subdivide every preceding bin into equally many consecutive
bins of equal size. We first choose the subdivision counts, then take N∗ sufficiently large and
divisible by the number of final bins. Each bin in a round has its own flags and witness slots.
    To define names, retain the moves and flags for all bins, the site, the node type and an
auxiliary symbol. The j-name of a term is the tree of its moves truncated after j levels, with
these data through depth j. It records neither identities of terms nor the positions of micro
layers within a site. Once the parameters and subdivision counts are fixed, the number of
outgoing move slots is bounded. Choose a finite ordered alphabet larger than the number of
strict ancestors that can be reached in 2N∗ enriched moves. Each term takes the first symbol
absent at those ancestors. The symbol is part of its name, but the local rules use names only
for equality. In particular, the check radius R′ was fixed before choosing the auxiliary alphabet.
    Process terms in order of construction, with temporary blanks first and parents before their
merges.
   • A blank term has known outgoing moves. Assign its symbol, form its names and compute
     every round’s refinement outputs for every j. Use equality of j-names and the bins
     containing j in the previous rounds. Call a round’s bin stable at the key if the vector of
     outputs for all input colors is constant on that bin. Round zero is stable.
   • In a bin a micro term is live if both direction keys are stable in that bin and in every
     containing bin of earlier rounds. Its calculated color is then constant on the bin. On
     each leg set flags for exactly the colors of the live micro terms encountered, including
     the source. Witness each flag by the first such occurrence of its color. If the source is
     the witness, no jump is needed. Otherwise the corresponding slot jumps to the witness.
     Assign the source its auxiliary symbol after all these moves have been determined.
In the second step the source’s liveness and color are calculated from its two keys, both strictly
older, so the specification is recursive. Every witness keeps the same direction key along the leg.
Its liveness in the preceding bin supplies the flag for its prefix color.

13.4    Extending a probe to a band
We use two perturbations of the pruned system. The first ensures that the rows certifying
client viability belong to a vertex basis. The second makes the tight rows independent at every
feasible point. A basis at a fixed restriction is a set of rows whose normals on the retained
coordinates form a basis of the coordinate space. Its equality solution is a vertex if feasible,
and every vertex has such a basis among its tight rows.
     At the probes choose independently small positive raises of all red constants, the same
raises for every probe and restriction. Exclude the values for which a basis solution satisfies an
additional red equality outside that basis. For any given basis and additional row, these values
lie on a proper affine hyperplane since the additional red constant varies independently of the
basis solution. There are only finitely many such exclusions. At every feasible basis of a viable
restriction, the tight red rows certifying viability for P then lie in the basis.
     Next vary time and all row constants slightly. Every basis infeasible at the probe after the
first raises has a strict violation and stays infeasible in a neighborhood. Hence each feasible basis
in that neighborhood already carries its certificate, a list of red identities among the basis rows.
Make one further generic perturbation of all constants within these neighborhoods. For each
probe and restriction, equalities on a minimally dependent set of normals are now inconsistent.
Indeed, a nonzero dependence on those normals yields a proper affine hyperplane of exceptional
right-hand sides. Avoiding this finite list is an open condition. We can thus choose closed
bands with the probes in their interiors, pairwise disjoint inside the episodes, and a common
positive tolerance for all subsequent perturbations of the right-hand sides. Throughout the
bands every feasible point has independent tight normals, and every feasible basis of a viable
restriction keeps its certificate for P . Making the changes sufficiently small also preserves the
anchor bounds.

directly in one bank share all their scheduler ancestors. A local row involving no propagated
output has a bounded number of coordinates in one module. These lie in the common bank and
at most two terminal banks, so closing the support under ancestors adds at most 3H requests.

14     Complementary vertices, prismatoids, and packing polytopes
The distant vertices of Theorem 1.1 can be made complementary, meaning that each facet
contains exactly one of them. A simple d-polytope with such a pair has exactly 2d facets. The
wedge of Klee and Walkup [5] lets us assign an uncovered facet to the two vertices without
shortening the distance between them.

Corollary 14.1 (Complementary vertices). For every prescribed positive integer function s(d) =
do(1) and all sufficiently large d, a rational simple d-polytope with 2d facets has complementary
vertices at s(d)-move distance dω(1) .

Proof. Start with the polytope of Theorem 1.1 having d facets and move allowance s(d). Let
v, w be two distant vertices and let F be the smallest face containing both. Every face of F is
a face of the original polytope, so restricting to F preserves the distance lower bound. In its
affine hull F is rational and simple. Write p = dim F and let m ≤ d be its facet count. Each of
v, w lies on p facets of F , and no facet of F contains both. Thus m − 2p facets of F contain
neither vertex.
    Choose such a facet with rational affine slack a(x). The wedge is

                               W = {(x, t) : x ∈ F, 0 ≤ t ≤ a(x)}.

Its bottom and top sheets replace the chosen facet, and every other facet of F lifts to a facet of
W . A vertex above a vertex of F with a(x) > 0 belongs to one sheet and p lifted facets. When
a(x) = 0, the single vertex above it belongs to both sheets and p − 1 lifted facets. This list
exhausts the vertices, since each sheet is an affine copy of F , and an interior point of a fiber
can move along that fiber. Hence W is a rational simple (p + 1)-polytope.
     Consider any face of W maximizing b · x + ct. Maximizing first over the fiber gives
b · x + max(c, 0)a(x), an affine function of x. The projection of the face is therefore a face of F ,
of dimension at most that of the original face. Every allowed move of wedge vertices projects to
an allowed move or a stationary step.
     Lift v to the bottom sheet and w to the top. Both vertices had positive slack in the chosen
facet, so each new sheet contains just one of the two lifted tips. The incidence of the other
facets with v, w is preserved. Repeating the operation m − 2p times produces complementary

vertices in dimension m − p ≤ d. Take the product with a cube of dimension d − (m − p) and
choose opposite cube vertices over the tips. Every product face projects to a face of the first

14.1    Two simplex bases and their middle section
Translate the midpoint of the complementary vertices to the origin. It is interior, since a
supporting facet through it would contain both vertices. In the polar T , the two vertices

these facets. Thus T is a rational simplicial prismatoid, the convex hull of two simplex bases in
parallel hyperplanes. For 1 ≤ s < d, let an s-step between facets join two that share a nonempty
face of codimension at most s within each facet. A primal face of dimension j containing
two vertices corresponds to a face of codimension j within their dual facets. Hence the s-step
distance Ds between T0 , T1 equals the primal s-move distance between the tips. For s = 1, this
is the width of the prismatoid, measured by adjacency across ridges [12].
