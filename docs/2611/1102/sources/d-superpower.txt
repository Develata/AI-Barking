Goldman Sachs has taken AI coding tools away from bankers and traders, while engineers keep access. Bloomberg reported the restriction in November, based on guidance issued in October. It covers tools including Claude Code, from Anthropic, and GitHub. The change reverses a more open experiment. In July, Goldman’s chief information officer, Marco Argenti, praised “vibe coding”: using AI to build a working prototype without writing all the code yourself. A trader or asset-management employee could test an idea, then pass the prototype to one of the firm’s 12,000 engineers to prepare it for production. That gave business staff a way to shape software before the engineering handoff. Some traders are frustrated by losing access. Their reported uses went beyond prototypes: monitoring client trading flows and producing written and graphic materials. But those examples show what people did with the tools, not proven productivity gains. Goldman spokesperson Nick Carcaterra said the initial access period let the firm assess productivity, risk management, and other factors. The firm then put the tools in the hands of people it considered best suited to use them over the long term. The key gap is what that assessment found. Goldman has disclosed no comparative productivity figures and no specific failure or event that prompted the restriction. So the limit is clear; the evidence behind the choice of long-term users is not.

Goldman Sachs is narrowing access to AI coding tools after months of experimentation: October guidance, reported by Bloomberg on November 2, 2026, says bankers and traders can no longer use tools such as Claude Code and GitHub, while engineers retain access. The shift limits nontechnical staff’s ability to turn ideas into prototypes before engineering handoff, though employees also used the tools for other work. Goldman says it is selecting long-term users after weighing productivity and risk; it disclosed no specific failure or comparative results.

Goldman Sachs bankers and traders are losing access to AI coding tools they had used for months, while engineers can keep using them. Bloomberg reported the restriction on November 2, 2026, citing internal guidance issued in October. The change covers tools including Anthropic [/companies/anthropic]’s Claude Code and Microsoft [/companies/microsoft]’s GitHub, according to PYMNTS’ account [https://www.pymnts.com/news/artificial-intelligence/2026/goldman-sachs-bars-bankers-from-employing-some-ai-tools/?utm_source=superpowerdaily-com&utm_medium=referral&utm_campaign=goldman-sachs-restricts-ai-coding-tools-to-engineers-bloomberg-reports&utm_content=article-link-3] of the reporting.

A retreat from staff-built prototypes

As recently as July, Goldman Chief Information Officer Marco Argenti had praised “vibe coding” by nontechnical employees: using AI to build software without doing all the coding themselves. A trader or asset-management employee could turn an idea into a quick prototype, then hand it to an engineer to prepare for production, The Decoder’s account [https://the-decoder.com/goldman-sachs-pulls-ai-coding-tools-from-traders-and-bankers/?utm_source=superpowerdaily-com&utm_medium=referral&utm_campaign=goldman-sachs-restricts-ai-coding-tools-to-engineers-bloomberg-reports&utm_content=article-link-4] explains. Argenti described those creations going to one of the firm’s 12,000 engineers for enhancement, giving business staff a role in developing solutions before technical specialists took over.

Some traders are frustrated by the restriction, a person familiar with the matter told Bloomberg. Their uses extended beyond software experiments: they had employed AI to monitor client trading flows and create written and graphic materials. Those examples describe how employees used the tools, rather than measured gains in productivity.

Goldman spokesperson Nick Carcaterra described the initial access as a way to determine the best uses for certain AI tools across different parts of the firm. He framed the subsequent choice of users as part of that evaluation:

After a period of experimentation where we assess a variety of factors including productivity and risk management, we put them in the hands of those who are best suited to leverage them for the long term.
Nick Carcaterra, Goldman Sachs spokesperson, speaking to Bloomberg, as quoted by PYMNTS

Carcaterra’s explanation did not identify a specific failure that prompted the restriction or provide figures comparing productivity among engineers and other employees. The bank’s stated rationale leaves those results unspecified.
