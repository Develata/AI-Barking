                                                        Abstract


           Generative AI has demonstrated human-level performance on technical work tasks, yet evi-
       dence on how these task-level capabilities are changing labor demand remains scarce. We study
       the firm-level adoption of an AI coding assistant across 5,414 multinational firms accounting
       for 38% of the U.S. programming workforce. To identify causal effects, we exploit a natural
       experiment generated by firms’ predetermined software procurement cycles. Early AI-adopting
       firms hired more workers before the launch of Copilot and had larger, more technical, and
       older workforces. After one year, Copilot adoption reduces quarterly technical hiring by 29%
       among review-induced adopters, with no effect on separations, overall technical headcount, or
       vacancy duration. The hiring slowdown is concentrated in the subset of junior technical roles
       that involve the highest share of coding work. Overall, AI coding assistance causes firms to shift
       demand towards more experienced workers and roles that pair some coding with broader design
       responsibilities.

1 Introduction
LLMs now rival or exceed human performance on some tasks in mathematics, legal and financial
analysis, and software development (Rein et al., 2023; Luong et al., 2025; Guha et al., 2023).
Progress has been especially rapid in programming, where AI coding assistants can generate, explain,
test, and debug code. As organizations incorporate these task-level improvements and redesign
workflows, firms will reduce demand for human work that can now be done more effectively by
machines while expanding opportunities for human work that complements AI. Understanding where
these changes will be felt helps workers, firms, and policymakers adjust efficiently. However, the
mapping from task-level technical capabilities to role- and firm-level demand changes is difficult to
predict and difficult to measure. Field experiments can randomize access to AI across workers, but
equivalent experiments across firms are generally infeasible. Absent randomization, firms that adopt
AI early may already follow different growth trajectories or employ distinct management practices.
Observational studies cannot reliably separate this endogenous choice by firms to adopt AI, other
concurrent economic shocks, and the direct effects of AI on labor demand.
   This paper exploits a natural experiment in firm-level AI adoption to provide causal evidence
on how AI coding assistants affect hiring and employment for technical workers. We study the
rollout of GitHub Copilot (hereafter Copilot) across 5,414 multinational firms employing 45 million
workers worldwide, including 38% of the U.S. programming workforce. Our identification strategy
rests on the centralized and complex nature of enterprise software procurement. In large firms,
individual developers cannot independently deploy new software tools; instead, adoption must pro-
ceed through a formal procurement process involving dedicated legal, security, compliance, and
purchasing personnel. All firms in our sample were GitHub customers using GitHub’s enterprise
product for code management before Copilot’s January 2023 launch. These enterprise customers
follow a regular schedule for contract reviews with GitHub, during which the relevant teams for
coordinating contract evaluations and budgetary decisions are assembled. Firms faced a lower or-
ganizational cost of adopting Copilot during these existing GitHub reviews relative to initiating
a separate procurement process. Because these schedules were established before Copilot existed,
their timing provides plausibly exogenous variation in the timing of adoption, unrelated to firms’
underlying employment trajectories.
   One year post-review, treated firms are 20 percentage points more likely to have adopted Copilot
than not-yet-reviewed firms. Using a staggered difference-in-differences design that compares earlier-
and later-reviewed firms, we estimate both the first-stage effect of review timing on Copilot adoption
and its reduced-form effect on employment. Under additional assumptions, the Wald ratio of these
two estimates identifies the causal effect of Copilot among firms induced to adopt earlier by the
instrument of their pre-scheduled contract reviews.
   We link GitHub’s administrative records on Copilot adoption and contract reviews to employ-
ment data from Revelio Labs, which tracks firm-level headcount, hiring, and separations by role
and seniority. This matched dataset allows us to observe actual enterprise AI adoption rather than
relying on indirect proxies or occupational exposure scores. We focus on firms headquartered in


                                                  1
the Americas and Europe, where GitHub’s enterprise customer base is concentrated and Revelio
provides comprehensive coverage. The resulting monthly panel runs from January 2021 through
June 2025, which spans two years before and two and a half years after Copilot’s January 2023 en-
terprise launch. Over this window, Copilot was the dominant enterprise coding assistant, predating
the rollout of the enterprise versions of competing tools such as Cursor and Claude Code.
   Within a firm, AI coding assistance affects labor demand through three channels: changes
in the scale of software production, the allocation of tasks across roles and seniority levels, and
productivity gains on existing tasks. While expanding software production raises overall labor
demand, productivity gains on existing tasks reduce the labor required for a given level of output.
Changes in how tasks are bundled and allocated across jobs further shape which roles and seniority
levels firms require. Our empirical estimates capture the net effect of these potentially offsetting
forces on employment, hiring, and separations.
   Because we observe Copilot take up directly, we can cleanly identify the characteristics of early AI
adopters and non-adopters. Consistent with Acemoglu et al. (2023), early AI-adopting firms in our
sample hired more workers each month before the launch of Copilot. They were also larger overall
with a higher share of technical workers and a lower share of junior workers. These compositional
differences illustrate the econometric challenge of separating the drivers of AI adoption from the
effects of adoption. Our data also highlight that generative AI tools arrived during a tumultuous
period of adjustment for technical workers that affected both adopting and non-adopting firms.
From January, 2022 to December, 2024, monthly mean hiring rates among firms that never adopted
Copilot during our sample decreased from 2.7% to 1.8%.
   Using our procurement instrument, we find that Copilot adoption reduces technical hiring with-
out increasing separations. One year after scheduled contract review, monthly hiring for technical
workers, those who build, test, deploy, secure, or support software systems, is 7.6% lower than at
firms whose reviews have not yet occurred. The corresponding Wald estimates imply a 29% reduc-
tion in quarterly technical hiring among complying firms that were induced to adopt Copilot earlier
by their scheduled contract reviews. From a pre-period baseline of roughly 55 quarterly technical
hires per firm, the reduced-form and IV estimates imply hiring slows to about 51 and 39 workers
per quarter, respectively. Against an average firm workforce of 8,000 employees, these reductions
represent less than 0.2% of headcount per quarter. By contrast, we find no meaningful change in
separations, suggesting that layoffs have not been a meaningful tool of adjustment for these firms.
We also find no change in overall technical headcount over the first year, consistent with the small
scale of the hiring changes relative to the stock of workers at these established firms.
   Technical job postings also fall while posting durations remain unchanged, suggesting that the
drop in hiring reflects lower labor demand rather than reduced matching efficiency. Several placebo
and robustness checks weigh against the possibility that this decline is not driven by corporate bud-
geting cycles, broader hiring freezes or AI tools adoption. Scheduled contract reviews have no effect
on technical hiring prior to Copilot’s launch or post-launch hiring for non-technical professionals
such as accountants, and yield similar estimates when controlling for firm-size and industry trends.

   Decomposing this decline in overall technical hiring, we find Copilot reduces hiring primarily for
junior workers in code-intensive roles. We measure each role’s coding intensity using pre-Copilot
job descriptions and distinguish between junior and experienced workers. Neither coding intensity
nor seniority alone predicts the hiring response: more code-intensive roles do not experience larger
overall hiring declines, and junior hiring does not fall uniformly across all technical roles. Rather,
the hiring decline is concentrated at the intersection. Four quarters after contract review, a one-
standard-deviation increase in coding intensity is associated with 6.8% lower quarterly hiring among
junior workers, with no comparable gradient among experienced workers. Comparing the two ends
of the coding-intensity distribution illustrates this pattern: among compliers, quarterly hiring of
junior software engineers falls by roughly 40%, whereas hiring of experienced software engineers
and of systems and infrastructure engineers is unaffected.
   This contraction in entry-level coding positions reshapes both where junior workers enter firms
and the seniority mix of technical teams. Before Copilot’s launch, 72% of junior technical hires
entered code-intensive roles; our estimates imply that adoption reduces this share to 52%, leaving
entry-level hiring nearly evenly split between more and less code-intensive roles. This shift away
from entry-level workers is striking given experimental evidence that novices realize the largest
task-level productivity gains from generative AI, demonstrating that larger individual productivity
effects need not translate into greater labor demand.
   Although the volume of experienced software engineering hires remains stable, firms raise the
bar on their qualifications and firm-specific tenure. Three to four quarters after contract review,
new hires into experienced software engineering positions have roughly 1.3 additional years of po-
tential experience and are 4.7 percentage points more likely to hold a master’s or doctoral degree.
Experienced hiring also shifts toward workers with firm-specific human capital: external hires into
experienced software engineering roles fall by approximately 13%, whereas internal fills remain un-
changed. Among junior software engineering hires, by contrast, observable education, experience,
and internal sourcing do not detectably change.
   Finally, we examine whether Copilot alters the geographic distribution of hiring between firms’
home countries and India, their primary offshore engineering hub. In principle, AI coding tools
could make lower-cost offshore talent more attractive by bridging time-zone and communication
gaps and helping remote developers get up to speed on proprietary systems. Conversely, if offshore
teams are disproportionately assigned well-defined implementation work, their roles may be more
directly exposed to AI substitution. Empirically, we find no evidence that adoption shifts hiring
toward India; rather, the point estimates point to a somewhat steeper slowdown in offshore hiring
than at headquarters.
   Together, our results show that AI coding assistance is already reshaping technical labor de-
mand, with adjustments emerging within a year. Even within technical roles, the early impacts
of AI fall unevenly, with marked decreases in hiring in the most code-intensive junior roles but
no clear changes in hiring for other technical junior roles. For experienced workers, the impacts
show up in the demand for specific skills rather than a change in heads hired. Two considerations
are important when interpreting these findings. First, our estimates capture firm-level employment


                                                  3
responses rather than economy-wide equilibrium effects. Reduced entry-level software engineering
hiring at large adopting firms need not translate into lower aggregate technical employment, as
displaced candidates may find work in other technical occupations or at smaller employers (Ray-
mond, 2023; Acemoglu and Restrepo, 2020). Conversely, if adopting firms expand output at the
expense of competitors, our estimates may capture business-stealing effects that do not translate
into aggregate employment gains, potentially making market-wide effects more negative than the
firm-level estimates (Acemoglu, Lelarge and Restrepo, 2020). Second, our estimates capture an
initial adjustment period rather than a long-run steady state. As firms continue to understand the
potential of AI tools, both to automate tasks and to support new work, hiring in different areas of
technical work could continue to adjust in either direction.
    This paper makes three contributions. First, we introduce a portable empirical strategy for
identifying the firm-level causal effects of rapidly evolving technologies like enterprise AI. Because
AI capabilities advance continuously across new tools and model upgrades, any single empirical
study captures a specific product generation, such as our analysis of GitHub Copilot between 2023
and mid-2025. However, our identification strategy is not specific to a single tool or release. It
exploits a pervasive feature of corporate software purchasing: enterprise vendors and AI providers
routinely sell new products, capability upgrades, and expanded token budgets through existing
customer contracts that follow predetermined review schedules. These recurring procurement cycles
therefore provide a generalizable research design that researchers can use to provide causal estimates
in successive waves of AI across firms, occupations, and industries.
    Second, we provide the first credible causal evidence on how firm-level adoption of generative AI
affects hiring, separations, and workforce composition. Existing studies report conflicting findings,
documenting reduced demand for AI-exposed workers, null employment effects, or faster growth.1
Our analysis shows how two distinct identification challenges may explain this divergence. First,
studies comparing occupations by AI exposure may risk picking up occupation-specific macroeco-
nomic contractions, which our estimates show account for at least half of the post-2022 drop in
technical hiring. Second, studies comparing adopting and non-adopting firms may face positive se-
lection into treatment: in our sample, eventual adopters were already growing faster before Copilot
launched. Our research design overcomes both challenges, separating the causal effect of adop-
tion from firm-level selection and aggregate shocks. Combining administrative adoption records
with predetermined procurement cycles overcomes both challenges to isolate the causal effect of
adoption.
    Third, we contribute to the literature on how technology shapes skill demand and workforce
adjustment by showing how adjustment to generative AI coding assistants unfolds across hiring,
separations, and skill requirements.2 Firms can reduce labor input by slowing hiring or increasing

2 Setting: GitHub Copilot for Enterprise
2.1     GitHub Enterprise
Commercial software development requires large teams of engineers to collaborate on shared, in-
terdependent codebases. When an engineer writes code to update a product feature or internal
system, that change cannot simply be released to users immediately. To prevent outages and secu-
rity breaches, proposed code must first be reviewed by peers, run through automated tests to ensure
it works with existing systems, scanned for security vulnerabilities, and approved for release. Large
organizations must also satisfy strict security and regulatory standards by controlling which employ-
ees can modify live systems, maintaining audit logs of every change, and governing the geographic
region where software code is stored and accessed.
    GitHub Enterprise, owned by Microsoft and used by 90% of Fortune 100 companies, is the
leading platform for managing this workflow at scale (GitHub, 2026b; Wessel et al., 2023; GitHub,
2026a). Importantly, GitHub Enterprise, which we refer to as GitHub, is not an AI tool. Rather, it
is the platform firms use to store their code, collaborate on software development, review changes,
and manage who has access to it. Focusing on pre-existing GitHub customers therefore isolates
established employers with substantial software engineering operations.

2.3       Enterprise Procurement Cycles and Contract Review
In large firms, individual developers and managers cannot simply download and use new software
tools on their own. Because enterprise software often interacts with proprietary systems and sensitive
corporate data, companies block employees from installing unapproved software applications on
work computers (National Institute of Standards and Technology, 2018; Weill and Ross, 2004).
Instead, adopting a new tool must go through a centralized procurement process: legal, security,
and procurement teams must vet the vendor, verify that proprietary code and customer data are
protected, and negotiate contract terms and pricing. Initiating this process outside of regular
purchasing cycles requires incurring organizational frictions and coordination costs. A scheduled
contract review already coordinates the legal, security, and finance teams for a planned evaluation.
By contrast, an off-cycle purchase requires managers to assemble a separate business justification,
wait in legal and security review queues, secure budgetary approval, and schedule meetings with all
the relevant decision-makers.
      Our identification strategy exploits recurring, pre-scheduled contract reviews built into firms’
existing contracts for GitHub’s source code software product. During these check-ins, a firm’s
procurement team and GitHub’s sales representatives meet to adjust license counts, evaluate new
features, and update contract terms. Even before Copilot’s release, account adjustments were
heavily concentrated around these dates. In the pre-period, the share of firms increasing their core
GitHub licenses quadrupled from roughly 10% to 40% in the two months surrounding a review,
while the share reducing licenses rose from 4% to 14% (Appendix Figure A.5).4 This concentration

instrument for Copilot adoption.5 Because these scheduled reviews are staggered across the calendar
year rather than bunched in a single quarter, firms reach their first post-launch evaluation in different
months, allowing us to compare firms arriving at a review to not-yet-reviewed firms at the same
point in calendar time (Panel B of Figure 1).
    While procurement stickiness clearly concentrates adjustments to core GitHub licenses around
review dates, how contract reviews shape the timing and speed of Copilot adoption is an empirical
question. On one hand, reaching a pre-scheduled review substantially lowered the administrative
friction of adopting the tool compared to initiating an off-cycle procurement process. Because
introducing a new product like Copilot is more complex than adjusting seat counts for an already
integrated product, these administrative frictions could be even more binding. On the other hand,
strong demand for generative AI might lead firms to adopt Copilot immediately, although the slow
path of adoption in Panel A of Figure 1 weighs against this. We examine this in our first-stage
analysis in Section 4.3.

3 Data
Our analysis combines three data sources: GitHub licensing data, GitHub customer relationship
management (CRM) data, and Revelio Labs employment data.

3.1    GitHub Data
The GitHub data consist of monthly firm-level snapshots from January 2021 through November
2025 linked via internal identifiers. The licensing records report the number of GitHub and Copilot
licenses held by each firm. We define Copilot adoption as the first month a firm holds a positive
number of Copilot licenses and treat adoption as an absorbing state. To avoid endogenous sample
entry, we restrict the sample to active GitHub customers as of September 2022. We obtain each
firm’s first post-launch contract review date from the CRM records as of December 2022, one month
prior to Copilot’s January 2023 launch.

3.2    Revelio Labs Employment Data
We measure employment stocks and flows using Revelio Labs, which aggregates public professional
profiles and online job postings into standardized worker-employer histories. From individual em-
ployment spells and educational records, we construct monthly firm-level counts of headcount, new
hires, and separations, both overall and by role-by-seniority cell, alongside monthly counts and
average durations of job postings. Because Revelio draws primarily on LinkedIn profiles, coverage
is strongest among large firms and technical workers in North America and Europe (Baird et al.,
2026). We therefore restrict our sample to private-sector firms headquartered in the Americas and
Europe (see Appendix Section B for details on variable construction and coverage).

3.3    Sample Characteristics
We link GitHub customers to Revelio using automated name matching supplemented by AI-assisted
LinkedIn searches and human review, applying pre-2023 quality filters to exclude false matches and
firms with thin profile coverage (Appendix Sections B.3–B.7 and Appendix Table A.3). The resulting
balanced panel tracks 5,414 firms monthly from January 2021 through June 2025. Benchmarking
Revelio against external records confirms strong alignment: total headcount correlates closely with
Compustat 10-K employment in log levels (r “ 0.756) and annual log changes (r “ 0.576), while
pre-launch software engineering headcount correlates strongly with purchased GitHub licenses in
log levels (r “ 0.684; Appendix Section B).
   Sample firms are large, established employers: at the September 2022 baseline (Appendix Ta-
ble A.4), the average firm is 47 years old and employs 8,284 workers globally, with 61.6% headquar-
tered in the United States and the largest concentrations in information technology (24.8%) and
financial services (12.9%). We classify eight roles as technical: software, data, quality-assurance,
infrastructure, systems, cybersecurity, and design engineers, alongside application developers. Tech-
nical roles represent 12.6% of sample employment (5.7% in software engineering alone) and 1.6

4 Empirical Strategy
4.1        Technical Labor-Market Trends and Pre-Adoption Differences
Simple comparisons between Copilot adopters and non-adopters face two empirical challenges. Copi-
lot launched during a sharp macroeconomic contraction in technical labor demand. Panel A of Fig-
ure 2 plots the mean monthly technical hiring rate (new technical hires divided by lagged technical
headcount). After rising from 2.5% to 3.0% in 2021, technical hiring began falling in March 2022,
ten months before Copilot’s enterprise release, coinciding with the Federal Reserve’s rapid monetary
tightening cycle (Iscenko and Millet, 2026). Eventual adopters and non-adopters followed different
trajectories in the pre-period. First, eventual adopters experienced a much steeper hiring surge
in 2021 and contraction throughout 2022 (Panel B). Second, eventual adopters employed a higher
share of technical workers, may have been differentially exposed to the broader tech slowdown,
and may not have followed parallel trends with non-adopters. As a result, standard observational
benchmarks could fail in two distinct ways: centering on firm adoption dates may confound treat-
ment with pre-existing firm growth trends (Panels C and D), whereas comparing adopters with
non-adopters around late 2022 may reflect differential exposure to the broader tech cycle (Panels E
and F).

4.2        Instrumented Differences-in-Differences with Staggered Timing
To isolate exogenous variation in adoption timing, we instrument for Copilot adoption using the
timing of each firm’s first scheduled GitHub contract review after Copilot’s launch (Ri ). Let ℓ “

    Equation (7) highlights three potentially offsetting forces. First, by lowering software develop-
ment costs, Copilot may induce the firm to undertake more projects, expanding total task volume
(∆ ln Ti ą 0) and raising labor demand proportionally across all cells. Second, Copilot may alter task
allocation (∆ ln mi,rs ). For example, senior engineers might use Copilot to handle implementation
work previously delegated to junior engineers, or developers might absorb testing tasks previously
assigned to quality assurance teams. Third, Copilot changes task productivity (∆ ln ai,rs ).12 Com-

time reviewing and debugging AI-generated code lowers effective productivity and increases labor
requirements.
    This framework clarifies what our empirical estimates can and cannot identify. In the data,
we observe employment and hiring in each cell (Li,rs ), but we do not observe the firm’s total
volume of software work (Ti ), how tasks are divided across cells (mi,rs ), or task-level productivity
(ai,rs ). Consequently, our estimates for any single group capture the combined net effect of all
three channels. However, comparing employment responses across cells within the same firm, such
as junior versus experienced engineers in the same role, differences out the firm-wide scale effect
(∆ ln Ti ), which is common to all cells in firm i. Any divergence across groups within a firm
must therefore reflect differential changes in task allocation (∆ ln mi,rs ) or task-level productivity
(∆ ln ai,rs ). Finally, because hiring decisions are forward-looking, our short-run estimates cannot
separate responses to realized productivity gains from adjustments driven by managers’ beliefs and
expectations about AI capabilities. As firms learn about the tool’s actual performance and as
project scale and task allocation adjust at different speeds, these net employment effects may evolve
over longer horizons.

6.1.2     Recruiting Activity, Vacancy Duration, and Incidence

Realized hiring reflects both how many positions firms seek to fill and how quickly they fill open
vacancies. A decline in hiring could therefore reflect either lower labor demand or a slower matching
process, for instance if AI adoption induced firms to search for scarcer candidates with AI-specific
skills. To distinguish between these explanations, Appendix Table A.11 reports the reduced form
effects of a scheduled contract review on three quarterly measures of technical recruiting activity: job
postings, duration postings remain open, and any posting activity. Four quarters after a scheduled
contract review, the intensive margin of open technical postings falls by 14% (´0.148 log points).
In contrast, contract review has no statistically detectable effect on the probability of having any


                                                         18
open technical posting or the time a posting is open, although there are some pre-trends in posting
duration. The slowdown in technical hiring seems to be driven by firms opening fewer positions
rather than taking longer to fill existing vacancies.
   Taken together, the employment flow and job posting results show that Copilot adoption initially
slows technical workforce growth by reducing new hiring rather than displacing incumbent workers.
Although the direct incidence of this adjustment falls on prospective hires, incumbent workers may
still be affected indirectly. As hiring slows across adopting firms, fewer outside job openings can
narrow incumbents’ outside options, reducing job-to-job mobility and weakening wage bargaining
within the firm (Caldwell and Danieli, 2024; Jäger et al., 2024). Concentrating adjustment on the
hiring margin could be consistent with models of labor demand with quasi-fixed labor costs or AI
coding assistance devaluing the skills of novices (Oi, 1962; Abowd, Corbel and Kramarz, 1999).

6.1.3    Placebo and Robustness Checks

We evaluate three alternative explanations to verify that the decline in technical hiring reflects Copi-
lot adoption rather than recurring corporate budgeting cycles, contemporaneous firm-wide hiring
freezes or general AI procurement, or differential trends by firm size and sector. First, scheduled
contract reviews could in principle coincide with annual corporate budgeting or fiscal planning dates
when firms routinely reset hiring targets. To test this directly, we conduct a pre-launch placebo
check by assigning each firm its scheduled contract review date prior to Copilot’s 2023 launch. Al-
though this restricts the post-review window to two quarters before Copilot’s launch, a recurring
budget-driven contraction decided at the contract review should begin to register in realized hiring
within that timeframe. Instead, we find no effects on overall technical hiring, separations, or junior
and experienced software engineering hiring (Appendix Table A.17). Moreover, even after Copilot’s
release, the technical hiring decline does not appear as an immediate adjustment at the review
date, as an annual budget reset would imply, but instead emerges gradually in the third and fourth
quarters after review, tracking the rollout of Copilot licenses.
   Second, even if pre-launch reviews did not affect employment, a firm’s 2023 review might coincide
with a broader post-2022 white-collar hiring freeze, an IT-wide budget cut, or the simultaneous
procurement of general office AI tools (although Copilot was the only AI product sold by GitHub’s
dedicated sales force). To test for firm-wide shocks, we examine hiring for accountants, the most
common non-technical professional occupation in our data (employed by 98% of sample firms,
with a pre-launch median of 34 per firm) and one exposed to both general AI tools and broader
white-collar hiring slowdowns. Appendix Figure A.12 shows no decline in either log accountant
hires or the probability of hiring an accountant following contract review. Four quarters after
review, the reduced-form estimate for log accountant hiring is a statistically insignificant 0.035
log points. Furthermore, as we show in Section 6.2, the slowdown is not uniform even within the
technical workforce: hiring is unchanged in less code-intensive technical roles and among experienced
engineers, weighing against a broad technical or IT-department hiring freeze.
   Third, because firms with earlier contract-review dates have larger pre-launch workforces, our
estimates could be confounded if larger and smaller firms, or different sectors, followed distinct


                                                  19
post-launch hiring trajectories. In Appendix Table A.18, we re-estimate our specifications adding
interactions between calendar-quarter fixed effects and pre-launch firm-size terciles, indicators for
the information and professional-services sectors, or both. Across all specifications, the four-quarter
reduced-form estimates for total technical hiring and separations change by at most 0.008 log points
(Columns 1 and 2), confirming that differential trends by firm size or sector do not drive our findings.

6.2      Hiring Composition by Code Intensity and Seniority
The preceding results show that Copilot adoption reduces overall technical hiring. We next examine
whether this decline is spread evenly across the technical workforce or concentrated among particular
roles and seniority levels, changing the composition of new hires.

6.2.1     Measuring the Coding Content of Technical Work

We define a position’s code intensity as the share of its described responsibilities devoted to activities
within Copilot’s core domain during our study period: writing, completing, testing, documenting,
explaining, and debugging code. To measure code intensity across roles and seniority levels, we use
the text of LinkedIn position descriptions for technical jobs at sample firms with start dates between
2018 and 2022, prior to Copilot’s launch.13 Unlike occupation-level task measures that assign a
single average to a broad occupational code, position descriptions capture how responsibilities vary
both across granular technical roles and across seniority levels within a role. Appendix Figure A.8
illustrates this variation using examples from outside our sample: a software engineer describes
building a job-scheduling platform and implementing APIs (Panel A), whereas a test automation
engineer combines programming in Python and LabVIEW with physical design and testing of liquid-
nitrogen heat exchangers (Panel B). Similarly, within infrastructure engineering, a technical lead
describes setting team strategy and recruiting, while a technical lead manager combines engineering
management with building machine learning infrastructure (Panels C and D).
    We score each description on this dimension using Claude Sonnet 4.6 (see Appendix Section B.10
for the prompt and implementation details). The model observes only the free-text description,
without the job title, occupation, seniority, location, firm, or start date. For our baseline cross-
role analysis, we average the description-level scores within each of the eight technical roles. Code
intensity is thus an ex ante measure of task overlap with Copilot’s capabilities rather than a measure
of actual time spent coding or realized Copilot usage.

6.2.3    Implied Magnitudes and Compositional Shifts

Because the hiring contraction is concentrated among junior positions in code-intensive roles, Copilot
adoption reshapes both the entry paths of junior workers and the seniority structure of technical
teams (Figure 9). At baseline, 72% of all junior technical hires enter through code-intensive roles;
with Copilot adoption, that share falls to 52%, leaving entry-level hiring nearly evenly split between
more and less code-intensive roles while the role mix of experienced hiring is largely unchanged (69%
to 74% code-intensive; Panel A). Viewed within role groups, the junior share of code-intensive hiring
drops from 57% to 45%, whereas the junior share in less code-intensive roles rises from 54% to 68%
(Panel B). In level terms (Panel C), junior hiring in code-intensive roles drops by about 7 spells per
firm-quarter, while experienced hiring in these roles is fairly flat. Comparing the two ends of the
coding-intensity distribution directly illustrates this divergence (Appendix Table A.14; see Appendix
Figure A.11 for the monthly reduced form event studies). Copilot adoption reduces quarterly junior
software engineering hiring by approximately 40% (´0.513 log points), with no detectable decline
among experienced software engineers (´0.160 log points) or systems and infrastructure engineers
(0.212 log points).

    Through the lens of our conceptual framework, a common change in the scale of software pro-
duction cannot explain why junior and experienced hiring diverge within the same role. Instead, this
divergence reflects differences across seniority levels in task-level productivity gains, job bundling,
or task allocation. First, Copilot may generate larger direct labor-saving productivity gains on the
coding tasks typically assigned to junior workers. Second, junior and experienced positions differ in


                                                          26
how coding is bundled with other responsibilities, which explains why the seniority gap persists even
when coding intensity is measured at the role-by-seniority cell level (Garicano, Li and Wu, 2026).
Whereas junior positions in code-intensive roles center on discrete implementation tasks that can
be specified, delegated, and evaluated independently at relatively low cost, experienced positions
bundle code production with system architecture, domain judgment, and cross-team accountability.
Because coding is more separable from the rest of the job in junior positions, Copilot may substitute
more readily for junior hiring even if it improves productivity in both groups.
   At the same time, the flat code-intensity gradient for experienced hiring does not imply that
Copilot leaves experienced work unchanged or simply complements experienced labor. Even as
Copilot saves experienced engineers time on their existing coding tasks, firms may expand the
scope of work assigned to them, or change their time allocation. Experienced engineers may use
Copilot to complete implementation work previously delegated to junior teammates, or spend more
time reviewing, debugging, and integrating AI-generated code alongside their core architectural and
coordination duties. These offsetting forces, labor savings on existing coding tasks alongside task
reallocation from junior positions and new verification demands, could leave net experienced hiring
approximately flat across the code-intensity distribution even as Copilot reshapes their day-to-day
work.

6.3     Experience, Credentials, and Internal Hiring among Software
        Engineers
Although firms maintain their volume of experienced software engineering hires after adopting
Copilot, stable hiring volume could mask changes in the types of engineers firms recruit. Within
software engineering, the largest and most code-intensive occupation in our sample, we next examine
whether firms adjust the educational credentials, prior experience, and internal sourcing of the
workers they hire. At baseline, experienced software engineers average 8.7 years since earning their
bachelor’s degree, compared with 4.8 years among junior engineers, and are significantly more likely
to hold an academic master’s or doctoral degree (Appendix Table A.15).
   In principle, AI coding tools could either substitute for or complement accumulated general and
firm-specific human capital. If Copilot makes programming knowledge and codebase conventions
readily accessible on the job, firms may relax degree, experience, or internal-tenure requirements
when filling positions. Conversely, if deeper technical training, industry experience, or familiarity
with the firm’s architecture makes engineers more effective at directing AI tools, evaluating gener-
ated code, and coordinating system design, firms could raise the bar on these qualifications among
new hires.

6.4    Geographic Composition of Technical Hiring
Beyond shifting the roles, seniority, and credentials of new hires, AI coding assistants could also
reshape where firms locate technical work. Firms in our sample employ technical workers both
domestically and offshore, with India the most common offshore location. During 2021–2022, ap-
proximately 58% of firms employed at least one technical worker in India, while 83% employed at
least one worker there in any occupation. This concentration reflects India’s emergence as a leading
global hub for offshore software development. Beginning in the early 2000s, India developed a large
export-oriented software services industry, supported by a substantial pool of English-speaking en-
gineers, strong technical universities, and labor costs well below those in the United States and
Europe (Arora and Gambardella, 2005; Parthasarathy, 2013).
   In principle, AI coding assistants could disproportionately benefit offshore workers through sev-
eral channels. First, LLMs can facilitate communication across language barriers, reducing coor-
dination costs associated with geographically distributed engineering teams (Brynjolfsson, Li and
Raymond, 2025). Second, coding assistants can help workers interpret technical specifications, navi-
gate unfamiliar codebases, and understand internal systems. These capabilities may be particularly
valuable for offshore engineers, who often have less access to the informal knowledge and institutional
context available to colleagues at a firm’s headquarters. Third, given substantial wage differentials
between domestic and offshore engineers, AI tools that reduce these productivity and coordination
disadvantages could make offshore labor an even more attractive substitute for domestic labor.
   On the other hand, tasks that can be performed offshore may be more easily codified and
therefore more susceptible to automation. Moreover, firms may be more willing to reduce hiring
outside their headquarters country, where reputational concerns, social pressures, and information
frictions may impose fewer constraints on employment adjustments (Landier, Nair and Wulf, 2009;
Bassanini, Brunello and Caroli, 2017).
   To examine this margin, Appendix Table A.16 estimates the effects of contract review on tech-
nical hiring in firms’ headquarters countries (“domestic”) and India, restricting the sample to the
4,509 firms with a presence in India during the pre-period. The reduced-form estimates are impre-
cise, partly reflecting the smaller sample and sparser hiring abroad, and do not reveal statistically
significant differences in hiring responses across locations. Four quarters after contract review, the
point estimate for India-based technical hiring is ´0.060 log points (SE “ 0.189), compared with
´0.029 log points for domestic technical hiring. Although the estimates are too imprecise to detect
differences across locations reliably, they provide no evidence of a shift toward offshore technical

hiring following Copilot adoption. If anything, the point estimates suggest a larger slowdown in
India.

tuals for four reasons. First, treatment effects are estimated in event time over the year following
each firm’s 2023 contract review (spanning 2023Q1 through 2024Q4 across cohorts), whereas the ag-
gregate decline ∆ is measured between calendar years 2022 and 2024. The decomposition therefore
treats the average effect over the first year post-review as the average effect in the 2024 post-period.

ing them to firm hiring levels assumes treatment effects are homogeneous across firms. Third, the
all-adopters benchmark (Appendix Equation (12)) does not have a clean upper bound. Because
always-takers adopt earlier on average than compliers, they may have initially deployed earlier,
less capable versions of the tool, or benefit more from the scale effects of AI adoption, leading to
smaller hiring reductions. On the other hand, they could have larger hiring slowdowns. Fourth, the
calculation is partial equilibrium: it treats the hiring of non-adopting firms as invariant to adoption
elsewhere, whereas in equilibrium some candidates not hired by adopting firms may be absorbed by
other employers in the sample. Finally, our design cannot separate which specific factors drove the
remaining half to four-fifths of the hiring slowdown, such as monetary tightening, the correction of
pandemic-era overhiring, changes in the tax treatment of software research and development, or AI
adoption operating through other channels.

7 Conclusion
As AI systems become more capable, a central question is how firms adjust their workforces. Which
roles will experience increases, decreases, or no change in demand? Will firms adjust on the hiring
margin, by reallocating or separating from incumbent workers, or all three? We study these margins
among large, technology-intensive multinational firms adopting GitHub Copilot, an early enterprise
AI coding assistant, exploiting variation in the timing of pre-existing enterprise software contract
reviews to identify causal effects. We find that the initial labor market adjustment occurs on the
hiring margin rather than through separations. Rather than reducing recruitment uniformly, adopt-
ing firms cut entry-level hiring in code-intensive roles while maintaining the volume of experienced
hires and raising the bar on their education, prior experience, and firm-specific tenure. The early
impact of AI coding assistance thus appears less as broad job displacement and more as a narrowing
of entry-level pipelines alongside greater demand for accumulated human capital. Thus, even when
generative AI provides the largest task-level productivity boost to novices, those individual gains
need not translate into greater labor demand.
   Our findings also point to several open questions about how the labor market effects of AI will
evolve across firms and over time. First, our estimates characterize large, established multinationals
operating complex software systems. Smaller or younger firms facing fewer coordination constraints
may reorganize production more rapidly, or respond to lower software development costs by ex-
panding output and technical hiring more elastically. Second, our sample period captures the first
wave of AI coding assistants, which operated primarily by autocompleting code within a developer’s
workflow. Newer agentic systems capable of executing multi-step engineering tasks autonomously
could deepen substitution or extend it further up the seniority distribution. Conversely, if early
corporate adjustments partly reflected expectations that outpaced realized productivity gains, or
if shrinking the junior cohort eventually creates bottlenecks in the supply of senior talent, firms’
hiring strategies may rebalance over a longer horizon.
   Finally, our firm-level estimates do not capture wage adjustments or aggregate employment.
Slower hiring and shifts in skill demand can alter compensation and the division of surplus between
firms and workers even when total headcount is stable. Moreover, a contraction in a specific entry
channel at large adopting firms need not translate one-for-one into lower aggregate technical employ-
ment (Raymond, 2023; Acemoglu, Lelarge and Restrepo, 2020). Workers who would otherwise have
joined these firms as junior software engineers may instead enter adjacent roles such as systems or
infrastructure engineering, or take software engineering positions at smaller or later-adopting firms.
On the other hand, firms who adopt AI may steal market share from other firms. Determining how
these firm-level shifts in hiring and career ladders reshape market-wide employment, wages, and
long-run labor demand remains a central question for future research.

A Background on GitHub and AI Coding Tools
A.1     GitHub
Pull-based software development originated in the open-source community. In 2005, Linus Torvalds
released Git, a distributed version control system that allowed developers to maintain independent
copies of a codebase and propose changes for integration. Three years later, Tom Preston-Werner,
Chris Wanstrath, and P.J. Hyett launched GitHub as a web-based collaboration layer built on top
of Git, introducing a structured pull-request workflow through which developers submit, review,
and merge proposed code changes (Appendix Figure A.1). The platform grew from 100,000 users
in 2009 to more than 31 million by 2018.
   In its early years, GitHub served primarily as an open-source collaboration platform offering
free hosting for public repositories. Commercial adoption grew out of bottom-up developer de-
mand: as software engineers increasingly used personal GitHub accounts for workplace projects in
place of legacy internal tools, corporate customers requested an enterprise version with centralized
administration and security controls, prompting the launch of GitHub Enterprise in 2011. Over
the following decade, the pull-request workflow became the standard model for commercial software
development at large organizations. Microsoft acquired GitHub in 2018, but GitHub continued to
operate as an independent business unit with its own enterprise sales force and contracting systems
(Nagle et al., 2023).

A.2      AI Coding Tools
Automated code assistance predates large language models. Rule-based tools such as Microsoft’s
IntelliSense, introduced in Visual Studio in 1997, provided syntax-aware identifier completion and
error flagging. By 2006, code-completion tools were widely used by Java developers, although early
empirical studies found that they primarily reduced documentation lookup rather than substantially
accelerating code production (Murphy, Kersten and Findlater, 2006; Jiang and Coblenz, 2024).
Later tools such as Tabnine introduced statistical and early deep-learning models to offer short
line-by-line completions.
    Large language models marked a step change in code generation capabilities. Because LLMs
predict subsequent tokens conditional on surrounding context, programming languages are particu-
larly well suited to their architecture: code follows strict syntactic and semantic rules, vast corpora
of paired source code and natural-language documentation are publicly available for training, and
compilers and test suites provide objective verification signals. These features supported rapid ca-
pability gains. On SWE-bench, an evaluation benchmark introduced in 2023 that tasks models with


                                                        40
resolving real GitHub issues across 12 Python repositories, frontier model resolution rates rose from
under 5% at launch to nearly 80% by early 2026 (Jimenez et al., 2024; Maslej et al., 2025).
    GitHub Copilot first launched as a technical preview in June 2021 (Friedman, 2021). Copilot for
Individuals became generally available in June 2022, followed by Copilot for Business for enterprise
customers in early 2023. Developer uptake was rapid: in a 2023 industry survey, 56% of professional
developers reported using GitHub Copilot, more than four times the share of the next most common
AI coding tool (Stack Overflow, 2023). The initial version of Copilot was powered by OpenAI Codex,
a model trained on billions of lines of public source code. Subsequent releases expanded the product
beyond in-line autocomplete to include an interactive chat interface (Appendix Figure A.2), allowed
users to select among multiple frontier models (McKenna and Perz, 2026), and enabled enterprise
customers to index private repositories to ground responses in their internal codebase.
    A growing body of research examines the effects of GitHub Copilot at the individual developer
level; Ani, Hamid and Zhamri (2024) provide an early review. Controlled experiments and field
studies evaluate its effects on task-level developer speed and output (Cui et al., 2026), the security
and quality of generated code (Fu et al., 2025), and computer science education (Shah et al., 2025).

B Data Construction, Measurement, and Validation
B.1        Revelio Occupational and Seniority Measures
Workers on LinkedIn often describe similar jobs using different titles, while identical titles can
correspond to different occupations across firms and industries. Revelio standardizes free-text job
titles using a hierarchical clustering algorithm that incorporates job titles, text from workers’ and
employers’ job descriptions, workers’ skills, coworkers, and prior experience (Revelio Labs, 2026a).
For example, titles such as “Software Developer,” “Backend Engineer,” and “Full Stack Developer”
are grouped into a common “Software Engineering” category. We use Revelio’s 150-cluster job
taxonomy, the most granular classification recommended for firm-by-year analysis (Revelio Labs,
2026b).
       We also use Revelio’s measure of worker seniority. Because titles convey different levels of senior-
ity across firms and occupations, Revelio constructs a seniority score using an ensemble model that
combines an individual’s title, company, and industry with the duration and seniority of previous
employment and age (Revelio Labs, 2026b). The resulting measure ranges from entry-level to senior
executive positions.14
       From the underlying individual position spells, we construct a monthly panel of employment
stocks, new hires, and separations at the firm level, as well as disaggregated panels at the firm-by-role
and firm-by-role-by-seniority levels.

B.2        Revelio Coverage and Measurement
Online professional profiles over-represent white-collar and technical occupations, younger cohorts,
and larger, more established firms. Coverage is particularly comprehensive in information technology
and among professional, scientific, and technical workers in North America and Europe (Baird et al.,
2026). These features make Revelio well suited to tracking the technical workforces of large GitHub
customers. Because coverage is thinner in other regions and has grown over time with platform
adoption, we restrict our sample to firms headquartered in the Americas and Europe.
       In addition to raw spell counts, Revelio adjusts their data to correct for two sources of measure-
ment error (Revelio Labs, 2026b). First, a cross-sectional sampling weight adjusts for differences
in profile representation across occupations, industries, and countries. Second, a time-series ad-
justment accounts for reporting lags between when an employment transition occurs and when a
worker updates their online profile. Both adjustments are calibrated against firm-level headcount
benchmarks from regulatory filings. Throughout our analysis, we use these adjusted employment
and flow series.

B.3     GitHub–Revelio Firm Matching Procedure
We link GitHub administrative records to Revelio Labs using firm name, country, industry, and
size indicators. As a first step, we apply Revelio’s internal name-matching algorithm, which strips
common legal suffixes (such as “LLC,” “Inc.,” and “GmbH”) and administrative tags (such as “Par-
ent”) before performing an exact string match against standardized company names in Revelio’s
database. The algorithm excludes company profile pages with zero recorded employees and, when
a match corresponds to a corporate subsidiary, aggregates employment to the ultimate parent firm.
   This initial string-matching step yields a high-confidence match for approximately 64% of
GitHub accounts. The remaining accounts are not matched automatically because their recorded
names are generic, abbreviated, or formatted differently across the two databases. Discrepancies typ-
ically arise when a firm operates under a distinct brand or division name on professional networking
platforms (e.g., “ABC Inc.” versus “ABC Science”), when non-English characters or transliterations
differ across systems, or when the GitHub license is held by a subsidiary whose legal name does not
appear as a standalone entity in Revelio. To resolve these cases, we combine an AI-assisted match-
ing pipeline with human verification, using firm country, industry, and employee size to identify and
validate the corresponding company profile page, as detailed in the next subsection.

B.4     LinkedIn Search Algorithm
  1. Search. Use Google’s site-specific search to search LinkedIn for the firm’s Salesforce firm
      name and country: site:linkedin.com/company “COMPANY_NAME” COUNTRY. If results are
      poor, search using less restrictive queries, including dropping exact search, removing company
      suffixes, reordering words, and using name stubs instead of full names.

  2. Filter. Among the results, identify the company page whose name closely matches the full
      Salesforce account name and whose LinkedIn-reported location matches the firm’s account
      country. Additional suggestive criteria include whether the company page is “claimed” by an
      owner (and therefore not automatically generated) and larger follower and employee counts.

  3. Record. Record the page’s LinkedIn slug from the URL (e.g., linkedin.com/company/
      activision; Figure A.3), and note possible issues with the match, such as company renames,

B.5     Match Quality Filters
We apply two pre-launch filters to screen out incorrect links between GitHub and Revelio and to
ensure adequate profile coverage among matched firms. First, we exclude matched firms with zero
recorded total employment or zero software engineers in Revelio prior to Copilot’s launch. Second,
we exclude matches in which the firm’s average pre-launch total employment in Revelio is smaller
than the number of GitHub licenses it purchased. Because each GitHub license corresponds to
an individual user license, a firm’s total workforce must be at least as large as its licensed count;
violations of this condition indicate either an incorrect entity match or severe profile undercoverage.
   These two criteria remove 1,215 matches, leaving 6,959 firms (80% of matched accounts) with
verified links and sufficient employment coverage. Excluded accounts are disproportionately public-
sector or government organizations, or firms headquartered outside North America and Europe,
where online profile coverage is sparser. Because both filters are defined strictly using pre-2023 data
prior to Copilot’s launch, they do not select firms on the basis of subsequent Copilot adoption or
post-launch employment trajectories.

stress test of Revelio’s name-matching algorithm using firms with known ground-truth identifiers.

B.8     Employment Data Validation
Our empirical analysis relies on Revelio Labs data to measure firm-level employment stocks, hiring
and separation flows, and workforce composition across technical roles. We validate these measures
against two independent external benchmarks: audited total headcount from regulatory filings and
administrative records of GitHub licenses.
   First, for the 19% of sample firms that file Form 10-K reports covered in Compustat, we compare
average annual employment in Revelio against total headcount reported in Compustat. The two
series differ in several institutional respects: 10-K filings report formal payroll employees at fiscal
year-end, whereas Revelio measures annual average active spells based on online profiles, which
can include contractors and freelancers. Despite these conceptual differences, the two measures are
strongly correlated in log levels (Pearson’s r “ 0.756, Spearman’s ρ “ 0.705). More importantly for
our longitudinal research design, which relies on within-firm variation over time, year-over-year log
employment changes in Revelio and Compustat also track each other closely, with a Pearson corre-
lation of 0.576 and a Spearman rank correlation of 0.598 (0.593 among eventual Copilot adopters
and 0.458 among non-adopters). The first two rows of Appendix Figure A.4 plot these level and
growth comparisons.
   Second, we validate Revelio’s measurement of technical workforces against firms’ pre-launch
GitHub license counts. Administrative license counts and Revelio role headcounts do not map one-
to-one: Enterprise contracts often provision licenses in blocks rather than matching exact active
headcount, not all software engineers require a license on a given enterprise instance, and firms fre-
quently assign licenses to technical workers in adjacent roles outside core software engineering. Even
so, pre-launch GitHub licenses and Revelio software engineering headcount exhibit a strong positive
correlation in log levels (Pearson’s r “ 0.684). At the median firm, the ratio of licensed GitHub
licenses to Revelio software engineers is approximately 2.4. When we expand the denominator to in-
clude adjacent technical roles that routinely interact with code repositories (specifically, application
development, data analytics, quality assurance, and systems and infrastructure engineering), the
log-level correlation remains similarly strong (r “ 0.673) and the median ratio of GitHub licenses to
technical workers is 0.9, confirming that Revelio captures the relevant population of technical staff
at sample firms. The third row of Appendix Figure A.4 plots these comparisons.
