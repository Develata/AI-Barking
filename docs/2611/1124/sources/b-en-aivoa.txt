Where Junior Hiring Slowed in Copilot’s First Year

In a study of early enterprise adoption, hiring slowed most in coding-heavy junior roles. Total technical headcount showed no clear change over the first year

A software team can end the year with roughly the same number of people even as the chances of joining it shrink. That is what researchers Lindsey Raymond of MIT and Eleanor Dillon of Microsoft Research found at firms that adopted GitHub Copilot early. The slowdown was concentrated in junior jobs that involved a lot of coding. Raymond and Dillon’s study [https://economics.mit.edu/sites/default/files/2026-11/AILaborDemand_06Nov2026.pdf]

Federal Reserve Chair Kevin Warsh set out a larger question in his November 16 speech about what he called superintelligence. In one scenario, higher productivity lifts growth and the United States stays close to full employment. In another, growth still exceeds its long-run average while automation displaces workers faster than they can find other jobs. A third brings a downturn if the expected returns on AI investment disappoint. Warsh offered those examples to help the Fed prepare. The Copilot study examines the rollout of an enterprise coding assistant, following firms from January 2021 through June 2025. It offers a narrower view of the change a labor market can undergo when new hiring diverges from the number of people already at work. Warsh’s speech [https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/speech/warsh20261116a.htm], the Copilot study [https://economics.mit.edu/sites/default/files/2026-11/AILaborDemand_06Nov2026.pdf]

Raymond and Dillon linked GitHub’s enterprise records to employment data at 5,414 multinationals. The firms had scheduled routine contract reviews before Copilot existed, and those with an earlier review were more likely to adopt it sooner. One year after the scheduled review, the authors estimate that firms whose review prompted early adoption made 29% fewer technical hires per quarter than they would have without that early adoption. The estimate covers all technical hires in that group, with the decline concentrated in coding-heavy junior jobs. New hires for experienced software-engineering roles were more likely to hold a master’s degree or doctorate. The study found no clear change in departures or in total technical headcount during the first year. The Copilot study [https://economics.mit.edu/sites/default/files/2026-11/AILaborDemand_06Nov2026.pdf]

Technical hiring was already slowing, including at firms that never adopted Copilot during the study. The estimate relies on the idea that the contract calendar affected hiring through the decision to adopt Copilot. The researchers looked for a similar effect of review timing on technical hiring before the tool was available, and on nontechnical hiring, such as accountants, after its release. They found neither pattern. The study leaves open where candidates who might have joined the companies went. Other employers or neighboring occupations, such as systems engineering, could have hired them. The Copilot study [https://economics.mit.edu/sites/default/files/2026-11/AILaborDemand_06Nov2026.pdf]

The White House paired potential productivity gains with job disruption in a report in 2016. In 2025, Stanford researchers used payroll records to find a relative weakening of employment among young workers in occupations highly exposed to AI. The Copilot study links a company’s use of a particular tool to its hiring records. A separate study of foreign affiliates, which used AI-related job ads as a sign of adoption, shows why that distinction matters. The share of junior employees fell mainly because senior employment grew. To understand how hard a field is to enter, look at the jobs it offers newcomers and what happens to those who seek them. 2016 White House report [https://obamawhitehouse.archives.gov/blog/2016/12/20/artificial-intelligence-automation-and-economy], Stanford’s 2025 payroll study [https://siepr.stanford.edu/publications/working-paper/canaries-coal-mine-six-facts-about-recent-employment-effects-artificial], foreign-affiliate study [https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/app/uploads/2026/11/Does-AI-Change-Labor-Demand.pdf]

Telephone automation offers a reason to follow those workers beyond any one company. In a study of U.S. cities that mechanized switching between 1920 and 1940, James Feigenbaum and Daniel Gross found that, among later cohorts of young U.S.-born White women, the loss of switchboard jobs was offset by gains in clerical and service work. Women already employed as operators fared worse. The comparable question today is where the people who would have started in junior software jobs can build a career. Feigenbaum and Gross’s study [https://www.dpgross.com/papers/answering-call-automation.pdf]

A coding assistant can take over a task that also gives a beginner practice. A firm that wants experienced engineers to review machine-written code and make design decisions has a choice about where that practice happens. It could give new hires a feature to carry through review, or reserve time for experienced engineers to work alongside them. I would watch junior hiring and where candidates eventually land alongside the size of the workforce. The engineers companies want to hire in a few years will need a chance to do the work now.
