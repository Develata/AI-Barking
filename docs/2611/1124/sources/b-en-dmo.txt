A 40% Drop in Junior Engineer Hiring: The First Clean Measurement of What Copilot Does to Labor Demand

 Digital Marketing Observer [https://www.digitalmarketingobserver.com/author/observer/]

 Posted on 6 hours ago 

 2 minutes read 

A new economics paper — AI-Assisted Programming and Labor Demand, by Lindsey Raymond (MIT) and Eleanor Wiske Dillon (Microsoft), posted to arXiv on November 23 — uses a natural experiment to measure what GitHub Copilot does to technical hiring. The result that has traveled furthest: junior software-engineer hiring fell roughly 40% at firms induced to adopt Copilot, while experienced-engineer hiring and systems/infrastructure hiring did not change.

The setup is what makes it credible. The researchers tracked 5,414 multinational firms covering 38% of the U.S. programming workforce, and exploited the fact that firms’ software-procurement review dates were set before Copilot launched. Firms with earlier reviews adopted sooner — which lets the paper separate Copilot’s effect from a firm simply being early-adopting and fast-growing.

The headline numbers, one year after adoption: quarterly technical hiring fell 29% at review-induced adopters, with no change in separations, overall headcount, or vacancy duration. The decline concentrated in the most code-intensive junior roles. Before Copilot, 72% of junior technical hires went into code-heavy roles; after adoption, that fell to 52%. Meanwhile, the paper finds the bar on experienced hires actually rose — about 1.3 additional years of experience and a 4.7-percentage-point higher share holding graduate degrees.

Two caveats from the authors themselves matter for how this gets used. First, these are firm-level responses, not economy-wide equilibrium: displaced junior candidates may find work at other firms or in other technical occupations, and some of the effect could be business-stealing between firms. Second, it captures an initial adjustment period, not a long-run steady state. What’s striking, the authors note, is that novices show the largest productivity gains from generative AI — yet it is their entry-level demand that contracts. Individual productivity gains, in other words, don’t automatically translate into stronger hiring. That distinction, more than any single percentage, is the finding worth arguing about.
