Abstract

Generative AI has demonstrated human-level performance on technical work tasks, yet evidence on how these task-level capabilities are changing labor demand remains scarce. We study the firm-level adoption of GitHub Copilot, an AI coding assistant, across 5,414 multinational firms accounting for 38% of the U.S. programming workforce. To identify causal effects, we exploit a natural experiment generated by firms’ predetermined software procurement cycles. Early AI-adopting firms hired more workers before the launch of GitHub Copilot and had larger, more technical, and older workforces. After one year, GitHub Copilot adoption reduces quarterly technical hiring by 29% among review-induced adopters, with no effect on separations, overall technical headcount, or vacancy duration. The hiring slowdown is concentrated in the subset of junior technical roles that involve the highest share of coding work. Overall, AI coding assistance causes firms to shift demand towards more experienced workers and roles that pair some coding with broader design responsibilities.

1   Introduction

LLMs now rival or exceed human performance on some tasks in mathematics, legal and financial analysis, and software development (Rein et al., 2023 [#bib.bib76]; Luong et al., 2025 [#bib.bib61]; Guha et al., 2023 [#bib.bib46]). Progress has been especially rapid in programming, where AI coding assistants can generate, explain, test, and debug code. As organizations incorporate these task-level improvements and redesign workflows, firms may reduce demand for human work that can now be done more effectively by machines while expanding opportunities for human work that complements AI. Understanding where these changes will be felt helps workers, firms, and policymakers adjust efficiently. However, the mapping from task-level technical capabilities to role- and firm-level demand changes is difficult to predict and difficult to measure. Field experiments can randomize access to AI across workers, but equivalent experiments across firms are generally infeasible. Absent randomization, firms that adopt AI early may already follow different growth trajectories or employ distinct management practices. Observational studies cannot reliably separate this endogenous choice by firms to adopt AI, other concurrent economic shocks, and the direct effects of AI on labor demand.

This paper exploits a natural experiment in firm-level AI adoption to provide causal evidence on how AI coding assistants affect hiring and employment for technical workers. While AI coding assistants are our broader object of study, we focus on GitHub Copilot because it is the setting for which we have data access and a clean empirical design. We study its rollout across 5,414 multinational firms employing 45 million workers worldwide, including 38% of the U.S. programming workforce. Our identification strategy rests on the centralized and complex nature of enterprise software procurement. In large firms, individual developers cannot independently deploy new software tools; instead, adoption must proceed through a formal procurement process involving dedicated legal, security, compliance, and purchasing personnel. All firms in our sample were GitHub customers using GitHub’s enterprise product for code management before GitHub Copilot’s January 2023 launch. These enterprise customers follow a regular schedule for contract reviews with GitHub, during which the relevant teams for coordinating contract evaluations and budgetary decisions are assembled. Firms faced a lower organizational cost of adopting GitHub Copilot during these existing GitHub reviews relative to initiating a separate procurement process. Because these schedules were established before GitHub Copilot existed, their timing provides plausibly exogenous variation in the timing of adoption, unrelated to firms’ underlying employment trajectories.

One year post-review, treated firms are 20 percentage points more likely to have adopted GitHub Copilot than not-yet-reviewed firms. Using a staggered difference-in-differences design that compares earlier- and later-reviewed firms, we estimate both the first-stage effect of review timing on GitHub Copilot adoption and its reduced-form effect on employment. Under additional assumptions, the Wald ratio of these two estimates identifies the causal effect of GitHub Copilot among firms induced to adopt earlier by the instrument of their pre-scheduled contract reviews.

We link GitHub’s administrative records on coding assistant adoption and contract reviews to employment data from Revelio Labs, which tracks firm-level headcount, hiring, and separations by role and seniority. This matched dataset allows us to observe actual enterprise AI adoption rather than relying on indirect proxies or occupational exposure scores. We focus on firms headquartered in the Americas and Europe, where GitHub’s enterprise customer base is concentrated and Revelio provides comprehensive coverage. The resulting monthly panel runs from January 2021 through June 2025, which spans two years before and two and a half years after GitHub Copilot’s January 2023 enterprise launch. Over this window, GitHub Copilot was the dominant enterprise coding assistant, predating the rollout of the enterprise versions of competing tools such as Cursor and Claude Code.

Within a firm, AI coding assistance affects labor demand through three channels: changes in the scale of software production, the allocation of tasks across roles and seniority levels, and productivity gains on existing tasks. While expanding software production raises overall labor demand, productivity gains on existing tasks reduce the labor required for a given level of output. Changes in how tasks are bundled and allocated across jobs further shape which roles and seniority levels firms require. Our empirical estimates capture the net effect of these potentially offsetting forces on employment, hiring, and separations.

Because we observe GitHub Copilot take up directly, we can cleanly identify the characteristics of early AI adopters and non-adopters. Consistent with Acemoglu et al. (2023) [#bib.bib6], early AI-adopting firms in our sample hired more workers each month before the launch of GitHub Copilot. They were also larger overall with a higher share of technical workers and a lower share of junior workers. These compositional differences illustrate the econometric challenge of separating the drivers of AI adoption from the effects of adoption. Our data also highlight that generative AI tools arrived during a tumultuous period of adjustment for technical workers that affected both adopting and non-adopting firms. From January, 2022 to December, 2024, monthly mean hiring rates among firms that never adopted GitHub Copilot during our sample decreased from 2.7% to 1.8%.

Using our procurement instrument, we find that GitHub Copilot adoption reduces technical hiring without increasing separations. One year after scheduled contract review, monthly hiring for technical workers, those who build, test, deploy, secure, or support software systems, is 7.6% lower than at firms whose reviews have not yet occurred. The corresponding Wald estimates imply a 29% reduction in quarterly technical hiring among complying firms that were induced to adopt GitHub Copilot earlier by their scheduled contract reviews. From a pre-period baseline of roughly 55 quarterly technical hires per firm, the reduced-form and IV estimates imply hiring slows to about 51 and 39 workers per quarter, respectively. Against an average firm workforce of 8,000 employees, these reductions represent less than 0.2% of headcount per quarter. By contrast, we find no meaningful change in separations, suggesting that layoffs have not been a meaningful tool of adjustment for these firms. We also find no change in overall technical headcount over the first year, consistent with the small scale of the hiring changes relative to the stock of workers at these established firms.

Technical job postings also fall while posting durations remain unchanged, suggesting that the drop in hiring reflects lower labor demand rather than reduced matching efficiency. Several placebo and robustness checks weigh against the possibility that this decline is driven by corporate budgeting cycles, broader hiring freezes or AI tools adoption. Scheduled contract reviews have no effect on technical hiring prior to GitHub Copilot’s launch or post-launch hiring for non-technical professionals such as accountants, and yield similar estimates when controlling for firm-size and industry trends.

Decomposing this decline in overall technical hiring, we find GitHub Copilot reduces hiring primarily for junior workers in code-intensive roles. We measure each role’s coding intensity using pre-GitHub Copilot job descriptions and distinguish between junior and experienced workers. Neither coding intensity nor seniority alone predicts the hiring response: more code-intensive roles do not experience larger overall hiring declines, and junior hiring does not fall uniformly across all technical roles. Rather, the hiring decline is concentrated at the intersection. Four quarters after contract review, a one-standard-deviation increase in coding intensity is associated with 6.8% lower quarterly hiring among junior workers, with no comparable gradient among experienced workers. Comparing the two ends of the coding-intensity distribution illustrates this pattern: among compliers, quarterly hiring of junior software engineers falls by roughly 40%, whereas hiring of experienced software engineers and of systems and infrastructure engineers is unaffected.

This contraction in entry-level coding positions reshapes both where junior workers enter firms and the seniority mix of technical teams. Before GitHub Copilot’s launch, 72% of junior technical hires entered code-intensive roles; our estimates imply that adoption reduces this share to 52%, leaving entry-level hiring nearly evenly split between more and less code-intensive roles.
This shift away from entry-level workers is striking given experimental evidence that novices realize the largest task-level productivity gains from generative AI, demonstrating that larger individual productivity effects need not translate into greater labor demand.

Although the volume of experienced software engineering hires remains stable, firms raise the bar on their qualifications and firm-specific tenure. Three to four quarters after contract review, new hires into experienced software engineering positions have roughly 1.3 additional years of potential experience and are 4.7 percentage points more likely to hold a master’s or doctoral degree. Experienced hiring also shifts toward workers with firm-specific human capital: external hires into experienced software engineering roles fall by approximately 13%, whereas internal fills remain unchanged. Among junior software engineering hires, by contrast, observable education, experience, and internal sourcing do not detectably change.

Finally, we examine whether GitHub Copilot alters the geographic distribution of hiring between firms’ home countries and India, their primary offshore engineering hub. In principle, AI coding tools could make lower-cost offshore talent more attractive by bridging time-zone and communication gaps and helping remote developers get up to speed on proprietary systems. Conversely, if offshore teams are disproportionately assigned well-defined implementation work, their roles may be more directly exposed to AI substitution. Empirically, we find no evidence that adoption shifts hiring toward India; rather, the point estimates point to a somewhat steeper slowdown in offshore hiring than at headquarters.

Together, our results show that AI coding assistance is already reshaping technical labor demand, with adjustments emerging within a year. Even within technical roles, the early impacts of AI fall unevenly, with marked decreases in hiring in the most code-intensive junior roles but no clear changes in hiring for other technical junior roles. For experienced workers, the impacts show up in the demand for specific skills rather than a change in heads hired. Two considerations are important when interpreting these findings. First, our estimates capture firm-level employment responses rather than economy-wide equilibrium effects. Reduced entry-level software engineering hiring at large adopting firms need not translate into lower aggregate technical employment, as displaced candidates may find work in other technical occupations or at smaller employers (Raymond, 2023 [#bib.bib75]; Acemoglu and Restrepo, 2020 [#bib.bib5]). Conversely, if adopting firms expand output at the expense of competitors, our estimates may capture business-stealing effects that do not translate into aggregate employment gains, potentially making market-wide effects more negative than the firm-level estimates (Acemoglu et al., 2020 [#bib.bib4]). Second, our estimates capture an initial adjustment period rather than a long-run steady state. As firms continue to understand the potential of AI tools, both to automate tasks and to support new work, hiring in different areas of technical work could continue to adjust in either direction.

This paper makes three contributions. First, we introduce a portable empirical strategy for identifying the firm-level causal effects of rapidly evolving technologies like enterprise AI. Because AI capabilities advance continuously across new tools and model upgrades, any single empirical study captures a specific product generation, such as our analysis of GitHub Copilot between 2023 and mid-2025. However, our identification strategy is not specific to a single tool or release. It exploits a pervasive feature of corporate software purchasing: enterprise vendors and AI providers routinely sell new products, capability upgrades, and expanded token budgets through existing customer contracts that follow predetermined review schedules. These recurring procurement cycles therefore provide a generalizable research design that researchers can use to provide causal estimates in successive waves of AI across firms, occupations, and industries.

Second, we provide the first credible causal evidence on how firm-level adoption of generative AI affects hiring, separations, and workforce composition. Existing studies report conflicting findings, documenting reduced demand for AI-exposed workers, null employment effects, or faster growth.11

2   Setting: GitHub Copilot for Enterprise

2.1   GitHub Enterprise

Commercial software development requires large teams of engineers to collaborate on shared, interdependent codebases. When an engineer writes code to update a product feature or internal system, that change cannot simply be released to users immediately. To prevent outages and security breaches, proposed code must first be reviewed by peers, run through automated tests to ensure it works with existing systems, scanned for security vulnerabilities, and approved for release. Large organizations must also satisfy strict security and regulatory standards by controlling which employees can modify live systems, maintaining audit logs of every change, and governing the geographic region where software code is stored and accessed.

GitHub Enterprise, owned by Microsoft and used by 90% of Fortune 100 companies, is the leading platform for managing this workflow at scale (GitHub, 2026b [#bib.bib43]; Wessel et al., 2023 [#bib.bib83]; GitHub, 2026a [#bib.bib44]). Importantly, GitHub Enterprise, which we refer to as GitHub, is not an AI tool. Rather, it is the platform firms use to store their code, collaborate on software development, review changes, and manage who has access to it. Focusing on pre-existing GitHub customers therefore isolates established employers with substantial software engineering operations.

2.2   GitHub Copilot

GitHub Copilot for Enterprise is an AI coding assistant powered by large language models (LLMs) that operates directly within a developer’s code editor.33
 3

 For the remainder of the paper, we refer to GitHub Copilot for Enterprise as “GitHub Copilot” to differentiate the coding assistant launched for enterprises in 2023 from other Microsoft Copilot AI tools and other versions of the coding assistant designed for individuals. Appendix Section A.2 [#A1.SS2] provides a brief history of ML-based coding assistants. Initially powered by OpenAI’s Codex, GitHub Copilot later integrated other frontier or customer-specific LLMs (Zhao, 2023 [#bib.bib87]; Zhao, 2024 [#bib.bib103]; Dohmke, 2024 [#bib.bib104]). GitHub Copilot offered two main features during our study period. In-line completion draws on surrounding code and comments to suggest single lines, entire functions, or unit tests, reducing the need to write boilerplate code or look up syntax (Panel A of Appendix Figure A.2 [#A1.F2]). The chat window provides a natural-language interface to the codebase for understanding the existing codebase, looking up business logic and debugging errors (Panel B). The fact that both types of assistance appear in the same application also reduces the need to switch applications to search documentation, or use email or Slack messages to ask questions, limiting performance losses from task switching (Gonçalves et al., 2011 [#bib.bib45]; Meyer et al., 2017 [#bib.bib64]; Leroy, 2009 [#bib.bib59]; Kumar et al., 2025 [#bib.bib56]).

From its January 2023 launch through mid-2025, GitHub Copilot faced little competition in the enterprise market (Nadella, 2025 [#bib.bib70]). In Stack Overflow’s 2023 Developer Survey, 56.0% of respondents regularly used GitHub Copilot, compared with 4.9% for the main enterprise alternative, Amazon CodeWhisperer (Stack Overflow, 2023 [#bib.bib80]; Wiggers, 2024 [#bib.bib85]). Other prominent competitors launched enterprise tiers only after our sample period ended, including Anthropic’s Claude Code in August 2025 and Cursor in October 2025 (Anthropic, 2025 [#bib.bib9]; Cursor, 2025 [#bib.bib32]). Observing GitHub Copilot licensing captures the primary margin of enterprise AI coding adoption during our study window, limiting the concern that non-adopting firms were adopting other enterprise AI coding assistants. Even if present, unmeasured adoption in the control group would attenuate our estimates toward zero. Panel A of Figure 1 [#S2.F1] shows that sample GitHub Copilot adoption rose gradually after January 2023, reaching 62% by mid-2025. Once firms adopt, they generally provision licenses across most of their technical workforce rather than restricting access to a small number of developers.

2.3   Enterprise Procurement Cycles and Contract Review

In large firms, individual developers and managers cannot simply download and use new software tools on their own. Because enterprise software often interacts with proprietary systems and sensitive corporate data, companies block employees from installing unapproved software applications on work computers (National Institute of Standards and Technology, 2018 [#bib.bib72]; Weill and Ross, 2004 [#bib.bib82]). Instead, adopting a new tool must go through a centralized procurement process: legal, security, and procurement teams must vet the vendor, verify that proprietary code and customer data are protected, and negotiate contract terms and pricing. Initiating this process outside of regular purchasing cycles requires incurring organizational frictions and coordination costs. A scheduled contract review already coordinates the legal, security, and finance teams for a planned evaluation. By contrast, an off-cycle purchase requires managers to assemble a separate business justification, wait in legal and security review queues, secure budgetary approval, and schedule meetings with all the relevant decision-makers.

Our identification strategy exploits recurring, pre-scheduled contract reviews built into firms’ existing contracts for GitHub’s source code software product. During these check-ins, a firm’s procurement team and GitHub’s sales representatives meet to adjust license counts, evaluate new features, and update contract terms. Even before GitHub Copilot’s release, account adjustments were heavily concentrated around these dates. In the pre-period, the share of firms increasing their core GitHub licenses quadrupled from roughly 10% to 40% in the two months surrounding a review, while the share reducing licenses rose from 4% to 14% (Appendix Figure A.5 [#A3.F5]).44
 4

 While firms adjust license counts in both directions, complete platform exits are extremely rare. This concentration shows that enterprise software procurement is sticky. Even for routine license adjustments on an existing product, whether adding seats or cutting unused ones, firm modifications cluster around scheduled review windows. A natural concern is that these contract reviews coincide with firms’ broader budgeting and hiring cycles. However, as we show in Section 6.1.3 [#S6.SS1.SSS3], scheduled reviews prior to GitHub Copilot’s launch had no effect on technical hiring or separations (Appendix Table A.17 [#A5.T17]).

Because review dates were set before GitHub Copilot’s launch, they could generate variation in when firms adopt that is plausibly unrelated to their underlying employment trajectories. We therefore use each firm’s first scheduled post-launch contract review, as recorded in December 2022, as an instrument for GitHub Copilot adoption.55
 5

 We use only the first post-launch review because subsequent review dates could be affected by earlier adoption decisions. Because these scheduled reviews are staggered across the calendar year rather than bunched in a single quarter, firms reach their first post-launch evaluation in different months, allowing us to compare firms arriving at a review to not-yet-reviewed firms at the same point in calendar time (Panel B of Figure 1 [#S2.F1]).

While procurement stickiness clearly concentrates adjustments to core GitHub licenses around review dates, how contract reviews shape the timing and speed of GitHub Copilot adoption is an empirical question. On one hand, reaching a pre-scheduled review substantially lowered the administrative friction of adopting the tool compared to initiating an off-cycle procurement process. Because introducing a new product like GitHub Copilot is more complex than adjusting seat counts for an already integrated product, these administrative frictions could be even more binding. On the other hand, strong demand for generative AI might lead firms to adopt GitHub Copilot immediately, although the slow path of adoption in Panel A of Figure 1 [#S2.F1] weighs against this. We examine this in our first-stage analysis in Section 4.3 [#S4.SS3].

3   Data

Our analysis combines three data sources: GitHub licensing data, GitHub customer relationship management (CRM) data, and Revelio Labs employment data.

3.1   GitHub Data

The GitHub data consist of monthly firm-level snapshots from January 2021 through November 2025 linked via internal identifiers. The licensing records report the number of GitHub and GitHub Copilot licenses held by each firm. We define GitHub Copilot adoption as the first month a firm holds a positive number of GitHub Copilot licenses and treat adoption as an absorbing state. To avoid endogenous sample entry, we restrict the sample to active GitHub customers as of September 2022. We obtain each firm’s first post-launch contract review date from the CRM records as of December 2022, one month prior to GitHub Copilot’s January 2023 launch.

3.2   Revelio Labs Employment Data

We measure employment stocks and flows using Revelio Labs, which aggregates public professional profiles and online job postings into standardized worker-employer histories. From individual employment spells and educational records, we construct monthly firm-level counts of headcount, new hires, and separations, both overall and by role-by-seniority cell, alongside monthly counts and average durations of job postings. Because Revelio draws primarily on LinkedIn profiles, coverage is strongest among large firms and technical workers in North America and Europe (Baird et al., 2026 [#bib.bib15]). We therefore restrict our sample to private-sector firms headquartered in the Americas and Europe (see Appendix Section B [#A2] for details on variable construction and coverage).

3.3   Sample Characteristics

We link GitHub customers to Revelio using automated name matching supplemented by AI-assisted LinkedIn searches and human review, applying pre-2023 quality filters to exclude false matches and firms with thin profile coverage (Appendix Sections B.3 [#A2.SS3]–B.7 [#A2.SS7] and Appendix Table A.3 [#A2.T3]). The resulting balanced panel tracks 5,414 firms monthly from January 2021 through June 2025. Benchmarking Revelio against external records confirms strong alignment: total headcount correlates closely with Compustat 10-K employment in log levels (r=0.756r=0.756) and annual log changes (r=0.576r=0.576), while pre-launch software engineering headcount correlates strongly with purchased GitHub licenses in log levels (r=0.684r=0.684; Appendix Section B [#A2]).

Sample firms are large, established employers: at the September 2022 baseline (Appendix Table A.4 [#A2.T4]), the average firm is 47 years old and employs 8,284 workers globally, with 61.6% headquartered in the United States and the largest concentrations in information technology (24.8%) and financial services (12.9%). We classify eight roles as technical: software, data, quality-assurance, infrastructure, systems, cybersecurity, and design engineers, alongside application developers. Technical roles represent 12.6% of sample employment (5.7% in software engineering alone) and 1.6 million U.S. workers, covering 38% of the U.S. programming workforce (Appendix Section B.9 [#A2.SS9] and Appendix Table A.5 [#A2.T5]).66

4   Empirical Strategy

4.1   Technical Labor-Market Trends and Pre-Adoption Differences

Simple comparisons between GitHub Copilot adopters and non-adopters face two empirical challenges. GitHub Copilot launched during a sharp macroeconomic contraction in technical labor demand. Panel A of Figure 2 [#S4.F2] plots the mean monthly technical hiring rate (new technical hires divided by lagged technical headcount). After rising from 2.5% to 3.0% in 2021, technical hiring began falling in March 2022, ten months before GitHub Copilot’s enterprise release, coinciding with the Federal Reserve’s rapid monetary tightening cycle (Iscenko and Millet, 2026 [#bib.bib51]). Eventual adopters and non-adopters followed different trajectories in the pre-period. 4.2   Instrumented Differences-in-Differences with Staggered Timing

To isolate exogenous variation in adoption timing, we instrument for GitHub Copilot adoption using the timing of each firm’s first scheduled GitHub contract review after GitHub Copilot’s launch (RiR_{i}). Let ℓ=t−Ri\ell=t-R_{i} index event time relative to this first scheduled review. We estimate the first-stage effect of reaching a contract review on GitHub Copilot adoption and the reduced-form effect on employment outcomes using the following event-study specifications:

(3)

where Di​tD_{it} is an absorbing indicator equal to one once firm ii holds a positive number of GitHub Copilot licenses and Yi​tY_{it} is an employment outcome such as headcount, hiring, or separations. For hiring and separations, which may be zero in a firm-month, we separately examine the extensive margin, an indicator for a positive value, and the intensive margin, the log outcome conditional on a positive value (Chen and Roth, 2024 [#bib.bib29]). We estimate firm-wide and broad occupational specifications on a balanced monthly panel; for more disaggregated role-by-seniority and within-firm analyses, where monthly hiring in individual cells is sparser, we aggregate the data to the quarterly level.

In both equations, firm fixed effects αi\alpha_{i} absorb time-invariant firm differences such as baseline size and industry, while calendar-period fixed effects λt\lambda_{t} absorb common macroeconomic and seasonal shocks. The coefficients πℓ\pi_{\ell} and ρℓ\rho_{\ell} measure the effect of contract review on GitHub Copilot adoption and employment at horizon ℓ\ell relative to firms that have not yet reached their scheduled review. Because contract reviews are staggered over time, conventional two-way fixed-effects estimators can be biased when treatment effects vary across cohorts or over time (Miyaji, 2024b [#bib.bib67]). We therefore estimate Equations (3 [#S4.E3]) and (4 [#S4.E4]) using the imputation estimator of Borusyak et al. (2024) [#bib.bib22], which is robust to treatment effect heterogeneity. Cohorts with contract reviews after 2023 serve as our last-treated comparison group, entering the sample only prior to their review date (RiR_{i}). All regressions are unweighted and thus represent effects across firms rather than employment-weighted effects. Standard errors are clustered at the firm level.

Under parallel trends and no anticipation in employment outcomes, the reduced form coefficient ρℓ\rho_{\ell} identifies the intention-to-treat (ITT) effect of reaching a scheduled GitHub contract review on employment. Parallel trends requires that, absent a contract review, employment trajectories do not vary systematically with a firm’s scheduled review date. No anticipation requires that firms do not adjust employment ahead of an approaching contract review. The reduced form is informative even without interpreting contract review as an instrument: it captures the intention-to-treat effect of reaching a scheduled opportunity to evaluate and procure GitHub Copilot on employment outcomes.

To move from the intent-to-treat effect to the effect of adopting GitHub Copilot itself, we scale the reduced-form estimates by the first-stage adoption response using the horizon-specific Wald ratio:

θ^ℓ=ρ^ℓπ^ℓ\widehat{\theta}_{\ell}=\frac{\widehat{\rho}_{\ell}}{\widehat{\pi}_{\ell}}

(5)

We construct 95% percentile confidence intervals for the Wald estimates using a firm-block bootstrap with 1,000 draws that re-estimates both legs and their ratio on the same subsample.

Interpreting θ^ℓ\widehat{\theta}_{\ell} as a causal treatment effect requires standard instrumental variables assumptions adapted to a staggered panel setting (Miyaji, 2024a [#bib.bib66]; Miyaji, 2024b [#bib.bib67]). Parallel trends and no anticipation must also hold for adoption.88
 8

 No anticipation does not rule out adoption before review; it requires that firms not time adoption in response to an approaching review. Contract review must increase adoption on average (relevance), must not delay adoption for any firm (monotonicity), and must affect employment only through GitHub Copilot adoption (exclusion). Finally, employment outcomes at horizon ℓ\ell depend on current adoption status rather than the specific duration of prior exposure (no carryover) (Miyaji, 2024a [#bib.bib66]).
Under these conditions, θ^ℓ\widehat{\theta}_{\ell} identifies the horizon-specific local average treatment effect on the treated (LATET) among compliers at horizon ℓ\ell (Miyaji, 2024a [#bib.bib66]; Miyaji, 2024b [#bib.bib67]). In our setting, compliers are firms that adopted GitHub Copilot once their scheduled review lowered the administrative hurdle, but would not yet have adopted off-cycle absent the review. For these firms, θ^ℓ\widehat{\theta}_{\ell} captures the effect of having adopted GitHub Copilot by horizon ℓ\ell relative to not yet having adopted. We evaluate the plausibility of these assumptions in Sections 4.3 [#S4.SS3], 4.4 [#S4.SS4], and 6.1.3 [#S6.SS1.SSS3].

 This means that while the share of “always takers” in the sample may remain constant, the share able to adopt off cycle changes. This shifting sample composition may by itself create an upward slope in the pre-review leads. Second, firms might genuinely be adopting GitHub Copilot at higher rates in anticipation of their approaching contract review. Because the baseline pre-review leads mix pre-launch zeros (December 2022 and prior) with post-launch observations (January 2023 and after), the pre-period coefficients cannot distinguish anticipation directly.

cumulative adoption in Figure 3 [#S4.F3] accumulates all prior adoptions over time. As a result, even a constant rate of off-cycle adoption will cause the cumulative share of adopters to drift upward as the review date approaches. By contrast, the monthly adoption hazard measures the flow into adoption in a given month: among firms that have not yet adopted entering month tt, it is the fraction that adopt GitHub Copilot for the first time during month tt among those who have not yet adopted in the prior month. If firms were timing their adoption in anticipation of an approaching contract review, this monthly hazard rate would rise in the months leading up to the review. However, among firms who have not yet adopted, the pre-review monthly adoption hazard is flat at roughly 4% across the months leading up to scheduled contract review and jumps sharply in the review month itself (ℓ=0\ell=0). This pattern shows no evidence of anticipation. Estimating hazard ratios in a complementary log-log model that controls for months since GitHub Copilot’s launch shows an equally flat pre-review profile (Appendix Figure A.6 [#A3.F6]).

4.4   Covariate Balance Across Contract Review Timing

Our identification strategy requires that, absent a contract review, firms scheduled for review at different dates would have followed parallel employment trajectories. While baseline covariate balance cannot verify parallel trends directly, it can help evaluate whether review timing is systematically related to pre-launch firm characteristics or employment growth.

Panel A of Figure 5 [#S4.F5] illustrates the differences by adoption status. Compared to non-adopters, eventual adopters were substantially larger, had higher baseline hiring and separation rates, employed a higher share of software engineers and a lower share of junior workers, and were more concentrated in the information technology sector. In contrast, Panel B compares firms with an early contract review to firms with a later review, split at the median contract review month of June 2023. Across all workforce composition, hiring, separation, and industry measures, the standardized differences are small and within the conventional 0.10 threshold for covariate balance (Austin, 2009 [#bib.bib12]).

The exception is firm size: firms with early contract reviews have log headcount that is 0.24 standard deviations higher than firms reviewed later. This pattern reflects how initial enterprise GitHub contracts were signed years prior to GitHub Copilot’s launch. Because sales commissions scale with contract value, enterprise sales teams prioritized the largest accounts early in the calendar year, and subsequent annual reviews remained anchored to those initial signing months. In our baseline specification, firm fixed effects absorb time-invariant size differences across firms, though they do not rule out differential employment trends by firm size. In Section 6.1.3 [#S6.SS1.SSS3], we add baseline firm-size-by-quarter fixed effects, as well as industry-by-quarter fixed effects, and show that our results are unchanged.

5   Conceptual Framework

To guide our empirical analysis, we introduce a simple accounting framework that decomposes GitHub Copilot’s effect on firm employment into three channels: the scale of software activity, the allocation of tasks across roles and seniority levels, and worker productivity.1111
 11

 Because our empirical design captures short run firm adjustments rather than economy-wide equilibrium responses in wages and employment, we use an accounting identity rather than an equilibrium model.

Let TiT_{i} denote the total volume of software-related tasks at firm ii. Workers are grouped into cells (r,s)(r,s) defined by role rr and seniority ss. Let mi,r​sm_{i,rs} be the share of software-related work assigned to cell (r,s)(r,s), with ∑r,smi,r​s=1\sum_{r,s}m_{i,rs}=1. Finally, ai,r​sa_{i,rs} is task-level productivity, defined as the number of tasks completed per unit of labor in that cell. Given this structure, the labor the firm requires in a particular role-by-seniority cell is:

Li,r​s=mi,r​s​Tiai,r​s.L_{i,rs}=\frac{m_{i,rs}T_{i}}{a_{i,rs}}.

(6)

Taking logs and differencing with respect to a firm-level GitHub Copilot adoption (Δ\Delta) gives:

Δ​ln⁡Li,r​s=Δ​ln⁡Ti⏟scale+Δ​ln⁡mi,r​s⏟task reallocation−Δ​ln⁡ai,r​s⏟productivity.\Delta\ln L_{i,rs}=\underbrace{\Delta\ln T_{i}}_{\text{scale}}+\underbrace{\Delta\ln m_{i,rs}}_{\text{task reallocation}}-\underbrace{\Delta\ln a_{i,rs}}_{\text{productivity}}.

(7)

Equation (7 [#S5.E7]) highlights three potentially offsetting forces. First, by lowering software development costs, GitHub Copilot may induce the firm to undertake more projects, expanding total task volume (Δ​ln⁡Ti>0\Delta\ln T_{i}>0) and raising labor demand proportionally across all cells. Second, GitHub Copilot may alter task allocation (Δ​ln⁡mi,r​s\Delta\ln m_{i,rs}). For example, senior engineers might use GitHub Copilot to handle implementation work previously delegated to junior engineers, or developers might absorb testing tasks previously assigned to quality assurance teams. Third, GitHub Copilot changes task productivity (Δ​ln⁡ai,r​s\Delta\ln a_{i,rs}).1212
 12

 In principle, the effect on task-level productivity could be positive or negative. While GitHub Copilot may speed up code generation, effective productivity could fall (Δ​ln⁡ai,r​s<0\Delta\ln a_{i,rs}<0). Completing assigned tasks faster reduces the labor required for a given workload, whereas spending extra time reviewing and debugging AI-generated code lowers effective productivity and increases labor requirements.

This framework clarifies what our empirical estimates can and cannot identify. In the data, we observe employment and hiring in each cell (Li,r​sL_{i,rs}), but we do not observe the firm’s total volume of software work (TiT_{i}), how tasks are divided across cells (mi,r​sm_{i,rs}), or task-level productivity (ai,r​sa_{i,rs}). Consequently, our estimates for any single group capture the combined net effect of all three channels.
However, comparing employment responses across cells within the same firm, such as junior versus experienced engineers in the same role, differences out the firm-wide scale effect (Δ​ln⁡Ti\Delta\ln T_{i}), which is common to all cells in firm ii. Any divergence across groups within a firm must therefore reflect differential changes in task allocation (Δ​ln⁡mi,r​s\Delta\ln m_{i,rs}) or task-level productivity (Δ​ln⁡ai,r​s\Delta\ln a_{i,rs}). Finally, because hiring decisions are forward-looking, our short-run estimates cannot separate responses to realized productivity gains from adjustments driven by managers’ beliefs and expectations about AI capabilities. As firms learn about the tool’s actual performance and as project scale and task allocation adjust at different speeds, these net employment effects may evolve over longer horizons.

6.1   Overall Effects on the Technical Workforce

6.1.1   Hiring, Separations, and Headcount

If sustained, the same hiring shortfall would produce a headcount gap of roughly 3.5% after about two years, approximately the magnitude needed for statistical significance at the 5% level given the current standard error, although both the effect and its precision may change at longer horizons. These results confirm that because monthly hiring flows represent a minor fraction of overall headcount, even sizable declines in hiring translate into modest cumulative effects on total workforce size over a one-year horizon.

Scaling these reduced form estimates by the first stage yields the implied effect of GitHub Copilot adoption on hiring flows among compliers. The month-twelve Wald estimate indicates that having adopted GitHub Copilot by month twelve reduces monthly technical hiring by approximately 34% among compliers. In the quarterly specification, which we use for our disaggregated analyses below, the fourth-quarter Wald estimate is −0.340-0.340 log points, or a 29% decline in quarterly technical hiring, with a 95% firm-block bootstrap confidence interval of [−0.948, 0.088][-0.948,\,0.088] log points (Appendix Table A.9 [#A4.T9] reports the full quarterly path). While the horizon-specific Wald ratios are similar in magnitude across quarters, their bootstrap intervals are wide, so the estimates cannot distinguish an immediate, constant effect from one that builds with exposure. Nor can our design separate the two, since identifying the duration of exposure would need additional instruments beyond the timing of contract review. Overall, although the Wald ratios are less precisely estimated than the reduced form because they compound sampling variation from both stages, both sets of estimates point to gradual workforce adjustment through lower hiring rather than worker quits or layoffs.

6.1.2   Recruiting Activity, Vacancy Duration, and Incidence

Realized hiring reflects both how many positions firms seek to fill and how quickly they fill open vacancies. A decline in hiring could therefore reflect either lower labor demand or a slower matching process, for instance if AI adoption induced firms to search for scarcer candidates with AI-specific skills. To distinguish between these explanations, Appendix Table A.11 [#A4.T11] reports the reduced form effects of a scheduled contract review on three quarterly measures of technical recruiting activity: job postings, duration postings remain open, and any posting activity. Four quarters after a scheduled contract review, the intensive margin of open technical postings falls by 14% (−0.148-0.148 log points). In contrast, contract review has no statistically detectable effect on the probability of having any open technical posting or the time a posting is open, although there are some pre-trends in posting duration. The slowdown in technical hiring seems to be driven by firms opening fewer positions rather than taking longer to fill existing vacancies.

Taken together, the employment flow and job posting results show that GitHub Copilot adoption initially slows technical workforce growth by reducing new hiring rather than displacing incumbent workers. Although the direct incidence of this adjustment falls on prospective hires, incumbent workers may still be affected indirectly. As hiring slows across adopting firms, fewer outside job openings can narrow incumbents’ outside options, reducing job-to-job mobility and weakening wage bargaining within the firm (Caldwell and Danieli, 2024 [#bib.bib26]; Jäger et al., 2024 [#bib.bib52]). Concentrating adjustment on the hiring margin could be consistent with models of labor demand with quasi-fixed labor costs or AI coding assistance devaluing the skills of novices (Oi, 1962 [#bib.bib73]; Abowd et al., 1999 [#bib.bib1]).

6.1.3   Placebo and Robustness Checks

We evaluate three alternative explanations to verify that the decline in technical hiring reflects GitHub Copilot adoption rather than recurring corporate budgeting cycles, contemporaneous firm-wide hiring freezes or general AI procurement, or differential trends by firm size and sector. First, scheduled contract reviews could in principle coincide with annual corporate budgeting or fiscal planning dates when firms routinely reset hiring targets. To test this directly, we conduct a pre-launch placebo check by assigning each firm its scheduled contract review date prior to GitHub Copilot’s 2023 launch. Although this restricts the post-review window to two quarters before GitHub Copilot’s launch, a recurring budget-driven contraction decided at the contract review should begin to register in realized hiring within that timeframe. Instead, we find no effects on overall technical hiring, separations, or junior and experienced software engineering hiring (Appendix Table A.17 [#A5.T17]). Moreover, even after GitHub Copilot’s release, the technical hiring decline does not appear as an immediate adjustment at the review date, as an annual budget reset would imply, but instead emerges gradually in the third and fourth quarters after review, tracking the rollout of GitHub Copilot licenses.

Second, even if pre-launch reviews did not affect employment, a firm’s 2023 review might coincide with a broader post-2022 white-collar hiring freeze, an IT-wide budget cut, or the simultaneous procurement of general office AI tools (although GitHub Copilot was the only AI product sold by GitHub’s dedicated sales force). To test for firm-wide shocks, we examine hiring for accountants, the most common non-technical professional occupation in our data (employed by 98% of sample firms, with a pre-launch median of 34 per firm) and one exposed to both general AI tools and broader white-collar hiring slowdowns. Appendix Figure A.12 [#A5.F12] shows no decline in either log accountant hires or the probability of hiring an accountant following contract review. Four quarters after review, the reduced-form estimate for log accountant hiring is a statistically insignificant 0.0350.035 log points. Furthermore, as we show in Section 6.2 [#S6.SS2], the slowdown is not uniform even within the technical workforce: hiring is unchanged in less code-intensive technical roles and among experienced engineers, weighing against a broad technical or IT-department hiring freeze.

Third, because firms with earlier contract-review dates have larger pre-launch workforces, our estimates could be confounded if larger and smaller firms, or different sectors, followed distinct post-launch hiring trajectories. In Appendix Table A.18 [#A5.T18], we re-estimate our specifications adding interactions between calendar-quarter fixed effects and pre-launch firm-size terciles, indicators for the information and professional-services sectors, or both. Across all specifications, the four-quarter reduced-form estimates for total technical hiring and separations change by at most 0.0080.008 log points (Columns 1 and 2), confirming that differential trends by firm size or sector do not drive our findings.

6.2   Hiring Composition by Code Intensity and Seniority

The preceding results show that GitHub Copilot adoption reduces overall technical hiring. We next examine whether this decline is spread evenly across the technical workforce or concentrated among particular roles and seniority levels, changing the composition of new hires.

6.2.1   Measuring the Coding Content of Technical Work

We define a position’s code intensity as the share of its described responsibilities devoted to activities within GitHub Copilot’s core domain during our study period: writing, completing, testing, documenting, explaining, and debugging code. To measure code intensity across roles and seniority levels, we use the text of LinkedIn position descriptions for technical jobs at sample firms with start dates between 2018 and 2022, prior to GitHub Copilot’s launch.1313
 13

 Because LinkedIn users can edit position descriptions retrospectively, some text may have been written or revised after GitHub Copilot’s launch; our sample restriction applies to the position’s start date. Unlike occupation-level task measures that assign a single average to a broad occupational code, position descriptions capture how responsibilities vary both across granular technical roles and across seniority levels within a role. Appendix Figure A.8 [#A4.F8] illustrates this variation using examples from outside our sample: a software engineer describes building a job-scheduling platform and implementing APIs (Panel A), whereas a test automation engineer combines programming in Python and LabVIEW with physical design and testing of liquid-nitrogen heat exchangers (Panel B). Similarly, within infrastructure engineering, a technical lead describes setting team strategy and recruiting, while a technical lead manager combines engineering management with building machine learning infrastructure (Panels C and D).

We score each description on this dimension using Claude Sonnet 4.6 (see Appendix Section B.10 [#A2.SS10] for the prompt and implementation details). The model observes only the free-text description, without the job title, occupation, seniority, location, firm, or start date. For our baseline cross-role analysis, we average the description-level scores within each of the eight technical roles. Code intensity is thus an ex ante measure of task overlap with GitHub Copilot’s capabilities rather than a measure of actual time spent coding or realized GitHub Copilot usage.

We refer to these four roles above the median as code-intensive roles. Coding is less central in infrastructure engineering (19%), cybersecurity engineering (15%), systems engineering (7%), and design engineering (5%), which, in our sample, primarily captures hardware, silicon, and product design roles where programming supports broader operational, security, or physical engineering responsibilities.

However, pooling all hires within a role may obscure heterogeneity across seniority levels, either because firms reduce junior hiring uniformly across technical roles as in cyclical downturns (Forsythe, 2022 [#bib.bib38]), or because GitHub Copilot substitutes more readily for the coding tasks performed by junior workers than for those bundled into experienced roles. 6.2.3   Implied Magnitudes and Compositional Shifts

Because the hiring contraction is concentrated among junior positions in code-intensive roles, GitHub Copilot adoption reshapes both the entry paths of junior workers and the seniority structure of technical teams (Figure 9 [#S6.F9]). At baseline, 72% of all junior technical hires enter through code-intensive roles; with GitHub Copilot adoption, that share falls to 52%, leaving entry-level hiring nearly evenly split between more and less code-intensive roles while the role mix of experienced hiring is largely unchanged (69% to 74% code-intensive; Panel A). Viewed within role groups, the junior share of code-intensive hiring drops from 57% to 45%, whereas the junior share in less code-intensive roles rises from 54% to 68% (Panel B). In level terms (Panel C), junior hiring in code-intensive roles drops by about 7 spells per firm-quarter, while experienced hiring in these roles is fairly flat. Comparing the two ends of the coding-intensity distribution directly illustrates this divergence (Appendix Table A.14 [#A4.T14]; see Appendix Figure A.11 [#A4.F11] for the monthly reduced form event studies). GitHub Copilot adoption reduces quarterly junior software engineering hiring by approximately 40% (−0.513-0.513 log points), with no detectable decline among experienced software engineers (−0.160-0.160 log points) or systems and infrastructure engineers (0.2120.212 log points).

Through the lens of our conceptual framework, a common change in the scale of software production cannot explain why junior and experienced hiring diverge within the same role. Instead, this divergence reflects differences across seniority levels in task-level productivity gains, job bundling, or task allocation. First, GitHub Copilot may generate larger direct labor-saving productivity gains on the coding tasks typically assigned to junior workers. Second, junior and experienced positions differ in how coding is bundled with other responsibilities, which explains why the seniority gap persists even when coding intensity is measured at the role-by-seniority cell level (Garicano et al., 2026 [#bib.bib42]). Whereas junior positions in code-intensive roles center on discrete implementation tasks that can be specified, delegated, and evaluated independently at relatively low cost, experienced positions bundle code production with system architecture, domain judgment, and cross-team accountability. Because coding is more separable from the rest of the job in junior positions, GitHub Copilot may substitute more readily for junior hiring even if it improves productivity in both groups.

At the same time, the flat code-intensity gradient for experienced hiring does not imply that GitHub Copilot leaves experienced work unchanged or simply complements experienced labor. Even as GitHub Copilot saves experienced engineers time on their existing coding tasks, firms may expand the scope of work assigned to them, or change their time allocation. Experienced engineers may use GitHub Copilot to complete implementation work previously delegated to junior teammates, or spend more time reviewing, debugging, and integrating AI-generated code alongside their core architectural and coordination duties. These offsetting forces, labor savings on existing coding tasks alongside task reallocation from junior positions and new verification demands, could leave net experienced hiring approximately flat across the code-intensity distribution even as GitHub Copilot reshapes their day-to-day work.

6.3   Experience, Credentials, and Internal Hiring among Software Engineers

Although firms maintain their volume of experienced software engineering hires after adopting GitHub Copilot, stable hiring volume could mask changes in the types of engineers firms recruit. Within software engineering, the largest and most code-intensive occupation in our sample, we next examine whether firms adjust the educational credentials, prior experience, and internal sourcing of the workers they hire. At baseline, experienced software engineers average 8.7 years since earning their bachelor’s degree, compared with 4.8 years among junior engineers, and are significantly more likely to hold an academic master’s or doctoral degree (Appendix Table A.15 [#A4.T15]).

In principle, AI coding tools could either substitute for or complement accumulated general and firm-specific human capital. If GitHub Copilot makes programming knowledge and codebase conventions readily accessible on the job, firms may relax degree, experience, or internal-tenure requirements when filling positions. Conversely, if deeper technical training, industry experience, or familiarity with the firm’s architecture makes engineers more effective at directing AI tools, evaluating generated code, and coordinating system design, firms could raise the bar on these qualifications among new hires.

Although AI coding assistants may deliver large task-level productivity gains to novices in experimental settings, in practice they may complement accumulated general and firm-specific human capital.

6.4   Geographic Composition of Technical Hiring

Beyond shifting the roles, seniority, and credentials of new hires, AI coding assistants could also reshape where firms locate technical work. Firms in our sample employ technical workers both domestically and offshore, with India the most common offshore location. During 2021–2022, approximately 58% of firms employed at least one technical worker in India, while 83% employed at least one worker there in any occupation. This concentration reflects India’s emergence as a leading global hub for offshore software development. Beginning in the early 2000s, India developed a large export-oriented software services industry, supported by a substantial pool of English-speaking engineers, strong technical universities, and labor costs well below those in the United States and Europe (Arora and Gambardella, 2005 [#bib.bib10]; Parthasarathy, 2013 [#bib.bib74]).

In principle, AI coding assistants could disproportionately benefit offshore workers through several channels. First, LLMs can facilitate communication across language barriers, reducing coordination costs associated with geographically distributed engineering teams (Brynjolfsson et al., 2025b [#bib.bib25]). Second, coding assistants can help workers interpret technical specifications, navigate unfamiliar codebases, and understand internal systems. These capabilities may be particularly valuable for offshore engineers, who often have less access to the informal knowledge and institutional context available to colleagues at a firm’s headquarters. Third, given substantial wage differentials between domestic and offshore engineers, AI tools that reduce these productivity and coordination disadvantages could make offshore labor an even more attractive substitute for domestic labor.

On the other hand, tasks that can be performed offshore may be more easily codified and therefore more susceptible to automation. Moreover, firms may be more willing to reduce hiring outside their headquarters country, where reputational concerns, social pressures, and information frictions may impose fewer constraints on employment adjustments (Landier et al., 2009 [#bib.bib57]; Bassanini et al., 2017 [#bib.bib17]).

To examine this margin, Appendix Table A.16 [#A4.T16] estimates the effects of contract review on technical hiring in firms’ headquarters countries (“domestic”) and India, restricting the sample to the 4,509 firms with a presence in India during the pre-period. The reduced-form estimates are imprecise, partly reflecting the smaller sample and sparser hiring abroad, and do not reveal statistically significant differences in hiring responses across locations. Four quarters after contract review, the point estimate for India-based technical hiring is −0.060-0.060 log points (SE=0.189\text{SE}=0.189), compared with −0.029-0.029 log points for domestic technical hiring. Although the estimates are too imprecise to detect differences across locations reliably, they provide no evidence of a shift toward offshore technical hiring following GitHub Copilot adoption. If anything, the point estimates suggest a larger slowdown in India.

A simple back-of-the-envelope calculation grounds this third benchmark: because our fourth-quarter treatment effect is similar in magnitude to the overall 2022 to 2024 hiring decline (29%) and roughly half of the sample had adopted GitHub Copilot by 2024, applying the complier effect to all adopters accounts for about half of the aggregate slowdown, with half driven by other macroeconomic factors.

We emphasize that these calculations are illustrative benchmarks rather than exact counterfactuals for four reasons. First, treatment effects are estimated in event time over the year following each firm’s 2023 contract review (spanning 2023Q1 through 2024Q4 across cohorts), whereas the aggregate decline Δ\Delta is measured between calendar years 2022 and 2024. The decomposition therefore treats the average effect over the first year post-review as the average effect in the 2024 post-period. Second, ρ^ℓ\widehat{\rho}_{\ell} and θ^ℓ\widehat{\theta}_{\ell} are estimated in logs on unweighted firm-quarters with positive hiring, so applying them to firm hiring levels assumes treatment effects are homogeneous across firms. Third, the all-adopters benchmark (Appendix Equation (12 [#A6.E12])) does not have a clean upper bound. Because always-takers adopt earlier on average than compliers, they may have initially deployed earlier, less capable versions of the tool, or benefit more from the scale effects of AI adoption, leading to smaller hiring reductions. On the other hand, they could have larger hiring slowdowns. Fourth, the calculation is partial equilibrium: it treats the hiring of non-adopting firms as invariant to adoption elsewhere, whereas in equilibrium some candidates not hired by adopting firms may be absorbed by other employers in the sample. Finally, our design cannot separate which specific factors drove the remaining half to four-fifths of the hiring slowdown, such as monetary tightening, the correction of pandemic-era overhiring, changes in the tax treatment of software research and development, or AI adoption operating through other channels.

7   Conclusion

As AI systems become more capable, a central question is how firms adjust their workforces. Which roles will experience increases, decreases, or no change in demand? Will firms adjust on the hiring margin, by reallocating or separating from incumbent workers, or all three? We study these margins among large, technology-intensive multinational firms adopting GitHub Copilot, an early enterprise AI coding assistant, exploiting variation in the timing of pre-existing enterprise software contract reviews to identify causal effects. We find that the initial labor market adjustment occurs on the hiring margin rather than through separations. Rather than reducing recruitment uniformly, adopting firms cut entry-level hiring in code-intensive roles while maintaining the volume of experienced hires and raising the bar on their education, prior experience, and firm-specific tenure. The early impact of AI coding assistance thus appears less as broad job displacement and more as a narrowing of entry-level pipelines alongside greater demand for accumulated human capital. Thus, even when generative AI provides the largest task-level productivity boost to novices, those individual gains need not translate into greater labor demand.

Our findings also point to several open questions about how the labor market effects of AI will evolve across firms and over time. First, our estimates characterize large, established multinationals operating complex software systems. Smaller or younger firms facing fewer coordination constraints may reorganize production more rapidly, or respond to lower software development costs by expanding output and technical hiring more elastically. Second, our sample period captures the first wave of AI coding assistants, which operated primarily by autocompleting code within a developer’s workflow. Newer agentic systems capable of executing multi-step engineering tasks autonomously could deepen substitution or extend it further up the seniority distribution. Conversely, if early corporate adjustments partly reflected expectations that outpaced realized productivity gains, or if shrinking the junior cohort eventually creates bottlenecks in the supply of senior talent, firms’ hiring strategies may rebalance over a longer horizon.

Finally, our firm-level estimates do not capture wage adjustments or aggregate employment. Slower hiring and shifts in skill demand can alter compensation and the division of surplus between firms and workers even when total headcount is stable. Moreover, a contraction in a specific entry channel at large adopting firms need not translate one-for-one into lower aggregate technical employment (Raymond, 2023 [#bib.bib75]; Acemoglu et al., 2020 [#bib.bib4]). Workers who would otherwise have joined these firms as junior software engineers may instead enter adjacent roles such as systems or infrastructure engineering, or take software engineering positions at smaller or later-adopting firms. On the other hand, firms who adopt AI may steal market share from other firms. Determining how these firm-level shifts in hiring and career ladders reshape market-wide employment, wages, and long-run labor demand remains a central question for future research.

Appendix A Background on GitHub and AI Coding Tools

A.1   GitHub

Pull-based software development originated in the open-source community. In 2005, Linus Torvalds released Git, a distributed version control system that allowed developers to maintain independent copies of a codebase and propose changes for integration. Three years later, Tom Preston-Werner, Chris Wanstrath, and P.J. Hyett launched GitHub as a web-based collaboration layer built on top of Git, introducing a structured pull-request workflow through which developers submit, review, and merge proposed code changes (Appendix Figure A.1 [#A1.F1]). The platform grew from 100,000 users in 2009 to more than 31 million by 2018.

In its early years, GitHub served primarily as an open-source collaboration platform offering free hosting for public repositories. Commercial adoption grew out of bottom-up developer demand: as software engineers increasingly used personal GitHub accounts for workplace projects in place of legacy internal tools, corporate customers requested an enterprise version with centralized administration and security controls, prompting the launch of GitHub Enterprise in 2011. Over the following decade, the pull-request workflow became the standard model for commercial software development at large organizations. Microsoft acquired GitHub in 2018, but GitHub continued to operate as an independent business unit with its own enterprise sales force and contracting systems (Nagle et al., 2023 [#bib.bib71]).

A.2   AI Coding Tools

Automated code assistance predates large language models. Rule-based tools such as Microsoft’s IntelliSense, introduced in Visual Studio in 1997, provided syntax-aware identifier completion and error flagging. By 2006, code-completion tools were widely used by Java developers, although early empirical studies found that they primarily reduced documentation lookup rather than substantially accelerating code production (Murphy et al., 2006 [#bib.bib69]; Jiang and Coblenz, 2024 [#bib.bib53]). Later tools such as Tabnine introduced statistical and early deep-learning models to offer short line-by-line completions.

Large language models marked a step change in code generation capabilities. Because LLMs predict subsequent tokens conditional on surrounding context, programming languages are particularly well suited to their architecture: code follows strict syntactic and semantic rules, vast corpora of paired source code and natural-language documentation are publicly available for training, and compilers and test suites provide objective verification signals. These features supported rapid capability gains. On SWE-bench, an evaluation benchmark introduced in 2023 that tasks models with resolving real GitHub issues across 12 Python repositories, frontier model resolution rates rose from under 5% at launch to nearly 80% by early 2026 (Jimenez et al., 2024 [#bib.bib54]; Maslej et al., 2025 [#bib.bib62]).

GitHub Copilot first launched as a technical preview in June 2021 (Friedman, 2021 [#bib.bib39]). GitHub Copilot for Individuals became generally available in June 2022, followed by GitHub Copilot for Business for enterprise customers in early 2023. Developer uptake was rapid: in a 2023 industry survey, 56% of professional developers reported using GitHub Copilot, more than four times the share of the next most common AI coding tool (Stack Overflow, 2023 [#bib.bib80]). The initial version of GitHub Copilot was powered by OpenAI Codex, a model trained on billions of lines of public source code. Subsequent releases expanded the product beyond in-line autocomplete to include an interactive chat interface (Appendix Figure A.2 [#A1.F2]), allowed users to select among multiple frontier models (McKenna and Perz, 2026 [#bib.bib63]), and enabled enterprise customers to index private repositories to ground responses in their internal codebase.

A growing body of research examines the effects of GitHub Copilot at the individual developer level; Ani et al. (2024) [#bib.bib8] provide an early review. Controlled experiments and field studies evaluate its effects on task-level developer speed and output (Cui et al., 2026 [#bib.bib31]), the security and quality of generated code (Fu et al., 2025 [#bib.bib40]), and computer science education (Shah et al., 2025 [#bib.bib58]).

Appendix B Data Construction, Measurement, and Validation

B.1   Revelio Occupational and Seniority Measures

Workers on LinkedIn often describe similar jobs using different titles, while identical titles can correspond to different occupations across firms and industries. Revelio standardizes free-text job titles using a hierarchical clustering algorithm that incorporates job titles, text from workers’ and employers’ job descriptions, workers’ skills, coworkers, and prior experience (Revelio Labs, 2026a [#bib.bib78]). For example, titles such as “Software Developer,” “Backend Engineer,” and “Full Stack Developer” are grouped into a common “Software Engineering” category. We use Revelio’s 150-cluster job taxonomy, the most granular classification recommended for firm-by-year analysis (Revelio Labs, 2026b [#bib.bib77]).

We also use Revelio’s measure of worker seniority. Because titles convey different levels of seniority across firms and occupations, Revelio constructs a seniority score using an ensemble model that combines an individual’s title, company, and industry with the duration and seniority of previous employment and age (Revelio Labs, 2026b [#bib.bib77]). The resulting measure ranges from entry-level to senior executive positions.1414
 14

 The seniority categories range from entry level (e.g., account intern, paralegal, software engineer trainee), junior (e.g., Junior Software QA Engineer, Legal Adviser), associate (e.g., attorney, senior tax accountant), manager, director, executive, and senior executive (e.g., CEO, CFO, COO) (Revelio Labs, 2026b [#bib.bib77]).

From the underlying individual position spells, we construct a monthly panel of employment stocks, new hires, and separations at the firm level, as well as disaggregated panels at the firm-by-role and firm-by-role-by-seniority levels.

B.2   Revelio Coverage and Measurement

Online professional profiles over-represent white-collar and technical occupations, younger cohorts, and larger, more established firms. Coverage is particularly comprehensive in information technology and among professional, scientific, and technical workers in North America and Europe (Baird et al., 2026 [#bib.bib15]). These features make Revelio well suited to tracking the technical workforces of large GitHub customers. Because coverage is thinner in other regions and has grown over time with platform adoption, we restrict our sample to firms headquartered in the Americas and Europe.

In addition to raw spell counts, Revelio adjusts their data to correct for two sources of measurement error (Revelio Labs, 2026b [#bib.bib77]). First, a cross-sectional sampling weight adjusts for differences in profile representation across occupations, industries, and countries. Second, a time-series adjustment accounts for reporting lags between when an employment transition occurs and when a worker updates their online profile. Both adjustments are calibrated against firm-level headcount benchmarks from regulatory filings. Throughout our analysis, we use these adjusted employment and flow series.

B.3   GitHub–Revelio Firm Matching Procedure

We link GitHub administrative records to Revelio Labs using firm name, country, industry, and size indicators. As a first step, we apply Revelio’s internal name-matching algorithm, which strips common legal suffixes (such as “LLC,” “Inc.,” and “GmbH”) and administrative tags (such as “Parent”) before performing an exact string match against standardized company names in Revelio’s database. The algorithm excludes company profile pages with zero recorded employees and, when a match corresponds to a corporate subsidiary, aggregates employment to the ultimate parent firm.

This initial string-matching step yields a high-confidence match for approximately 64% of GitHub accounts. The remaining accounts are not matched automatically because their recorded names are generic, abbreviated, or formatted differently across the two databases. Discrepancies typically arise when a firm operates under a distinct brand or division name on professional networking platforms (e.g., “ABC Inc.” versus “ABC Science”), when non-English characters or transliterations differ across systems, or when the GitHub license is held by a subsidiary whose legal name does not appear as a standalone entity in Revelio. To resolve these cases, we combine an AI-assisted matching pipeline with human verification, using firm country, industry, and employee size to identify and validate the corresponding company profile page, as detailed in the next subsection.

For firms not matched by Revelio’s algorithm, we have an AI agent (Claude Sonnet 4.6) follow this algorithm to construct a candidate match. A second agent then scores the confidence of each proposed match as high, medium, low, or no_match by evaluating the similarity between the firm’s Salesforce name and the LinkedIn page name, whether the country matches, and whether the LinkedIn page has a plausible number of employees. We retain only high- and medium-confidence matches for the final sample.

RCID lookup.

Each matched LinkedIn slug is converted to a Revelio company identifier (RCID) by querying Revelio’s company_mapping table on WRDS, which maps LinkedIn URLs to RCIDs. Of the 2,803 high- and medium-confidence agent matches, 2,679 (96%) yield an RCID, confirming that the matched LinkedIn pages correspond to firms in Revelio’s employment database.

Duplicate resolution.

In some cases, multiple GitHub firms (typically a parent and subsidiary) match to the same LinkedIn page. We identify 82 such duplicate matches that are parent-subsidiary relationships and consolidate them.

URL repair.

A second human reviewer checked, in four batches, the firms whose LinkedIn slugs were dead, redirected, or otherwise inaccessible. The reviewer provided corrected slugs where possible, recovering matches for 46 of 59 firms with broken URLs. This URL repair step corrects link rot but does not independently re-evaluate the agent’s firm identification.

B.5   Match Quality Filters

We apply two pre-launch filters to screen out incorrect links between GitHub and Revelio and to ensure adequate profile coverage among matched firms. First, we exclude matched firms with zero recorded total employment or zero software engineers in Revelio prior to GitHub Copilot’s launch. Second, we exclude matches in which the firm’s average pre-launch total employment in Revelio is smaller than the number of GitHub licenses it purchased. Because each GitHub license corresponds to an individual user license, a firm’s total workforce must be at least as large as its licensed count; violations of this condition indicate either an incorrect entity match or severe profile undercoverage.

These two criteria remove 1,215 matches, leaving 6,959 firms (80% of matched accounts) with verified links and sufficient employment coverage. Excluded accounts are disproportionately public-sector or government organizations, or firms headquartered outside North America and Europe, where online profile coverage is sparser. Because both filters are defined strictly using pre-2023 data prior to GitHub Copilot’s launch, they do not select firms on the basis of subsequent GitHub Copilot adoption or post-launch employment trajectories.

B.6   Validation Sample and Match Accuracy

We evaluate the accuracy of our firm-matching pipeline through three validation exercises. The first two draw random samples of 100 firms from the algorithmic and AI-assisted pipelines, respectively, and compare the assigned match against the company profile page independently identified by a human reviewer using firm name, country, and industry. The third exercise conducts a larger-scale stress test of Revelio’s name-matching algorithm using firms with known ground-truth identifiers.

1. 

Revelio algorithmic match (precision audit, N = 100). The reviewer compared the Revelio-assigned company (RCID) to the firm they identified independently, recording whether the match was correct, incorrect (wrong firm), or whether no correct match should have existed. Because the audit draws only from firms Revelio matched, it measures precision but not recall.

2. 

AI agent-assisted match (precision and recall, N = 100). The reviewer compared the agent’s proposed LinkedIn slug to the slug they identified independently. Draws include firms the agent declined to match, so the sample supports both precision and recall.

3. 

Name-perturbation stress test (N = 995). For 995 firms with known ground-truth RCIDs, we append legal suffixes (e.g., “Ltd.,” “GmbH”) and parent qualifiers (e.g., “ – Parent”) to the firm name before sending it through Revelio’s matching algorithm. This isolates the algorithm’s sensitivity to name-cleanliness variation holding the target firm fixed.

Table A.1 [#A2.T1] reports precision, recall, and F1 for each test. Revelio’s algorithmic matches are correct for 83 of 100 sampled firms (precision 0.83). The AI agent achieves higher precision (0.93) and near-complete recall (0.99), for an F1 of 0.96: most incorrect cases are firm-level errors (typically a subsidiary matched to its parent), not missed matches. The name-perturbation test yields the same precision (0.93) as the agent pipeline but lower recall (0.76): the algorithm returns no match for 22.7% of perturbed names. This reflects the algorithm’s reliance on exact-match-after-cleaning and motivates the agent-assisted pipeline for firms with non-standard name representations.

B.8   Employment Data Validation

Our empirical analysis relies on Revelio Labs data to measure firm-level employment stocks, hiring and separation flows, and workforce composition across technical roles. We validate these measures against two independent external benchmarks: audited total headcount from regulatory filings and administrative records of GitHub Enterprise licenses.

First, for the 19% of sample firms that file Form 10-K reports covered in Compustat, we compare average annual employment in Revelio against total headcount reported in Compustat. The two series differ in several institutional respects: 10-K filings report formal payroll employees at fiscal year-end, whereas Revelio measures annual average active spells based on online profiles, which can include contractors and freelancers. Despite these conceptual differences, the two measures are strongly correlated in log levels (Pearson’s r=0.756r=0.756, Spearman’s ρ=0.705\rho=0.705). More importantly for our longitudinal research design, which relies on within-firm variation over time, year-over-year log employment changes in Revelio and Compustat also track each other closely, with a Pearson correlation of 0.5760.576 and a Spearman rank correlation of 0.5980.598 (0.593 among eventual GitHub Copilot adopters and 0.458 among non-adopters). The first two rows of Appendix Figure A.4 [#A2.F4] plot these level and growth comparisons.

Second, we validate Revelio’s measurement of technical workforces against firms’ pre-launch GitHub license counts. Administrative license counts and Revelio role headcounts do not map one-to-one: Enterprise contracts often provision licenses in blocks rather than matching exact active headcount, not all software engineers require a license on a given enterprise instance, and firms frequently assign licenses to technical workers in adjacent roles outside core software engineering. Even so, pre-launch GitHub Enterprise licenses and Revelio software engineering headcount exhibit a strong positive correlation in log levels (Pearson’s r=0.684r=0.684). At the median firm, the ratio of licensed GitHub Enterprise licenses to Revelio software engineers is approximately 2.4. When we expand the denominator to include adjacent technical roles that routinely interact with code repositories (specifically, application development, data analytics, quality assurance, and systems and infrastructure engineering), the log-level correlation remains similarly strong (r=0.673r=0.673) and the median ratio of GitHub Enterprise licenses to technical workers is 0.90.9, confirming that Revelio captures the relevant population of technical staff at sample firms. The third row of Appendix Figure A.4 [#A2.F4] plots these comparisons.

Appendix F Contribution of GitHub Copilot to the Decline in Technical Hiring

This section details the counterfactual calculations reported in Section 6.5 [#S6.SS5] and Figure 10 [#S6.F10]. The sample consists of firms whose first scheduled post-launch contract review fell in 2023. We measure baseline pre-launch hiring Hi​0H_{i0} as firm ii’s mean quarterly technical hires over 2022 and post-period hiring Hi​1H_{i1} as mean quarterly technical hires over 2024, yielding an observed decline of Δ=16.1\Delta=16.1 hires per firm-quarter.

To compute ΔGitHub Copilot\Delta_{\text{GitHub Copilot}}, we evaluate forgone hiring firm-quarter by firm-quarter across event-time horizons ℓ=0,…,4\ell=0,\dots,4 relative to each firm’s scheduled contract review quarter (the event clock of Equation 4 [#S4.E4]) and take an average. In the review quarter itself (ℓ=0\ell=0) we set ρ^0=θ^0=0\widehat{\rho}_{0}=\widehat{\theta}_{0}=0, in the point estimates and in every bootstrap draw, because the first stage is small in that quarter and the Wald ratio is unstable there; the average runs over five quarters. Let Hi​ℓH_{i\ell} denote firm ii’s observed technical hires ℓ\ell quarters after its scheduled review and Di​ℓD_{i\ell} its adoption indicator in that quarter. The three benchmarks in Figure 10 [#S6.F10] are computed as:

(10)

In Equation (10 [#A6.E10]), the horizon-specific reduced-form coefficient ρ^ℓ\widehat{\rho}_{\ell} is applied to all firms. In Equation (11 [#A6.E11]), the horizon-specific Wald estimate θ^ℓ=ρ^ℓ/π^ℓ\hat{\theta}_{\ell}=\hat{\rho}_{\ell}/\hat{\pi}_{\ell} is applied to adopting firms and multiplied by the complier share of adopters at horizon ℓ\ell, π^ℓ/P^​(Di​ℓ=1)\widehat{\pi}_{\ell}/\widehat{P}(D_{i\ell}=1), where π^ℓ\widehat{\pi}_{\ell} is the first-stage effect and P^​(Di​ℓ=1)\widehat{P}(D_{i\ell}=1) is the sample adoption rate at horizon ℓ\ell. Because adopting firms are larger on average than non-adopters, reweighting the complier effect by adopter hiring levels yields a larger reduction than the unweighted reduced form (3.1 versus 1.7 hires per firm-quarter). In Equation (12 [#A6.E12]), the Wald estimate is applied to all adopting firms without scaling down by the complier share. This assumes that an always taker kk quarters after its own adoption experiences the same proportional hiring effect as a complier kk quarters after its review.
