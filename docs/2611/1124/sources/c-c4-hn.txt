I just had to come to terms with this. I launched a not for profit radio station in Canada (TripleC.fm) and wanted to take a stand that was reasonable, and open to future changes.
Here's the policy we came up with... https://triplec.fm/ai-policy [https://triplec.fm/ai-policy] The main thing it boils down to was "did a person make the musical decisions?", if so, it's fine...if AI was involved in the decision making, it's not.
The point that resonates the most with me from your comment is about synths. I called it out on the page because it's got a interesting history. The two events that jump out the most to me are from 1975 where Queen printed "No synthesizers!" on A Night at the Opera and 1982 when a branch of the UK Musicians' Union voted to ban synthesizers and drum machines from sessions and live work. The ban was later lifted (in 1997).
I'm not going to pretend to know exactly where this is going to end up, but it's certainly not a discussion that's over just yet. I personally suspect one day someone will publish some track that was mostly AI generated that is some kind of amazing drug for our ears and that'll spark a really interesting moment for debate. It hasn't happened yet, maybe it never will, but if it does it'll be an interesting time.

verelo [user?id=verelo] 19 hours ago [item?id=50635876] | root [#50630597] | parent [#50635790] | next [#50632461] [–] [javascript:void(0)]

Fair, it's not a clean line and I'm not debating that it is! I don't think it's really about right and wrong. It's where we've chosen to draw it for what we play, and we'll get some calls wrong, and we're going to have to be open to pushback when that happens.
AI melody with your own parts around it: no for us. The AI wrote and played that melody, and what you add around it doesn't change who made that part.
Your notation plus a reference sound: that's the genuinely grey one. If the tool plays exactly your notes with a sound you picked, it's basically a synth. If the model is deciding the phrasing and the sound itself, it's playing the part. We'd ask the artist what it actually did and judge that song on its own. Our submission process asks for details, and if we're still not sure based on what they tell us, we follow up with further questions.
Drum machine: totally fine. It plays exactly the pattern you program. Asking a model to "make me a beat" is the difference.
The test we keep coming back to in these examples mostly comes down to: if a session player had done that bit instead, would they be the one who got the writing / playing credits?

That's exactly the long-term problem with AI-produced works.
Record companies can use this to reproduce songs in their catalog that they've lost the rights to with just enough tweaks to avoid copyright/likeness legal troubles.
They can also use AI to create works from artists/bands discovered by A&R reps and completely cut off their ability to profit since the record companies own almost all of the popular distribution channels and many of the venues they can play at (through Ticketmaster/Live Nation).
All of this, I think, will culminate with artists becoming more aggressive about self-distribution and refusing to sign on with record labels at first then a reduction in new music over time as making a living doing music graduates from "difficult" to "impossible."

Almost everyone will say they're huge music fans, enjoying music is probably one of the most common shared human experiences. But in there are vastly different levels of how "into it" people really are.
Now obviously this is true for a lot of things, where there are casual fans and people who are more into it as a serious hobby. But for music there is a significant amount of people who will say that they're huge music fans that in reality have extremely shallow knowledge of the music scene, even of the artist that they're fans of. "I'm a huge country fan Chris Stapleton is my favorite singer" you ask what they thought of his latest album from the other year and get back a " Oh I don't know but Tennessee Whiskey is my favorite song." It's the people who used to mostly just put on top 40 radio, who now probably just have 5 or 6 spotify or youtube algorithm curated playlists saved that they listen on repeat.
Now, I don't mean to invalidate people's experience and I don't want to push some elitist takes on "being a real music fan". It's perfectly fine to listen to music without making it an active hobby.
But the last time I saw a discussion on AI music here some people were saying things like "Most music is just commercial junk made for profit anyway." And obviously that's just not true, it's a perception you get from having a limited exposure to the wider musical world. It's hard to respond to something like that without sounding like a condescending elitist.

Suno and other generators embed an audio watermark that is set as few bytes at random frequencies and so far been impossible to detect and remove. That mark carries exactly which generator made it. There's also AI generation artefacts that are used in detection to identify ai generated music in general. The artefacts are well known - high frequency noise and marks where the audio signal bands are stitched together during generation. 
And AI detectors give track a score, if only part of track is AI generated, then the score would be lower than 100%.
What is curious - how exactly does Tidal decide? Because there are AI generation plugins for audio production, there will be big studio produced tracks showing up some detection of AI in the mix. 
Tidal can't be denying a track if any AI is detected in it. It has to be close to 100% of track detected as AI gen to deny it.

rdiddly [user?id=rdiddly] 20 hours ago [item?id=50634744] | prev [#50631699] | next [#50630514] [–] [javascript:void(0)]

There's no way to detect AI reliably or enforce this rule fairly.
A better approach would be to lean harder into existing efforts to surface "good" music for the listener and let "bad" music sit in obscurity. If an AI slop track sucks ass, but has zero plays, then it has done no harm to any listeners as far as I can tell. It has used up storage space on the CDN servers and contributed to the company's costs though. How about placing tracks in deep storage after they go 6 months with zero plays. Then you "have" and "can offer" the song, but aren't paying for active storage and replication.
Actually maybe a slop track HAS in fact harmed the listeners just by being there, in the way, and having to be filtered out. Like spam. But "more is better" has always been a big part of any streaming service's value proposition, so what is the complaint really? Filtering and selection (curation) is a core part of it. You can't listen to everything.
In talking about harm I'm focusing on the listener by the way and ignoring the prior harm by exploiting energy, space, water and content, partly because that's already happened and partly because as soon as AI crafts a track that is good, everyone but the most stubborn virtue-signalers will toss all that shit right out the window and listen to it, and come up with the justifications after the fact.
Arguably an AI work represents, to some degree, the will of the person who prompted it, and is therefore a valid artwork. But if they have prompted it poorly, it will suck. Quality has to be the primary criterion, not authorship. Most people are already using quality as a proxy for authorship anyway - "If it sucks this bad, it must be AI." But humans make sucky stuff all the time. And humans working with AI have made some good stuff. So authorship is unknowable and possibly irrelevant. Is it good or is it bad, needs to become the primary question.
Really, it already is. The things we hate about AI writing, AI code, AI images, etc. mostly center around them sucking. "The hand has 7 fingers." That is a form of sucking, because real hands have 5 fingers and if you deviate from that, there should be an aesthetic or other meaningful reason that resonates with viewers. Even just using the word slop - you're either saying it's messy (a form of sucking) and was done carelessly, literally without care (a closely related form of sucking), or you're comparing it to something we feed to pigs, and don't eat ourselves, and why don't we eat it ourselves? Because it sucks. If pig slop were delicious, everybody would eat it and say "To hell with everyone who turns up their noses at pig slop! I do what's right for me!"

Boomers, Gen X, and millennials have a notion of music connecting people, but this hasn't broadly been true for long years now. The rise of portable listening devices made music a personal experience not necessarily shared with other people. The explosion in availability meant that your tastes might not overlap even with your closest friends'. The decline of live music in many cities (due to increasing rents, competition from the internet, etc.) means many people no longer get the experience of going out to hear music with other people.
Even as a millennial, due to my taste for music foreign to my home region I can't say music ever connected me to other people IRL. And now I can't even talk about music with other people on the internet, since the music forums that once existed declined with the rise of social media.
