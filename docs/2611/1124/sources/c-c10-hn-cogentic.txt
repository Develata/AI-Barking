Right now people only spend a medium amount of resources on factoring, because nobody knows if it's even possible to do it in a way that comprehensively breaks RSA. If it is possible and you publish proof, that changes fast and you'll have others who can do it very quickly, and no guarantee that it doesn't then become public knowledge in short order.
That's still probably the best way to do it, but it's not going to be fun. If you see someone publish a factorization of the challenge number then it's time to drop everything and immediately stop using RSA.
And then the question is whether the result generalizes to elliptic curves.

I don't think the recent spate of math discoveries should be very surprising. Consider 10 years ago when AlphaGo beat Lee Sedol, what was really happening there? You had an very large search space (all possible Go moves) that humans had been navigating by a combination of intuition, trial-and-error and passing knowledge between master-student. Then a program was build that could explore that search space both from existing searches (AlphaGo) and from scratch (AlphaGo Zero), and the program found some interesting results (winning moves no one had thought of before).
Today's LLMs are vastly bigger and they operate in an immensely larger search space (all of symbolic representation of the world) using immensely more power, and now they're finding results in that search space that people could not. In both cases, the space-search program needed a way to verify whether its results were valid - the Go board in AlphaGo's case, and Lean in current math work. So it really shouldn't be surprising that the space-searchers found something people either missed or was too big/far away to be accessible (that is, it is unknown whether the current RH proof can be simplified and/or understood like classical human proofs)

Even for investors who don't believe in recursive self-improvement, I think Anthropic's current valuation is unreasonably low.
They are currently valued at about US$1.1 trillion, and had US$17 billion of revenue last quarter, up from US$11.6 billion the quarter before, which amounts to doubling every 6 months (a slowdown; the previous quarter was up 145% from US$4.73 billion in revenue for Q1, which amounts to doubling every 4 months). They're committed to spending about US$70 billion over the next year. They're sitting on US$200 billion in cash, which is enough runway for about another year at their current burn rate, or two years if they had to strip bare because of big problems. The last debt they issued (US$60 billion) was at 10.5%, so they could probably borrow another year's worth if they needed to. That interest rate suggests that their creditors think they have about a 5% per year chance of going bankrupt. Their gross profit margin on the inference they sell is reportedly about 50%, so the only reason they're losing money now is that they're doing a lot of R&D.
This doesn't sound to me like the kind of situation where they are really at a major risk of bankruptcy in the next year. Indeed, it sounds to me like they're almost guaranteed to double their revenue in the next year, since currently they're doubling their revenue every 6 months, and their product is pretty sticky—once you've delegated your job or your company to Claude, you aren't getting it back. So any decline in their revenues would have to be pretty gradual.
If AI becomes "normal technology" and they cease their explosive growth, say a year from now, presumably they'll also cease their explosive R&D expenditures, so they'll be profitable if they can navigate the transition. The form of the integer factorization result is a bit surprising.
It's built on top of the general number field sieve (GNFS) which already gives a MUCH better asymptotic complexity than Google's algorithm. But GNFS relies on three unproven heuristics/conjectures.
Cogentic ended up not proving these conjectures, but replacing parts of GNFS that relied on them, paying a big complexity tax.
The result is still curious, but the bar for the AI proofs has been raised so high, that I'd expect it to successfully attack those conjectures directly.

GNFS would take 10^47 operations while this takes 10^2572 for a 4096 bit rsa key. The only improvement here is that this is guaranteed to work for arbitrary numbers.

My money would be no for normal RSA key sizes
> and will post quantum algorithms also help protect against these?
Elliptic curve cryptography already uses a different mechanism that isn’t susceptible to factorization improvements. Post quantum algorithms are similar but in using other mathematical building blocks but they’re really meant to fight a hypothetical quantum computer - without that elliptic curve and other classical methods are preferable as they’re smaller keys and much more efficient.

Summary of the new results versus previous established bounds:
1. Integer factorization (randomized / Las Vegas):
- New: `L_N[1/3, 105]`
- Previous: L_N[1/2, 1]
In practice the General Number Field Sieve was assumed to run in L_N[1/3, 1.923] time. But it's unproven.
Here L_N[a,c] = exp((c+o(1)) * (log N)^a * (log log N)^(1-a)).
2. Discrete logarithms:
- New: exp((2+o(1)) * (log n)^2 / log log log n)
- Previous: exp(O((log n)^2))
So it wins by logloglogn in the denominator.
3. Approximate Nash equilibrium
- New: polynomial time for epsilon = 1/5
- Previous: polynomial time for epsilon = 1/3 + delta
4. Deterministic integer factorization
- New: exp(O((log N)^(3/4)))
- Previous established bound: N^(1/5+o(1))
5. Log-rank conjecture (How many bits do you need to communicate to compute a boolean function f(x,y) \in {0,1}?)
- New: O(r^0.499995)
- Previous: O(r^0.5)
