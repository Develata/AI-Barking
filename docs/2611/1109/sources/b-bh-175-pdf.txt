                                    The Hawking–Page transition at weak coupling
                                                               Claude (Anthropic)


                                                                      Abstract
                                 In anti-de Sitter space a black hole forms through a first-order phase transition, discov-
                             ered by Hawking and Page. In N = 4 super Yang–Mills theory on S 3 this is deconfinement
                             at strong coupling. Does the transition remain first order at weak coupling, where there is
                             no classical geometry? The free theory has a transition at its Hagedorn temperature, and
                             it was suggested in 2003 that the two could be continuously connected, either by a single
                             first-order line or through a pair of continuous transitions at weak coupling, which have
                             no counterpart in classical gravity. One number decides: the sign of the quartic coefficient
                             b in the effective action of the Polyakov loop. It first appears at three loops and, to our

                             knowledge, has not been computed in the two decades since. We find b = −1.48 × 10−3 λ2 ,
                             where λ is the ’t Hooft coupling. The transition is first order. It lies slightly below the
                             Hagedorn temperature, and its three saddle points appear, exchange dominance and dis-
                             appear in the same order as thermal anti-de Sitter space and the small and large black
                             holes. The structure of the Hawking–Page transition is thus already present in perturba-
                             tion theory. The computation is Hamiltonian, and almost every number in it was obtained
                             in two independent ways. It reproduces the two-loop Hagedorn temperature known from
                             integrability and the exact results available in closed subsectors.

1     Introduction and summary
1.1   A transition with two descriptions
Anti-de Sitter space (AdS) acts as a box. Light rays reach its boundary and return in a finite
time, so that a gas of gravitons inside it comes to thermal equilibrium. Hawking and Page
studied this equilibrium in 1983 [1]. At low temperature the dominant configuration is a gas
of particles in thermal AdS, with a free energy of order one. There is a second solution of
Einstein’s equations with the same boundary conditions, a black hole at the centre of the√space.
In AdS5 with radius R, black holes exist only above a minimum temperature Tmin = 2/πR
[2]. Above it there are two. The small one has negative specific heat and is unstable. The
large one is stable, and at

its free energy drops below that of the gas. Above THP the black hole dominates. The free
energy changes from order one to order 1/GN , where GN is Newton’s constant, and the
transition is first order.
    Fifteen years later Witten interpreted the transition in gauge theory [2]. Through the
AdS/CFT correspondence [3, 4, 5], gravity in AdS5 × S 5 is N = 4 super Yang–Mills theory
with gauge group SU (N ) on the boundary S 3 × R, at large N and large ’t Hooft coupling
       2 N . Since 1/G           2

from O(1) to O(N 2 ). This is deconfinement. The order parameter is the Polyakov loop, the
holonomy of the gauge field around the thermal circle. It vanishes in thermal AdS, where the
thermal circle is not contractible, and it is non-zero in each black-hole saddle, where the circle
shrinks smoothly at the horizon. On S 3 its phase is averaged over, so that its expectation
value vanishes in both phases and it is its magnitude that distinguishes them [2, 6].
    A deconfinement transition in a conformal theory on a compact space may seem surprising.
There is no dynamical scale to set the temperature, and there are no asymptotic states to
confine. The origin of the transition is Gauss’s law, which on a compact space requires all
states to be gauge singlets. At large N that constraint alone is enough to produce a sharp
transition, and it does so even at zero coupling.

1.2   Historical background
The ideas that meet in this problem have three separate origins.
    The first is the physics of hadrons. In 1965 Hagedorn observed that the number of hadronic
resonances grows exponentially with their mass, and concluded that hadronic matter has a
highest temperature [7]. Ten years later Cabibbo and Parisi read the same growth as the
sign of a phase transition, above which quarks are liberated [8]. Polyakov and Susskind then
showed that a gauge theory does deconfine when it is heated, and that the order parameter
is the holonomy of the gauge field around the thermal circle [9, 10]. The potential for this
holonomy was computed at one loop [11, 12], and Svetitsky and Yaffe used its symmetry
under the centre of the gauge group to classify the possible transitions [13]. The limit of many
colours introduced by ’t Hooft [14] makes the distinction between the phases sharp. Thorn
pointed out that the free energy is of order one in the confined phase and of order N 2 in
the deconfined phase, and asked whether the confined phase has a highest temperature [15].
Skagerstam imposed the singlet constraint on free quarks and gluons by an integral over the
gauge group, which is the device used throughout this paper [16]. In the same years Gross and
Witten, and Wadia, found that integrals over a unitary matrix at large N have a third-order
transition at which the density of eigenvalues develops a gap [17, 18]. Lattice simulations later
established that the transition of pure SU (N ) gauge theory in infinite volume is first order for
N ≥ 3, with a latent heat of order N 2 [19, 20].

                                                3
     The second origin is the thermodynamics of black holes. Bekenstein and Hawking gave the
black hole an entropy and a temperature [21, 22]. Gibbons and Hawking showed how to obtain
both from a Euclidean path integral in which the black hole is a saddle point [23]. In flat space
the canonical ensemble does not exist, because the specific heat of a Schwarzschild black hole
is negative. Hawking and Page showed that a negative cosmological constant removes this
difficulty [1]. The third origin is string theory, which has a Hagedorn temperature of its own.
Sathiapalan, Kogan, and Atick and Witten traced it to a mode of the string that winds the
thermal circle and becomes massless there [24, 25, 26]. Atick and Witten argued further that
the interactions of this mode make the transition first order, and that the free energy above
it is of genus zero, which is the analogue of order N 2 . Susskind, and Horowitz and Polchinski,
proposed that a highly excited string and a black hole are one object in two regimes, and that
each turns into the other when the horizon is of string size [27, 28]. For type II strings a later
analysis of the self-gravitating string finds an obstruction to connecting it with the black hole
as a classical solution [29].
     The three strands were joined by the AdS/CFT correspondence [3, 4, 5, 30]. One sign
of the connection had appeared earlier. The entropy of the black three-brane is 3/4 of the
entropy of free N = 4 fields [31], and the first corrections at strong and at weak coupling move
the two values towards each other [32, 33]. The comparison was extended to the theory on a
sphere in [34]. Witten’s identification of the Hawking–Page transition with deconfinement on
S 3 [2] then raised the question of what the transition becomes at weak coupling. From the
leading α′ correction, Gao and Li and then Caldarelli and Klemm argued that it disappears
below a critical coupling [35, 36], whereas Sundborg found a similar transition at zero coupling
[37].

1.3   The same transition in the free theory
That Gauss’s law alone produces a transition was found by Hallin and Persson for quarks
in 1 + 1 dimensions [38], understood in general by Sundborg [37] and developed in detail by
Aharony, Marsano, Minwalla, Papadodimas and Van Raamsdonk [6], whom we call AMMPV.
Consider λ = 0. The singlet states on S 3 are traces of products of the elementary fields and
their derivatives. The number of single-trace states grows exponentially with energy, like the
states of a string, and so the theory has a Hagedorn temperature [7]. On a sphere of unit
radius it is

Below TH the free energy is of order one. Above it the free energy is of order N 2 . The
Polyakov loop jumps from zero to a finite value. All of this occurs in a theory of free fields,
and it resembles the transition at (1).
    Sundborg had noted that the free transition resembles a Hawking–Page transition in a
string theory of vanishing tension [37]. AMMPV proposed that the free transition and the
Hawking–Page transition could be the two ends of one line of transitions in the (λ, T ) plane.
At weak coupling the line is a Hagedorn transition of a string of vanishing tension. At strong
coupling it is the formation of a black hole.
    The free theory is, however, a degenerate starting point. At λ = 0 the effective potential
for the Polyakov loop is exactly flat at the transition, and the order parameter jumps because
nothing opposes it. Any interaction, however weak, lifts the degeneracy. Which way it is lifted
decides the character of the transition.

1.4         The question
Let U be the holonomy of the gauge field around the thermal circle and un = tr U n /N its
moments. At large N the partition function on S 3 × S 1 is an integral over the single unitary
matrix U , weighted by an effective action Seff [U ] obtained by integrating out everything else.
Near the transition only u1 is light, and after the other moments have been integrated out the
action takes the Landau form
                                       Seff
                                            = m21 (T, λ) |u1 |2 + b(λ) |u1 |4 + . . .                                       (3)
                                       N2
The mass m21 is positive at low temperature and vanishes at the Hagedorn temperature TH (λ).
The quartic coefficient b vanishes in the free theory and starts at order λ2 . AMMPV showed
that its sign decides between the two phase diagrams of figure 1.
    If b < 0, the potential (3) develops a second minimum away from the origin before m21
reaches zero. There is one first-order transition, slightly below TH . The Hagedorn temperature
survives as the temperature at which the confined phase, already metastable, becomes locally
unstable. This is the pattern of the Hawking–Page transition.
    If b > 0, the confined phase remains the only minimum until m21 changes sign. Then u1
grows continuously from zero. This is a second-order transition at TH . At a slightly higher
temperature the eigenvalue distribution of U develops a gap, which is a third-order transition
of the kind found by Gross and Witten and by Wadia [17, 18]. Classical gravity has no
counterpart of either. If b were positive, the phase diagram would in the simplest scenario
reorganise at intermediate coupling, through a tricritical point [6].
    The question is therefore the sign of b in N = 4 super Yang–Mills theory. This paper
determines it.

1.5         Why it has been open
AMMPV posed the question in 2003 and announced the computation [6]. They noted that b
is fixed by a set of two-loop and three-loop vacuum diagrams on S 3 × S 1 . In 2005 the same


                                                             5
authors carried the computation out for pure Yang–Mills theory [39]. Fourteen three-loop
diagrams, each a sum over spherical harmonics and all but one with a logarithmic divergence
of its own, combine with their counterterms to b = −5.7 × 10−4 λ2 in their conventions. The
transition of pure Yang–Mills theory on a small sphere is first order. The authors wrote that
they planned to do N = 4 next, and remarked that they had no independent way to verify
their number. A later two-loop calculation by three of them tested the formalism and the
regularisation against results on R4 , though not the three-loop number itself [40].
    The N = 4 computation did not appear. It is considerably larger. There are six scalars and
four fermions as well as the gauge field, with Yukawa couplings and quartic scalar couplings,
and in Coulomb gauge every one of them carries colour charge and feeds the instantaneous
Coulomb interaction. The number of diagrams, species assignments and colour orderings is
larger by more than a factor of ten.
    Other examples accumulated in the meantime. The plane-wave matrix model has a decon-
finement transition of the same kind [41, 42, 43], and it has b < 0 in perturbation theory [44],
although a lattice study at N = 8 and 12 finds that the transition appears to be continuous
at the weakest couplings [45]. Gauge theory in 0 + 1 dimensions on a circle with heavy adjoint
scalars also has b < 0 [46]. Pure Yang–Mills theory in 2+1 dimensions on a small S 2 , however,
was reported to have b > 0 [47]. AMMPV had already stressed that there is no sign theorem
[39]. In their pure-glue result the manifestly negative part of b is hundreds of times smaller
than the part that could have had either sign. The answer for N = 4 could therefore not be
anticipated.
    One further example lies within the N = 4 theory itself. The theory has supersymmetric
black holes in AdS5 , which preserve one sixteenth of the supersymmetry [48, 49, 50, 51].
Their entropy is reproduced by a supersymmetric partition function with complex chemical
potentials [52] and accounted for by the superconformal index [53, 54], which counts only
protected states and does not depend on the coupling [55, 56]. In the supersymmetric ensemble
these black holes compete with a gas of gravitons, and there is a Hawking–Page transition
between the two. The phases of the index at large N were studied in [57, 58]. The index
is again a matrix integral over the holonomy, but its effective action is complex. Because of
destructive interference the mode u1 does not condense at the point where it first becomes
tachyonic. Deconfinement is delayed, and it is of first order, already at zero coupling. The
deconfinement line found in this way agrees closely with the one found in gravity [59]. The
protected sector thus has a first-order transition at every coupling. This does not settle the
question for the thermal partition function. The generic states that dominate the canonical
ensemble do not contribute to the index, and the mechanism is a different one. For the thermal
partition function the effective action is real, and the order of the transition rests on the sign
of b.
    The matrix model for the holonomy was developed in several other directions. Its behaviour
close to the transition, where the corrections in 1/N become large, was analysed in [60, 61]. It
was used as an effective description of the black holes of AdS5 , including those with R-charge
and the small black hole as it approaches the string scale [61, 62, 63], and it was rewritten
in terms of free fermions [64]. Matter in the fundamental representation was included in [65],
related models were applied to deconfinement in QCD [66], and gauge theories on circles and
tori were treated in [46, 67]. The two-loop partition function on S 3 was obtained for pure Yang–
Mills theory in [40] and with general adjoint matter in [68]. The gauge-theory description of
the unstable saddle, which is the small black hole, has been pursued in [69, 70, 71, 72, 73, 74].
    Much more is known about the other coefficient in (3). The shift of m21 , and with it the
Hagedorn temperature, was found at one loop by Spradlin and Volovich [75],

1.7   What is not shown
We do not show that the first-order line continues unbroken to strong coupling. We show only
that it begins as a first-order line at weak coupling. We work at leading order in 1/N and to
order λ2 . The free partition function at finite N is analysed in [88]. One finite counterterm
needed for the two-loop Hagedorn check, which does not enter b, is fixed by fifteen exact
conditions but has not been derived analytically. The computation was carried out by an AI
system. Appendix C describes how it was organised.


2     The free theory
We begin with the theory at λ = 0. The results of this section are due to Sundborg [37] and
AMMPV [6]; AMMPV trace a special case to Hallin and Persson [38], and the counting of
single-trace words is also in Polyakov [89]. A short review is [90]. We review the free theory
because every later section perturbs around it, and because the mechanism can be followed
completely.
    We put the theory on a three-sphere and set the radius to one. Energies are then measured
in units of 1/R. We write β = 1/T for the inverse temperature and

2.2     Gauss’s law and the matrix model

3     The order of the transition
We now switch on a small coupling and ask what becomes of the flat potential. The analysis
is that of AMMPV [6]. We give more detail on the three saddle points than is usual, because
the comparison with gravity rests on them.

3.1   The general form of the effective action
At λ ̸= 0 one can still integrate out all the non-zero modes of all the fields on S 3 × S 1 in
the presence of a constant holonomy U . They are massive, with masses of order 1/R, and
perturbation theory in λ is well defined. The result is an effective action Seff [U ] which is no
longer quadratic in the moments un . Two properties restrict its form.
    The first is the centre symmetry. The action is invariant under U → e2πik/N U , which
at large N becomes un → einα un for any phase α. Each term must carry total charge zero.
The allowed terms of low order are |u1 |2 , |u2 |2 , u21 u−2 with its conjugate, and |u1 |4 , where
u−n = u∗n .
    The second is large-N counting. A vacuum diagram with ℓ loops of momentum has at
most ℓ + 1 index loops, and each index loop gives one trace of a power of U . At order λℓ−1
one therefore finds products of up to ℓ + 1 traces. The quadratic terms are corrected already
at one loop in λ, that is by two-loop diagrams. The cubic term u21 u−2 needs three traces and
also first appears at order λ. The quartic term needs four traces and first appears at order λ2 ,
in three-loop diagrams.

3.4   Positive b: two continuous transitions
For b > 0 the potential m21 u2 +bu4 has its only minimum at u = 0 until m21 changes sign. Above
TH the minimum is at u2 = −m21 /2b and grows continuously. The free energy is −N 2 m41 /4b,
quadratic in T − TH , and its second derivative jumps. That is a second-order transition with
mean-field exponents.
   The minimum reaches u = 12 at m21 = −b/2. There the density develops its gap and the
potential changes from one branch of (19) to the other, with a jump in its third derivative.
This is a third-order transition of the Gross–Witten–Wadia kind [17, 18], at a temperature
above TH by a relative amount b/3β0 .
   Neither transition has a counterpart in classical gravity. If b were positive at weak coupling,
the two lines would have to meet and turn into a first-order line somewhere at intermediate
coupling, as on the right of figure 1.
   The task is thus defined. We need m21 to the order at which TH is shifted, the cubic
coupling κ at order λ, and b̃ at order λ2 . The first two are one-loop quantities and we review
them next. The third is the new computation.


4     One loop: the Hagedorn temperature
The first effect of the interaction is to move the Hagedorn temperature. This was computed
by Spradlin and Volovich [75]. They replaced thermal diagrams by the trace of the dilatation
operator over gauge-invariant operators, below TH . We use the same operator at fixed holon-
omy, where their counting of necklaces gives way to Wick contractions in a twisted ensemble,
so we set this out in some detail. We then record what integrability has since added.

5.3          Four kinds of contribution
Figure 3 shows what the terms are. In each, four index loops carry the holonomies U , U , U −1
and U −1 , and the question is what sits between them.
   The square of the one-loop operator. The term Q4 is the connected average of two copies of
H. Each copy acts on two letters. For the average to be connected and to produce four index
loops, the two copies must share at least one letter, and the colour structures of (33) must be
contracted in a non-planar way. It can be computed from the one-loop operator alone, and it
needs no regularisation.
   Exchange of u2 . The cubic coupling c3 is a one-loop quantity and the exchange is a tree
diagram of the matrix model. It is the analogue, in this problem, of a contribution that Atick
and Witten identified for strings near their Hagedorn temperature [26]. There the winding
mode that becomes massless couples to the dilaton, and integrating out the dilaton gives the
winding mode a negative quartic self-coupling. That is why they argued the string Hagedorn



                                                                                      18
transition is first order. Here u1 is the winding mode and u2 plays the part of the dilaton. It
turns out to be a small part of the answer.
    The two-loop operator, tree part. The operator D4 contains a piece that acts on three letters
at once and turns them into three others. It comes from tree-level scattering at order g 4 , for
instance two cubic vertices and a quartic one. When it acts across two traces it contributes
                            (a)
to q4 . We call this part q4 . It has no loop and is finite.
    The two-loop operator, loop part. The operator D4 also contains a piece that acts
on two letters and returns them to themselves, through a one-loop diagram. The planar
part of it renormalises m21 . The part that contributes to q4 is of the double-trace form
:tr(WA W̌ A ) tr(WB W̌ B ):, in which each letter is returned to its own trace while the loop
                                        (b)
connects the two. We call this part q4 . Each diagram in it is logarithmically divergent.
    There is a third part of D4 , which acts on a single letter through a two-loop self-energy. It
produces only two index loops and contributes to m21 , not to b. It will appear only in section 8.

5.4   What is known and what is not
Two of the three traces of the one-loop operator that enter c3 are in the work of Spradlin
and Volovich, and the corresponding coefficient for general adjoint matter is given as a sum
over angular momenta in [68]. The function Q4 has not appeared before, but it is a finite
computation with a known operator.
     The function q4 requires the two-loop dilatation operator at the first non-planar order on
all letters. It is known for the two holomorphic scalars of the su(2) sector [84]. The planar part
is known on the larger su(2|3) sector [98], for the pure scalar piece of the so(6) sector, which
is not closed at this order [99], and for twist-two operators through the anomalous dimensions
of [100, 101]. For the non-compact su(1, 1|2) sector Zwiebel found the two-loop generator and
conjectured that its lift to finite N holds for any gauge group [85]. None of this covers what
we need. At the Hagedorn temperature all letters contribute. The scalars without derivatives,
which make up the well-studied sectors, carry less than half of the weight in z(xH ) = 1. We
therefore construct the relevant parts of D4 from the Hamiltonian.

5.5   A freedom of scheme
One subtlety must be addressed before numbers are quoted.
    The effective action Seff [U ] is not unique. Only its integral over U is physical. Consider
adding to the dilatation operator a term proportional to the generator of gauge transforma-
tions, G = A [WA , W̌ A ]. Such a term vanishes on every gauge-invariant state and does not
            P

change the spectrum. The ensemble ⟨ · ⟩U at fixed U contains non-singlet states, however,
and there the term does not vanish. One finds that it changes Z[U ] by a total derivative
on the group manifold. In terms of the moments it is a change of variables, of the form
u2 → u2 + g 2 γ u21 and u1 → u1 + g 2 γ ′ u2 u−1 , with γ and γ ′ functions of the temperature.
    This matters because two natural forms of the one-loop operator differ by exactly such
a term. Beisert’s form (33) has all of its content in a two-letter kernel. The operator that
one obtains literally from second-order perturbation theory of the Hamiltonian has a different
two-letter kernel, with no diagonal part, together with a one-letter self-energy. On singlets the
two agree. At fixed U they give different functions c3 (x) and Q4 (x). We call the two choices
the commutator scheme and the literal scheme.
    A change of variables cannot change the order of a phase transition. Indeed the redefinitions
above shift b̃ and κ2 /m22 in (18) by equal and opposite amounts at the critical point, and shift
q4 (x) by an amount proportional to 1 − z(x), which vanishes there. Hence I and q4 (xH ) are

6     The computation

6.6   Independent evaluations
An error in one vertex coefficient among several hundred would be invisible in the final number.
The computation was therefore organised so that, with the one exception recorded at the end
of this subsection, no number is used until two independent routes agree on it.
    There are two constructions of the vertices. One follows the harmonic expansion of [102]
and expresses the integrals over S 3 through 9j symbols. The other realises the harmonics as
homogeneous harmonic polynomials on R4 and integrates them directly. They share no code.
They agree on every cubic, quartic and Coulomb coefficient, and they give the same one-loop
series c3 , d1 and Q4 to y 14 .
    There are three contraction engines for piece (a) and two for piece (b). One works with
reduced matrix elements and recoupling coefficients, one with explicit magnetic quantum num-
bers, and one at the level of words of matrices with mechanical normal ordering. The three
agree on piece (a) coefficient by coefficient from y 6 to y 12 , and topology by topology to y 10 .
    There is a separate route that does not use the effective Hamiltonian at all. It evaluates
three-loop vacuum diagrams on S 3 × S 1 in the manner of AMMPV, with the time integrals
done by divided differences and the contributions classified by the number of lines that wind.
It shares the vertex coefficients with the first construction and nothing else. It reproduces piece
(a) exactly to y 10 , separately for each vertex content and each power of β, and it reproduces
the first two coefficients of piece (b).
    Finally there is an analytic test. If the thermal lines are restricted to the two scalars of the
su(2) sector, with fugacities xZ and xϕ and unrestricted virtual states, the known two-loop
operator of [84] predicts both pieces. Piece (a) must be the polynomial −(xZ − xϕ )2 (x2Z + x2ϕ −
3xZ − 3xϕ ), and on the critical line xZ + xϕ = 1 piece (b) must be −2(x2Z + xZ xϕ + x2ϕ ). Three
engines reproduce the first exactly. For the coefficient −2 in the second we find −2.0000000
with one engine and −2.00(1) with the other.
    One caveat must be recorded. The coefficients of piece (b) from y 7 onwards were produced
by one engine only. The second engine confirms y 4 , y 5 and the two largest entries of y 6 . The
unconfirmed terms contribute −0.34 of the −1.50. The margin for the sign of b is fourteen in
these units.

9    The mode cut-off and its counterterms
The only regulator that is natural for a Hamiltonian on S 3 is a cut-off on the angular mo-
mentum of the modes. It is not a good regulator in the usual sense. This section describes

                                                 28
what it breaks, the three finite counterterms that repair it, and why the quartic coefficient b
is not affected by any of them. The first counterterm was found by AMMPV. The second is a
two-loop relative of theirs. The third has precedents as an operator: a renormalisation of the
A0 propagator, with a finite part, is among the quadratic counterterms of AMMPV [39], and
the term is listed for N = 4 in [102]. We have not seen its finite coefficient in N = 4, or its
effect on the spectrum, elsewhere.

9.1   What the cut-off preserves
The theory on R × S 3 is invariant under the superconformal group. A cut-off J on the angular
momentum of every mode preserves the rotations SO(4) of the sphere, the R-symmetry SU (4)
and time translations. It preserves nothing else. The conformal boosts mix harmonics of
neighbouring angular momentum and do not commute with the projection onto modes below
the cut-off. Neither do the supercharges, once the interaction is switched on. If the cut-off is
placed on the left spin jL only, as is convenient, parity is lost too.
    The general principle is then that every local operator of dimension four or less that is
allowed by the surviving symmetries will be induced, with a coefficient that is a power of J
fixed by the dimension of the operator, and a finite part that no surviving symmetry fixes. In
a Lorentz-invariant regularisation most of these operators are forbidden. Here they must be
found and removed by hand, by imposing the broken symmetries as renormalisation conditions.
AMMPV met this at one loop [39], with a quadratically divergent gluon mass and different
wave-function renormalisations for the time and space components. Aharony, Marsano and
Van Raamsdonk added a curvature-dependent gluon mass counterterm [40], and Mussel and
Yacoby, who follow them with matter included [68], a scalar mass whose finite part is fixed by
conformal invariance.

9.2   The double-trace quartic term
The first counterterm is the one of section 6. It is a quartic, double-trace, dimension-four
operator without derivatives, such as (trAi Aj )2 or tr(Ai Ai )tr(Xa Xa ), where Xa are the scalars.
Its divergent part cancels between diagrams and its finite part is proportional to nB − nF .
                                        1        1
For pure glue we find the coefficients 60 and 60   of AMMPV. For N = 4 it vanishes, mode by
mode.
    This is the only one of the three counterterms that has the colour structure to contribute
to b. It is absent. The argument for its absence assumes, however, that the cut-off is applied
uniformly. If one cuts off different diagrams on different lines, or cuts off the total energy of
the intermediate state, the diagrams see effectively different values of J and the cancellation
of logarithms leaves a finite piece. We apply the cut-off to the angular momentum of one
specified line of the loop, the same in every diagram.

9.5   Why b is not affected
None of this affects the quartic coefficient, for two reasons.
    The first is topological. The mass term and the Coulomb term are single-trace operators
with two and four fields, and they are of order g 4 . At the order we work they can only be
inserted once, at tree level. The resulting diagrams have the topology of a one-loop or two-loop
vacuum diagram, with at most three index loops. The coefficient of |u1 |4 needs four. This
is the argument of AMMPV [39]. These counterterms contribute to m21 , and to the cubic
coupling at the next order, but they cannot contribute to b at order λ2 .
    The second is that the quantities that do enter b are insensitive to how the cut-off is
labelled, which is the practical symptom of a missing counterterm. Piece (a) has no loop.
In piece (b) the double-trace kernel converges absolutely once the species are summed. As
an illustration, the entry of the kernel between the lowest scalar and the first excited scalar
multiplet comes out as −606.0000, −605.9998 and −606.0002 for three different choices of
the line that carries the cut-off. The exact value is −606. The planar kernels that enter d4
are different. Some of them converge only conditionally, and there the choice of line matters
at the level of 49 to 43 in individual entries. The exact two-loop energies select one choice
unambiguously.
    The resulting picture is coherent. The quantity we set out to compute is the part of
the three-loop free energy that is cleanest: double-trace, protected from counterterms by
nB = nF , and absolutely convergent. The quantity we used to test the machinery, the two-
loop Hagedorn temperature, is the part that is most sensitive to this regularisation. That it
nevertheless comes out right, once the broken symmetries are restored, is the stronger test.

10     Discussion
10.1    What has been shown
The quartic coefficient of the Polyakov-loop effective action of N = 4 super Yang–Mills theory
on S 3 is negative at order λ2 . It follows that at small λ and large N the theory has a single
first-order deconfinement transition, at a temperature Tc below the Hagedorn temperature by
a relative amount 9.4 × 10−5 λ2 . In a window of temperatures around Tc there are three saddle
points: a confined one with free energy of order one, a deconfined one with free energy of order
N 2 and a gapped eigenvalue distribution, and an unstable one between them.
     This is the structure of the gravitational problem. There the three saddles are thermal anti-
de Sitter space, the large√ black hole and the small black hole, and they exist together above
a temperature Tmin = 2/πR, with the first two exchanging dominance at THP = 3/2πR.
The identification of the saddles across the two regimes was proposed in [6] and sharpened
in [61, 62, 63], and the present result adds only that it is consistent at weak coupling. The
simplest phase diagram in the plane of λ and T , a single line of first-order transitions from
λ = 0 to λ = ∞, is allowed. The alternative, in which the line splits into two at some finite
coupling, was the only possibility had b been positive. It is now the less economical of the
two.
     We cannot say more than allowed. Whether the line in fact continues unbroken through
intermediate coupling is not something that a weak-coupling calculation can test.

10.2    The unstable saddle and the Hagedorn temperature
                                                                                   √
The unstable saddle merits a comment. At weak coupling it sits at |u1 | = us ∝ TH − T , it
merges with the confined saddle at TH , and it carries the information that the confined phase,
although locally stable all the way to TH , is only metastable above Tc . This is the scenario
that Atick and Witten proposed for string theory in flat space [26]: the Hagedorn temperature
is never reached in equilibrium, because a first-order transition driven by the winding mode
intervenes. They argued for it from the sign of a quartic coupling of the winding mode, induced
by its coupling to the dilaton and the graviton. The argument here has the same structure.
The winding mode is u1 , and the exchange of u2 , which makes a negative contribution to b
whatever its coupling, plays the part of the massless exchange.
     On the gravity side, a small black hole in anti-de Sitter space becomes as small as the
string length as its temperature approaches the Hagedorn temperature of the string, and
it is expected to turn into a highly excited string [28, 63, 29, 113]. At weak coupling the
corresponding statement is that the unstable saddle merges with the confined phase at TH .
The three temperatures (67) thus have direct counterparts. The interpolation of TH √      (λ) is
                                                                                    1/4
known from integrability [81, 82, 83]: it rises from 0.380 at λ = 0 and grows like λ /(2 2 π)
at large λ, where it is the Hagedorn temperature of the string in a nearly flat region. The
first correction to this behaviour has been derived on the string side by Urbach, who records
that Maldacena found it independently (unpublished) [113], and it agrees with the numerical
integrability result. Formulas for the next orders have been conjectured or proposed, partly
with that numerical input, and they agree with it as well [114, 115, 116]. The transition
temperature stays of order one, reaching 3/2π = 0.477. At weak coupling the two are separated
by a relative 10−4 λ2 , and at strong coupling by a factor that grows like λ1/4 . It would be
interesting to follow Tc (λ), but no analytic method is known. A Monte Carlo route through
the plane-wave matrix model has been proposed [117]. The free energy of the deconfined phase
is not a spectral problem of the planar dilatation operator.

10.3    Open problems
Several extensions are within reach of the same methods.
    Derive the 43 . The finite renormalisation (75) should follow from a one-loop computation
of the Coulomb propagator and the fermion kinetic term with a mode cut-off. We expect it
to be the statement that the cut-off renormalises the speed of light.
    Resolve the pure Yang–Mills class. This needs a comparison with the expressions and
integrals that [39] give for each diagram, or a third calculation. A Lagrangian calculation of
diagrams 3c, 3f and 3n alone would do.
    Chemical potentials. With chemical potentials for the R-charges and the spins, b becomes
a function on a five-dimensional space, which interpolates between (63) and the entries of
table 3. The codes take the chemical potentials as input. The phase diagram with R-symmetry
chemical potentials was mapped at zero coupling, and at weak coupling and high temperature,
in [118], and near the critical chemical potentials in [119]. The Hagedorn temperature with
chemical potentials has been studied with integrability [83, 120]. The gravitational side has
charged and rotating black holes with a phase structure of their own, and a comparison at the
level of the order of the transition should be possible.
    Other theories. For a gauge theory on S 3 with adjoint matter, the first term in (48) is a
function of the letter content and the one-loop operator only, and the second is not. The one
example known to us of a weakly coupled large-N gauge theory with a single-trace Lagrangian
on a sphere of two or more dimensions that was reported to have b > 0 is pure Yang–Mills
theory on a small S 2 [47]. When scalar fields with arbitrary couplings are added, b can be
given either sign, in more than two space-time dimensions by a double-trace term [39]. It
would be useful to know whether a single-trace example exists on S 3 , and what decides the
sign. The Yang–Mills coupling to matter seems to favour b < 0: the plane-wave matrix model
in perturbation theory [44], the massive matrix quantum mechanics of [46], pure glue on S 3
and now N = 4 all have it.
    The next order. The λ3 term of b needs the three-loop dilatation operator off shell, and
four-loop vacuum diagrams. No qualitative question that we know of needs it.

10.4    A closing remark
Witten’s identification of the Hawking–Page transition with deconfinement is one of the oldest
entries in the AdS/CFT dictionary. It has been tested at strong coupling and at zero coupling.
At zero coupling it holds in a weak sense only, since the free transition is of a degenerate kind
that fits no gravitational picture. The computation reported here removes the degeneracy.
The first effect of the interaction is to make the transition into what it is at strong coupling:
first order, with a small unstable black hole in between. This outcome was not guaranteed.
The one-loop operator alone makes it plausible, and the two-loop operator, which might have
opposed it, as it does in pure Yang–Mills theory, turns out to help.

C     How the computation was done
This appendix records how the work was organised, since this bears on how the result should
be judged.

Organisation
The work was divided among many instances of the same AI system, several at a time, each
with a narrow brief and no memory of the others except through files. One instance coordi-
nated. It wrote the briefs, kept a design document with the conventions and the current state
of each quantity, and decided what to believe.
    Three rules were imposed from the start.
    The first was that no number enters the result unless two independently written programs
agree on it, or it has been checked against an exact result. It was kept with one exception,
recorded in section 6.6: the coefficients of piece (b) from y 7 onwards come from a single


                                               35
engine. The independence extended to the method. For the vertex coefficients, for instance,
one construction used the 9j symbols of [102], and another used explicit harmonic polynomials
in R4 restricted to the sphere. Three contraction engines were written on top of them.
    The second was that every program must pass a gate before it is used in production. A
gate is a case with a known answer, run by the same code with no change but the input. The
gates were the su(2) sector, the matrix models with N = 2 and 3, the two-loop results of
AMMPV, the supertrace identities and, later, the exact two-loop energies.
    The third was that a disagreement is never averaged or argued away. It is pursued until
one side is shown to be wrong. The Coulomb counterterm of section 9 was found this way.
Two engines agreed with each other and disagreed with the Bethe ansatz, and it took the
dispersion test to show that both were faithfully computing the wrong theory.
