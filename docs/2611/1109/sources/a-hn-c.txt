There's a lot of research along this particular line, if you're interested!
This is a dataset basically constructed by taking a bunch of DeepSeek R1 trajectories and filtering out the incredibly long rambling ones: https://huggingface.co/datasets/open-thoughts/OpenThoughts-1... [https://huggingface.co/datasets/open-thoughts/OpenThoughts-114k]

In general, a lot of people in industry are finding cost savings these days by tuning a smaller model over longer agent trajectories (not exactly the same as distilling the reasoning traces, since those aren't exposed by many frontier labs anymore) collected from their production agents. Typically works best when you have a pretty workflow-y agent whose tasks all share a similar shape.

Caleb is right.
Also it seems the results are not straightforward.
Let say you have a model that score 100 in a benchmark and you distill it into a model half the size. It has very few guarantees that it will score close to 100 again.1
But I assume one of the hard problems is to know that the model might not perform well enough outside of the distillation part.
There is also another caveat that models do display some level of sub language control, by this I mean that innocuous text might contain information relevant for the own agent or for other agent with it shares tasks.
(Like when your partner says there is nothing wrong)
Because distillation uses very few resources, we don't know exactly what gets send forward or not.2
As Caleb said, many companies found that they can tune small open weight agents over procedural known tasks because that's easy to inspect and check and because there is little space dor ai-loitering, it tends to be less problematic.
The key difference, at least in my understanding, is that you are not concerned with the weights or reducing data. You are basically tuning agents existing weights to emulate another agent answer.
1 - The process of distillation is super effective for specific domains though.
2 - I know I will be called out for supposed AI mystery machine. What I mean is that Large models rely on latent reasoning and nuanced context, often lost during condensation. Distilling models to use fewer tokens or parameters makes predicting retained details difficult.
Some papers suggest that model preferences introduced during training, whether intentionally or inadvertently, may be inherited by distilled and fine-tuned models.

I used to work on evals/observability at Opik: https://github.com/comet-ml/opik [https://github.com/comet-ml/opik] And I can say from working with lots of users running agents at scale, there is definitely a pretty commonly reached frontier of difficulty/complexity/abstraction where the capability difference between something like Fable/Astra and smaller models is a big deal. You can still often tackle the hardest tasks with something like Opus, it just typically involves more direct intervention and design work from the user.
But again, this is just anecdata on my part. All of this is still pretty ambiguous and lots of companies are trying to solve the problem of matching models to task difficulty (I.e. routers), with varying degrees of success.

You don't have to use benchmarks at all, especially when models are so hard to quantify anyway. Simply shrugging and saying "well, no way around that" is not a solution. In addition, there are other, better ways to prevent benchmaxxing than simply "not publishing your solution". The recent maxxbench benchmark has shown one example.
Fourth, you don't need to provide a better alternative to criticize something. Criticism is valid even if you don't have a better alternative yourself
And lastly, I actually feel like there IS a better alternative than AA - not using aggregate score at all. If you need a model for coding, look at the individual benchmarks themselves for a / very / rough overview of how a models is doing. Trying to boil down so many different areas and capabilities into a single score, which is then weighted by little more than subjective vibes from the people behind AA is bound to be too little information and too much information loss. This, I personally feel, seems to mostly exist as a meaningless leaderboard for marketing purposes.
