                                                    Abstract

        This report studies how freelance work is changing in the AI era. We compare the activities
        people undertake in ChatGPT with the work clients request on Upwork and examine
        how clients combine tasks into projects. AI-related postings request slightly more tasks on
        average than other postings. Even among projects with similar numbers of tasks, AI-related
        postings bring together tasks that clients less often requested together before the generative
        AI era.


        Key findings
        AI use and freelance demand have different activity mixes. ChatGPT activities skew toward
        writing and advice, while Upwork postings lean toward technical implementation and visual
        production. This pattern is consistent with businesses continuing to hire freelancers for work that
        requires implementation, production, and delivery.
        The relationship between direct AI use and changes in freelance demand is not uniform: some
        activities gaining share on Upwork are common in ChatGPT, while others are not. There is not a
        simple substitution pattern between Upwork tasks and those common on ChatGPT. This contrast
        suggests a continuing role for workers who provide technical implementation, creative production,
        and finished deliverables.
        AI-related work is becoming more diverse. Work within AI-related postings is becoming less
        concentrated in a few activities. Among postings with at least two tasks, the average share of
        historically uncommon task pairs is higher for AI-related work. AI is changing both the breadth
        and mix of work that clients seek to hire.
        AI-related postings carry larger project budgets. Posted fixed-price budgets are approximately 52
        percent higher in AI-related postings than for non-AI work after accounting for work subcategory
        and posting month. The value of AI-related freelance work may lie partly in delivering broader
        projects that combine many capabilities.

1     Introduction
1.1   How AI is changing work
Public debate about AI and work often centers on a single question: how many jobs will AI replace? Yet,
technological change can reshape tasks and workflows well before occupations disappear or new jobs
enter official statistics. Understanding AI’s labor-market effects holistically requires examining how the
content and organization of work are changing today.
    The first report in OpenAI Economic Research’s “Work at the Frontier” series provides evidence
of one way users are starting to broaden their work activity. It finds that 43.5 percent of work-related
ChatGPT messages assigned to an occupation-specific activity involve tasks traditionally associated with
an occupation other than the user’s own. This “task crossover” suggests that AI is expanding the range
of activities people attempt beyond established occupational boundaries. But ChatGPT data alone do
not show how demand for freelance work is changing, or how clients are combining different activities
to form projects.
    This is important to understand, as technical capability does not on its own determine labor-market
outcomes. When AI can perform part of a job, employers and firms may respond in different ways. Some
activities may be performed directly with AI rather than purchased from another person. In other cases,
lower costs may expand demand, or complementary work may become more valuable—for example,
integrating AI into systems, evaluating AI outputs, and managing complex projects.
    Upwork Marketplace is a global platform that connects businesses with on-demand access to highly
skilled independent talent. The platform data provide a demand-side view of this adjustment. Job
postings show which activities clients seek, how those activities are bundled into projects, and the
budgets or rates clients attach to them. Freelance markets may reveal these changes relatively quickly
because clients can readily post emerging work, purchase one component of a larger role, or seek
combinations of tasks that previously belonged to different specialists.
    This is represented in prior Upwork research, which suggests that AI is reorganizing demand toward
new forms of work, with discrete AI-execution tasks becoming more common but less lucrative, while
demand is growing for more complex AI-augmented services, including work that involves supervising,
reviewing, and correcting AI-generated outputs.
    Upwork provides a useful setting for comparison with ChatGPT because its projects involve work
that is already remote and digitally mediated. This makes it possible to compare activities people
undertake directly with AI with digitally delivered work that clients seek from freelancers. The datasets
are not directly matched, and neither represents the labor market as a whole. We therefore interpret their
similarities and differences as descriptive evidence about the organization of work, with limits on what
they can establish about substitution between direct AI use and paid freelance services.
    This report combines evidence from ChatGPT use and Upwork job postings to examine two dimen-
sions of change. First, we compare the activities people undertake directly with ChatGPT with the
work clients purchase from freelancers, and examine how the mix and stated value of Upwork demand
are changing. Second, we study how activities are organized within job postings—whether AI-related
projects contain broader task bundles and whether they combine tasks in ways that had less precedent
before the generative-AI era.
    We find both similarities and differences in the activities people undertake with ChatGPT and those
clients seek from freelancers. Within AI-related Upwork Marketplace postings, demand is spreading
across a broader range of activities and shifting toward integration (e.g., integrating AI APIs into
applications), multimodal production (e.g., creating AI-generated video and producing motion graphics),

and applied business workflows (e.g., marketing automation and social-media campaign management).

not only which activities people undertake, but also how clients assemble work into projects.

1.2   Related research and contribution
A longstanding literature argues that the labor-market effects of technological change are best understood
by examining tasks rather than occupations. One common idea in this literature is that technology can
substitute for workers in some activities while increasing the value of complementary human inputs,
and the creation of new tasks can offset some of the resulting displacement (Autor, 2015; Acemoglu and
Restrepo, 2019; Autor et al., 2024). This perspective suggests that the effects of generative AI may first
appear as changes in what workers do and how activities are organized.
   Research on generative AI documents both broad technical exposure and uneven effects in practice.
Eloundou et al. (2024) estimate that large language models could affect tasks across a wide range of
occupations. Experimental and workplace studies find that generative AI can improve productivity
and output quality in professional writing and customer support, with especially large gains for less
experienced customer-support workers (Noy and Zhang, 2023; Brynjolfsson, Li, and Raymond, 2025).
At the same time, the benefits depend on the task: Dell’Acqua et al. (2026) find that AI improves
performance on tasks within its capabilities but can reduce accuracy on tasks outside this “jagged
technological frontier.”
   Recent theory emphasizes that jobs are endogenous bundles of tasks rather than fixed occupational
containers, with boundaries set by the tradeoff between specialization and coordination costs (Rosen,
1978; Becker and Murphy, 1992; Ocampo, 2022). Because AI can change the costs of performing tasks,
coordinating across them, and transferring context between them, it can split and rebundle jobs even
when no task is fully automated (Demirer et al., 2026; Gans, 2026), with effects that depend on how
easily tasks can be separated from the jobs they sit in (Garicano, Li, and Wu, 2026). To the best of our
knowledge, direct empirical evidence on how AI is reshaping task bundles within real projects remains
limited.
   Evidence from online labor markets points to both demand-side disruptions and worker adaptation.
Hui, Reshef, and Zhou (2024) find declines in employment and earnings among freelancers in categories
more exposed to generative AI. Yiu et al. (2026) show that more-exposed freelancers also respond
by shifting away from their previous domains and directing their bids toward higher-value contracts,
although domain-specific human capital can constrain this repositioning. This report complements that
work by focusing on the organization of the work being purchased, using job-posting data from an
online labor marketplace.
   Additionally, technological change can reorganize work not only by creating or eliminating individual
tasks, but also by changing the boundaries that determine which tasks people do together. Which
tasks are bundled into a job reflects, in part, a classic tradeoff in the organization of work. Rosen (1978)
emphasizes how substitution possibilities across different kinds of labor shape the division of labor,
while Becker and Murphy (1992) show that greater specialization must be balanced against the costs of
coordinating complementary activities across workers. Job boundaries therefore reflect both the gains
from dividing work more finely and the costs of separating activities that must ultimately be combined



                                                     3
in production. AI can shift this tradeoff by changing the relative costs of performing particular tasks,
coordinating across them, and transferring context from one task or worker to another.
   Recent research further examines how AI can change the way tasks are grouped into jobs. Demirer
et al. (2026) model firms as choosing how production steps are grouped into tasks and jobs, as AI
changes which sequences of work can be performed together. Gans (2026) shows that AI can induce
task rebundling even without fully automating an activity by changing the costs of transferring context
across task boundaries. Garicano, Li, and Wu (2026) similarly show that the effects of AI depend on
how easily tasks can be separated from the jobs in which they are embedded. When tasks form a weak
bundle, AI can pull one component out and narrow the remaining human job, whereas stronger task
complementarities can keep the existing bundle intact.
   This idea also builds on a broader task-based theory of occupational boundaries. In Ocampo (2022),
workers have discrete skill types while tasks vary continuously in their skill requirements, and occu-
pations emerge endogenously as bundles of tasks assigned to workers. Technological change alters
comparative advantage and task prices, which can change the optimal assignment of tasks across work-
ers. Existing bundles can therefore break apart and tasks can be reassigned and regrouped, redrawing
the boundaries of occupations. Another aim of this report is to understand how work is rebundling and
reorganizing in response to AI.


2     AI use and freelance hiring point to a changing composition of work
2.1   Upwork and ChatGPT comparison
ChatGPT conversations and Upwork job posts offer complementary views of how work gets done.
ChatGPT data show which activities people undertake directly with a general-purpose AI system, while
Upwork data show which activities clients seek freelancers to perform. Comparing their distribution
across broad work areas reveals where direct AI use and paid freelance demand align and where they
differ.
   Exhibit 2.1 shows that how people use ChatGPT and the work that employers solicit on Upwork ex-
hibit markedly different activities. This contrast suggests a division between activities people frequently
undertake through a general-purpose AI interface and those for which clients seek specialized remote
workers. Direct ChatGPT use is more concentrated in text-based and advisory activities, while Upwork
postings are more concentrated in technical implementation, visual production, and work that often
requires specialized tools, domain expertise, or delivery of a finished product.
   Content writing and professional and business writing account for 14.5 percent and 10.2 percent of
sampled ChatGPT conversations, respectively, compared with approximately 1.3 percent and 0.4 percent
of Upwork job posts. Customer service, financial planning, and market research are also relatively
more prominent in ChatGPT use. By contrast, graphic and presentation design, video and animation,
quality-assurance testing, mobile development, digital marketing, and web development represent
substantially larger shares of Upwork postings than of ChatGPT conversations.
   Next, we examine whether the relative intensity of AI use across activities is related to changes in
the composition of demand for freelance work on Upwork Marketplace. We treat Upwork Marketplace
job postings in H1 2023 as a baseline for how demand for remote work was distributed across activities.
ChatGPT provides a way to use AI to perform many activities, but some may still prefer to hire human
workers for many reasons, including if AI capabilities are lacking on their specific tasks. We therefore

2.2   Price and quantity margins of AI-related work
AI-related job posts on the Upwork Marketplace carry substantially higher posted project budgets than
non-AI postings. When requesting freelance work on Upwork, clients can choose between posting a
fixed-price budget for a project or an hourly rate they are willing to pay. We compare AI-related and
non-AI postings separately for fixed-price projects and hourly work. Fixed-price budgets capture the
proposed payment for a project or deliverable, while posted hourly rates capture the proposed price of
worker time. For hourly postings that result in a hire, we also examine hours worked during the first 90
days of the contract. Together, these measures help distinguish differences in project budgets, the price
per hour, and the amount of labor used.
   Exhibit 2.4 shows that fixed-price budgets are approximately 70 percent higher for AI-related jobs
overall and remain about 52 percent higher after controlling for work subcategory and posting month.
The gap in posted hourly rates is smaller, where the gap between AI and non-AI-related jobs is only
about 9 percent and falls to roughly 1.5 percent after the same adjustment.
   This contrast reflects an important difference between the two contract types. In a fixed-price contract,
the client purchases a project or deliverable as a whole, so the posted budget incorporates both the value
and the scope of the work. Hourly contracts allow us to distinguish between the posted price of worker
time and the quantity of labor purchased. While adjusted differences in posted hourly rates are small,
AI-related work involves substantially more hours per hired contract. Among hired hourly contracts with
positive recorded hours, hours worked during the first 90 days after hire are approximately 41 percent
higher for AI-related work, controlling for work subcategory and calendar month. The AI/non-AI gap is
therefore much more pronounced in the amount of labor purchased per hired contract than in the posted
price per hour.
   This pattern also helps interpret the large fixed-price gap. In a fixed-price contract, the amount of
labor, project scope, and value of the final deliverable are bundled into a single budget. Larger AI-related
budgets may therefore reflect larger or more intensive projects rather than simply a higher price for an
otherwise identical unit of labor. Taken together, the large fixed-price budget gap, negligible hourly-rate
gap, and substantially greater realized hours point to a common pattern: AI-related work is associated
with greater demand for labor and larger projects, rather than primarily with a higher price for each
hour of worker time. For example, AI may increase the returns to experimentation and refinement,
encouraging clients to explore more alternatives and invest additional time in improving the quality of
the final output, or to having workers complete and “own” larger, more complex bundles of tasks. This
mechanism is analogous to the rebound effect in the energy literature, where lower costs can encourage
greater use (Jevons, 1865; Sorrell, 2009).
   This distinction between price and quantity margins is consistent with recent evidence that labor-
market adjustment associated with AI can operate strongly through employment and hiring while wage
responses are smaller or more heterogeneous (Chandar and Klein Teeselink, 2026; Rinz, 2026; de Souza,
2025). The freelance setting makes this distinction particularly transparent because hourly contracts
separately reveal a requested price for worker time and the subsequent quantity of labor used.

3     AI is becoming embedded in broader and more novel workflows
As AI changes the relative costs of performing tasks and coordinating across tasks, it is likely to affect
both which tasks are performed and which tasks are performed together. We examine this possibility
in three steps. First, we ask whether AI-related demand is spreading across a broader range of tasks.
Second, we examine whether those tasks are being combined into broader projects. Finally, among
projects with similar numbers of tasks, do AI-related postings combine tasks that clients less often
requested together before the generative AI era?

3.1   AI-related work spans more activities and larger bundles
We summarize the overall task mix across AI-related job postings using the effective number of task
clusters. A higher value means that work is less concentrated in a few activities. Between early 2023 and
late 2025, the effective number of task clusters increased from 14.6 to 21.2—approximately 45 percent.
An effective count of 21.2 tasks means that its concentration is equivalent to a distribution with about 21
equally represented clusters.
   Thus, AI-related work is becoming less concentrated in a few dominant activities and more evenly
spread across activities. This broadening is consistent with AI becoming embedded in a wider range of
economic activities rather than remaining concentrated in a small number of early applications such as
chatbot development or text generation. We plot the effective number of task clusters in AI-related job
postings for comparison across these two periods in Panel A of Exhibit 3.1.
   Having shown that AI-related work is distributed across a wider range of economic activities, we next
examine whether this breadth also appears within individual jobs. To do so, we compare the number of
distinct task clusters in AI-related and non-AI-related job postings.

   As an illustration, some AI-related postings combine chatbot or machine-learning development
with computer vision and AI API integration, while others combine AI-generated video with video
production, editing, and motion graphics. These projects bring AI-specific capabilities together with the
implementation and production tasks required to turn them into finished products.

3.2   AI jobs combine tasks that were rarely paired before
We next turn to the composition of the bundle itself. If AI is associated with a reorganization of work, we
might also expect to see changes in which tasks clients choose to purchase together. To understand this,
we compare how frequently AI-related and non-AI-related jobs combine tasks that were uncommon
together before the generative-AI era. For each current job posting containing at least two distinct tasks,
we identify all unique task pairs and count how many pre-AI postings contained each pair.
   We classify pairs appearing in fewer than 200 pre-AI postings, including those with no observed
occurrence, as historically uncommon; pairs appearing in at least 200 pre-AI postings are established.
We then calculate the share of each job’s task pairs meeting this definition and average those shares
separately for AI-related and non-AI-related jobs. Each job receives equal weight, regardless of how
many task pairs it contains.
   The composition of these projects also differs. Historically uncommon combinations—task pairs
appearing in fewer than 200 pre-AI postings—account for an average of 8.4 percent of pairs within
AI-related jobs, compared with 2.4 percent within other jobs. This measure captures both combinations

4   Conclusion
The findings suggest that AI is changing both the division of work and the organization of freelance
projects. ChatGPT use is relatively concentrated in writing and advice, while Upwork postings place
greater emphasis on technical implementation, visual production, and specialized delivery. Within
AI-related freelance work, demand is spreading across more activities and shifting toward creative
production and applied business workflows. AI-related jobs also combine tasks in less historically
familiar ways, even among projects of comparable breadth.
    The contrast between larger project budgets, more hours per hired hourly contract, and a small
difference in posted hourly rates suggests that project scope is an important part of this adjustment. AI
may make individual activities easier to perform while encouraging clients to undertake more ambitious
projects, explore additional alternatives, or invest more effort in refinement. Projects that combine AI-
generated video with editing and motion graphics, or chatbot development with application integration,
illustrate how new capabilities can create demand for complementary work.
    These patterns connect the adoption of AI to the economics of organizing work. By changing the
costs of performing activities and coordinating across them, AI may alter which tasks clients find useful
to purchase together. Value may increasingly lie in defining the problem, connecting tools and activities,
applying domain judgment, and delivering a finished outcome. The resulting adjustment can redraw
project boundaries as clients assemble different combinations of capabilities to achieve their goals.
    Upwork and ChatGPT do not represent the labor market as a whole, but together they offer an early
view of a broader shift: AI is changing not just the tasks people perform, but the way those tasks are
divided, combined, and brought to market.

A    Composition of task-cluster pairings
Exhibit 3.2 shows that AI-related jobs contain a greater share of task pairs with limited pre-AI precedent.
This section examines the composition of historically uncommon pairings within AI-related jobs. In
particular, we ask whether historically uncommon pairs in AI-related jobs primarily join AI-associated
tasks with traditional work, or instead reflect traditional tasks being combined with one another in less
familiar ways.
   Most historically uncommon pair occurrences in current AI-related jobs consist of two tasks that each
appeared frequently in pre-period postings. Of such pair occurrences, 98.6 percent involve two tasks
that were each observed in at least 200 pre-period postings. Thus, historically uncommon combinations
do not generally consist of two wholly new tasks. Rather, they often join tasks that each had an observed
pre-period history but were seldom requested together.
   To further characterize these combinations, we apply a curated AI-task definition at the task level.
The curated list maps 141 normalized inferred task labels to eight AI-focused work specializations: AI
Chatbot Development; AI Content Writing; AI Data Annotation & Labeling; AI Image Generation &
Editing; AI Integration; AI Speech & Audio Generation; AI Video Generation & Editing; and Generative
AI Modeling. A task cluster is classified as AI-associated if at least half of its component task labels
appear in this dictionary; otherwise, it is classified as traditional.
   The benefit of this approach is that it classifies clusters according to their task content rather than
according to how often they appear in jobs already labeled AI-related. It therefore reduces the mechanical
overlap between the historically uncommon pairings being described and the rule used to identify an
AI-associated cluster. If the same mixed AI–traditional pattern appears under both the curated and the
report’s existing usage-based measure of task-level AI exposure, the interpretation does not depend
solely on defining a task as AI-intensive because it is frequently used in AI-related jobs.
   For completeness, we also use the report’s existing job post measure of task-level AI exposure: the
share of current postings containing a task cluster that are classified as AI-related. For this decomposition,
we classify clusters with an AI-related share of at least 20 percent as AI-intensive and all other clusters as
traditional. This converts the report’s continuous exposure measure into a binary cluster classification
for the purpose of categorizing task pairs.
   Using each of the two definitions, we classify task pairs as AI-only (both clusters are AI-associated or
AI-intensive), mixed (one cluster is AI-associated or AI-intensive and the other is traditional), or fully
traditional (neither cluster meets the AI rule).
   Under both definitions, mixed AI–traditional pairs are the largest component of historically uncom-
mon pair occurrences in AI-related jobs: about four per 100 total pair occurrences, compared with
roughly one to two for each other pair type. This pattern is consistent with AI-related jobs bringing
AI-associated capabilities together with traditional work in combinations that had limited historical
precedent. It should not be interpreted as showing that AI has broadly caused traditional tasks to be
recombined with one another.

B     Methodology

AI-related work classification
   We use Upwork’s internal AI-related jobs pipeline (a GPT-powered LLM with structured prompting)
designed to identify AI-related jobs on the platform. The model evaluates the full context of each job post,
including the job title, description, requested skills, category/subcategory/specialization ontology, and
other job characteristics and information, when applicable, to determine whether the job is AI-related. As
AI evolves rapidly, this context-aware approach identifies AI-related jobs beyond predefined keywords
by understanding broader context and client intent. The pipeline runs at scale across job posts and
produces a standardized AI-related job classification for downstream analytics and reporting.
Task construction
    We use detailed activity descriptions to represent the types of work performed on Upwork. We
create a library of 3,000+ activity descriptions from Upwork’s occupational taxonomy, and historical job
posts. We then group similar activity descriptions into 400+ standardized tasks, also called task clusters
in the technical methodology, using embedding-based clustering, using UMR (Upwork’s Meaningful
Representation), Upwork’s semantic AI model, with additional AI-assisted and manual review to
improve consistency and interpretability. This creates a stable framework for tracking how different
types of work, job volume, and value change over time, including shifts associated with the adoption of
AI.

  voice talent; Audio & Music Production are questions related to editing, mixing, or composes

  sure to distinguish actual professional tutoring/research from a student's own schoolwork

• Community Management & Tagging includes community moderation and image tagging; Customer
  Service & Tech Support concerns general customer service, onboarding, success, and technical
  support.
• If multiple categories apply, choose the primary requested deliverable. Do not treat
  topic/industry alone as a task: a blog post discussing healthcare and medicine blog is
  Content Writing, medical translation is Translation & Localization Services, and software
  for HR is software development rather than Recruiting & Human Resources.
