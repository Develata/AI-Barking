LinkDrift: How Leaked Links Exposed Internal Files at Fortune 500 Companies

The links had already leaked. The files were still accessible. We followed URLs from malware logs and public indexes to sensitive corporate material, without a login.

A link we found in malware logs opened a folder of security-monitoring material in a large company’s SaaS file-sharing platform. No login. No password. The listing included detection rules, search queries, logging configurations, and sample logs. We reported the exposure, and the company acknowledged it and restricted access.

The problem extended to other companies on the Fortune 500 list. We identified publicly accessible sensitive or internal material at 19 companies. Across the cases, we found operational runbooks, financial models, personal data, and documents intended only for business partners.

The links were already circulating outside these companies, yet they still worked. Access required no exploit. And one link could expose an entire folder containing nested folders and thousands of files.

This is LinkDrift. In this post, we explain how these links spread, what remained accessible, and how security teams can find and close similar exposures.

The scale

We started with exposed links, then worked backward to identify the organizations they belonged to. After removing duplicates and matching links to their owners, our dataset covered 326 organizations.

Among the Fortune 500 companies in that dataset, about 800 links provided access without an account. Most did not require a password, and many allowed files to be downloaded.

Those 800 links were not all sensitive exposures. Most of the material we accessed was intended to be public, including conference presentations, published reports, and marketing content. A smaller subset exposed sensitive or internal material at 19 Fortune 500 companies.

The tracker below counts accessible links, not individual files. A single link can open a folder containing many nested folders and thousands of files. Link counts alone do not show how much material is accessible.

 [#lightbox-2]

An excerpt from our research tracker showing sector, country, Fortune tier, and accessible-link counts. Company-identifying columns are excluded. (Click to enlarge.)

Our inspection varied by case. In some, filenames and folder structure were enough to identify the nature of the material, so we did not open the files. In others, we opened a sample to confirm the contents, then stopped.

These findings came from a limited sample of links already circulating in external datasets. Many links in our dataset remained untested. We believe the wider exposure extends beyond the cases documented here, but this research does not establish its full scale.

How the links spread, and why they still worked

A shared link can travel far beyond its intended recipients. We found URLs in three types of sources:

Infostealer logs. Malware can collect browser data and other information from infected devices, including share URLs. These logs may later be sold, distributed, or added to searchable indexes.

URL scanners. Someone submits a link to check whether it is safe. If the submission is public, the scanner can make the URL searchable by others.

Public repositories and archives. Links appear in code repositories, public tickets, forums, and archived pages, where they can remain discoverable long after they were first shared.

 [#lightbox-3]

A query for the file-sharing domain returned a list of share URLs already recorded in a public index. The link tokens are masked.

The exposures came in two forms:

Links that required only the URL. For shares set to “anyone with the link,” no account or password was needed. Once the URL circulated outside its intended audience, it still provided access to the material.

Links whose passwords had also leaked. Some shares required a password, but that password was available alongside the URL in the external data. Password protection alone could not prevent access once both had leaked. This can happen without any change to the application’s settings.

The links remained accessible when we tested them. The platform supported expiry, but these links had not expired at the time of our checks. From outside the company, we usually could not establish whether access had always been available or had changed over time.

What else was exposed

The security-monitoring folder was one example. Other findings included:

An events and ticketing business: internal documentation about partner sign-in and identity architecture, internal environment names, and an integration specification for an operational access-control system.

A consumer-services platform: partner-only procedures for handling refunds and reviewing fraud.

A tax-preparation firm and a residential property manager: files containing personal and financial information. We prioritized these cases for disclosure.

A Fortune 500 commercial real-estate services firm: folders that appeared to be private deal rooms but were accessible to anyone with the link.

At more than one company, links also exposed file-upload forms without requiring an account. We did not submit files or test what happened after submission.

We are withholding specific filenames, document contents, and URLs. Those details are shared privately with the affected organizations.

How companies responded

Responses to our disclosures varied. Some companies acknowledged the exposure, restricted access, and sent detailed messages thanking us for the report. In other cases, follow-up checks showed that access had been restricted, but we received no reply.

Some organizations have not responded to our reports or follow-ups. Our disclosure work is ongoing.

 [#lightbox-6]

A security leader’s response thanking the team for a responsible, professional disclosure. Identifying details are redacted.

These cases involved how companies used and configured a file-sharing platform. They did not require exploiting a vulnerability in the platform or the company’s own code. That can make ownership less obvious: the platform provides the sharing controls, while the customer decides how to use them and who checks that they remain appropriate.

Why approval and monitoring can still leave gaps

These cases raise a broader question: how does an organization know that the applications its employees use have the protections they need today?

Point-in-time checks. An application may launch without the right protections, or its settings and usage may change after an initial review. A previous approval does not establish whether the required controls are in place today.

Limited application and tenant coverage. Monitoring that depends on supported products and connected tenants can leave other applications, accounts, and workspaces unchecked. Coverage of the main corporate tenant does not establish coverage everywhere company data is shared.

Applications employees build. The same access questions apply to enterprise applications created by employees using low-code platforms and AI coding tools. Our research has also identified public-access exposures in these applications.

How to check your own exposure

Start with the sharing settings and links that could expose your organization’s files:

Check shared folders and their contents. Review what each folder link makes accessible, including nested folders and files. Looking only at individual file-sharing settings can leave the broader exposure unclear.

Do not rely on keeping a URL secret. A link can reach people and systems beyond its original recipients. Sensitive material should require access by approved users.

Set expiry dates for public links that are genuinely needed. For sensitive material, require authenticated access rather than relying on a shared password alone.

Review links that accept uploads. Keep them open only where there is a clear business need and an owner responsible for them.

Monitor for exposed links and credentials. External exposure checks complement configuration checks by identifying URLs and passwords that have already circulated outside the company.

If you find an exposure, revoke or restrict the link and verify that unauthorized access has stopped. Review available access records and rotate any exposed credentials. Asking recipients to delete their copies does not close an active share.

Keeping approved applications secure

Organizations need to verify the protections in both the SaaS products they buy and the applications their employees build.
