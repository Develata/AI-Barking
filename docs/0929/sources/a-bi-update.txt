A YouTuber who said his
Muse AI agent
gave out his home address without his permission has got to the bottom of what happened — and it comes down to clicking one button.
Matt Robb said in a Tuesday update on X that he had selected an "Allow Always" option when he first asked
Meta's AI agent
to sell his computer keyboard on Facebook Marketplace, believing it would still seek his approval before accepting offers.
"It didn't, so be careful," Robb wrote.
Instead, Robb said, the setting allowed Muse to send messages on his behalf using a template containing the pickup address he had previously supplied. A Marketplace buyer later showed up at his building after Muse accepted a $600 offer and exchanged messages with him
without Robb's knowledge
, he said.
"I didn't think it would send it out to everyone that gave me an offer," Robb wrote. "It's worth checking."
Robb said he spoke with Meta's Muse team, which reviewed the logs with him and said it would make the permission prompt clearer to avoid any misunderstanding.
In a reply to Robb's Tuesday post, David Singleton, a Meta Superintelligence Labs staffer, said that Meta had confirmed there was "no breach of privacy controls."
"We appreciate the opportunity to learn from your experience with Muse and keep making it better for everyone!" Singleton added.
'Double-check what permissions AI has in your life'
The episode is an example of the
growing pains
facing the buzzy yet nascent personal AI agent market.

Tools like Muse and
Instinct promise to book trips
, sift through inboxes, and complete online errands on behalf of the user. However,
there's a tradeoff
: they require access to accounts, payments, messages, and personal information.
Early users are still working out what
permissions to grant
and how closely to supervise agents, amid reports of unintended actions, hallucinations, and security concerns.
Over the weekend, Robb said in a viral post that Muse had accepted a lowball offer for a keyboard, shared his address, and communicated with the buyer when he arrived at Robb's building — all without his knowledge.

In Tuesday's update, Robb said Meta also told him that a separate
display error
had made a $600 offer appear to have been accepted, even though he had set a minimum price of $700.
The glitch removed the "7" from the message displayed to the buyer, he said, making Muse's response — "Sounds good, 00 it is!" — appear to confirm the lower price.
Singleton said in his reply that Meta had fixed the "00" price-display issue.
Robb suggested that Meta add a "Sent By Muse" label beneath each agent-generated message so recipients can distinguish AI-generated messages from those sent by a human.
"There was really no way of knowing who sent what in the chat, which is very concerning," he wrote.
Robb said he and the buyer resolved the mix-up, and the buyer later came by to purchase another item.
His takeaway for other early adopters: "Take care everyone, and double-check what permissions AI has in your life."
