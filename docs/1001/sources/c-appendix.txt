A. Measuring exposure

Our analyses use data from O*NET, a database of around 900 occupations
linked with descriptions of around 19,000 job tasks. We first use Claude to
classify tasks as physical, cognitive, or interpersonal. Our analysis focuses on
physical tasks, which we think require robots to automate. Though humanoid
robots might perform cognitive and interpersonal work too, automating most
of this work doesn't require better robots per se. We then assess whether robots
today can perform each physical task and under what circumstances by
querying Claude with web search. Full prompts appear in Appendix F.

A.1 Task taxonomy

We decompose tasks into physical, cognitive, and interpersonal work, following
a long tradition in labor economics and job analysis. For example, the job
taxonomy in the Dictionary of Occupational Titles categorizes work by
whether it acts on "Data/People/Things."

We define three variables to describe the physical, cognitive, and interpersonal
components of job tasks. For component k  {physical,cognitive,interpersonal}
and task t, define

· timetk: time for task t spent on k work. Times sum to 100.
· leveltk: level of skill on component k required to perform task t, 0-5. This scale

    measures the practice a person needs to reach that level, from none (1) to elite
    performance that few trained adults reach (5). Level 0 means the task makes
    only incidental use of that component.
· essentialtk: indicator for whether task t could not be completed were
    component k removed.

We use Claude to rate each O*NET 29.3 task on these dimensions, providing it
with the task description and the title and description of the occupation that
performs it. Since O*NET tasks can be quite broad, we prompt Claude to
describe specific instances of tasks that a typical US worker would encounter.
For example, consider the nondescript task "Perform teller duties as required"
for new accounts clerks. Claude notes that this work involves handling a
"suspected counterfeit bill, ... or a frustrated customer disputing a fee," as well
as "counting currency by hand ... buying and selling cash to the vault ... pulling

require a roughly equal mix of physical, cognitive, and interpersonal time.

Tasks we identify as physical are shaded orange. These require at least 50%

                  physicalt = 1[timetp  50 or leveltp  3 or essentialtp = 1]

"Dig trenches" meets all three criteria. "Teach dance students" also counts
despite being only 35% physical by time, since it requires physical expertise
built over years and could not be accomplished without physical skill.

As shown in Appendix Figure 1, tasks cluster in the bottom-left physical corner,
and along the cognitive-interpersonal edge on the right. Most work is either
mostly physical, or a mix of cognitive and interpersonal. The fact that many
physical tasks do not also require much cognitive or interpersonal input suggests
that a physically capable robot could perform most physical tasks in the economy.
We assess robot exposure for the roughly 7,600 tasks classified as physical.

In this figure and throughout, we drop occupations without O*NET task
descriptions and those without employment and wage data from BLS (2025).
These jobs account for 3% of employment and are more likely to be in the two
broad occupation groups "Management, business, science & arts" and
"Production, transportation & material moving." Since the former is likely less
exposed to robots and the latter more, these exclusions on net seem unlikely to
change overall exposure estimates.

A.2 Task taxonomy validation

To validate these ratings, we link occupation time and skill level variables to
data from the 2023 and 2025 BLS Occupational Requirements Surveys, a
survey of employers to collect data on job requirements. We identify a subset of
variables describing physical, cognitive, and interpersonal job requirements.1
These are aggregated into indices for physical demands, problem solving skills,
and social demands.2

We compare these occupation requirements to our own scores of how physical,
cognitive, and interpersonal jobs are. We average task time and skill variables

by occupation, weighting by time spent on tasks. For each occupation o and
each component k  {physical,cognitive,interpersonal}, define

                                    timeok = to (timetk/100) wt

                                        levelok = to leveltk wt

Here, wt is our estimate of the share of time occupation o spends on task t.
Variable timeok therefore measures share of time spent on physical, cognitive,
and interpersonal work, while levelok measures time-weighted skill demands.

Appendix Figure 2 displays correlations between our ratings and the BLS
occupational requirement indices. The corresponding sets of physical,
cognitive, and interpersonal variables are quite correlated. For example, the
physical time share correlates 0.76 with the physical demands index, and the
cognitive and interpersonal skill levels correlate 0.89 and 0.78 with the problem
solving and social demands indices. All physical variables negatively correlate
with all cognitive and interpersonal variables. Meanwhile, cognitive and
interpersonal variables are positively correlated. These patterns mirror those in
Appendix Figure 1.

A.3 Robot exposure

Our occupation robot exposure index begins by rating exposure for tasks
classified as physical, as described above. For these tasks t, we measure ordinal
task exposuret  {E0,E1,E2,E3} by the kind of environment E that a robot
requires to perform this task:

· E0: Robot cannot perform task.
· E1: Robot can perform task in a purpose-built robotic work environment, like

    a factory assembly line.
· E2: Robot can perform task in a structured human work facility, like a

    logistics warehouse.
· E3: Robot can perform task in an unstructured environment, like a city road.

We say that a robot can perform a task if it produces a unit of output at quality
an employer would accept. To focus on potential job automation, our prompt
emphasizes that a robot must act largely without human control. That

A.4 Alternative robot exposure measures

We also consider several other ways of measuring exposure. These produce
similar conclusions about robot exposure.

Our exposure classifier counts robot capabilities shown in demonstrations
when Claude finds these credible. Demonstrations aren't as solid evidence that
robots can do tasks as commercial deployments. But dropping tasks that cite
only demonstrations, which we explicitly recorded as part of the evidence
retrieval, lowers the share of exposed physical work by only 1 percentage point.
It also produces nearly the same ranking of jobs by exposure as our main
measure (rank correlation 0.99).

Sometimes Claude can't find a robot that does a task exactly as written, but
counts the task as exposed when robots do related ones. For example, automotive
service technicians and mechanics "Align wheels, axles, frames, torsion bars, and
steering mechanisms of automobiles, using special alignment equipment and
wheel-balancing machines." Claude can't find a robot that does so in repair shops,
where this occupation typically works, but rates this task E1 because of
wheel-aligning robots in car factories.3 For "Dig holes for power poles, using
power augers or shovels, set poles in place with cranes, and hoist poles upright,
using winches," Claude cites a pile-driving robot that moves around solar farm
sites and drives steel poles into the ground, along with other robots that dig.4

Excluding ratings that rely on related robots decreases the share of exposed
physical work from about three-quarters to a half. Though these cases require
judging whether capabilities transfer across tasks, they show which tasks
robots could likely do, or are close to doing, with today's technology. We also

define an exposure measure based on the gap between where humans perform
a task and where robots can do it today:

            exposureCoverageo = to (exposuret/humanEnvironmentt)wt

Here, humanEnvironmentt is the typical human working environment for task t.
Taking the ratio between robot exposure and human working environments
counts a task less if robots are further from doing it like humans. This occupation
exposure index ranges from 0, meaning robots cannot do any tasks, to 1,
meaning robots can do all tasks in the same environments as do workers. Again,
this measure is highly rank-correlated with our primary exposure index, at 0.99.

In Figure 6, we combine robot exposure with LLM exposure measures from
Eloundou et al. (2024). Specifically, we define

                                  llmExposureo = to 1[t > 0]wt

               llmAndRobotExposureo = to 1[t > 0 or exposuret > 0]wt

Here, t is Eloundou et al.'s GPT-4 task exposure measure. Eloundou et al. use
an older O*NET edition, so these calculations omit around 50 tasks.

A variant of this measure that counts fractional rather than binary robot
exposure for each task produces a similar pattern of results. Specifically, we
calculate the share of a task's instances that are exposed, and use that to
measure LLM and robot exposure:

        llmAndRobotExposureFraco = to max{1[t > 0], exposureFract}wt

For example, exposure in production jobs decreases from around 90% to 80%
when using the share of exposed task instances. This may reflect instances
where today's robots encounter complex exceptions that require humans to
handle. For example, 75% of instances are rated E1 for the task "Spray chopped
fiberglass, resins, and catalysts onto prepared molds or dies using pneumatic
spray guns with chopper attachments." Robots can spray fiberglass on molds
for large structures like yacht hulls, but Claude notes that they can't yet do the
task instance, "Complex molds and detail work: spraying deep narrow recesses,
lockers, undercuts and flange returns, and spraying over foam cores,

B. Historical robot exposure

Our measures of job exposure to robots today are most informative if they
predict which jobs are more or less likely to face disruption in the future. We
validate our measure by connecting historical robot exposure to changes in job
employment and pay over the past 50 years.

B.1 Historical task and labor market data

To track robot exposure and labor market outcomes over time, we compile task
description and labor market data for 5 periods from 1977 to today. Sources are
listed in Appendix Figure 3. We take snapshots of the US labor market decadally
from 1980 to now, taking employment, wage, demographic, and occupation data
from the 5 percent Census when available and the 5-year American Community
Survey when not.6 From the historical O*NET and DOT editions close to these
dates, we get rich descriptions of work as it existed at the time.7

Using DOT data, we construct job titles, descriptions, and task statements to
roughly match the structure of O*NET. DOT data enumerate about 12,000 jobs
with paragraphs describing specific work duties. Similar to Kogan et al. (2023)
and Autor and Thompson (2025), we split sentences into task descriptions.

Our labor market data cleaning follows Autor et al. (2024). To link job
descriptions and labor market data over time, we crosswalk DOT occupation
codes, O*NET-SOC codes, and Census occupation codes following Autor and
Dorn (2013) and Autor et al. (2024). Census occupation codes identify around
300 consistently defined jobs in our sample. When aggregating finer
occupation codes to linked Census codes, we weight by estimated employment
in that period.8 To compute average occupation wages, we first adjust
individual wages each decade by race, sex, education, and age (Autor et al.,
2024). This adjustment helps remove effects of changes in worker composition
in an occupation over time.9 Employment outcomes account for hours worked
and so measure full-time-equivalent workers. We use Census rather than
OEWS data, which we use in present-day analyses, to keep a consistent labor

B.2 Measuring historical robot exposure

Ideally, we would measure historical robot exposure with an equally capable
version of Claude trained only on historical data. Models trained on modern
data "know" which tasks in 1977 were eventually automated, which could
contaminate historical judgments.10 To mitigate this look-ahead bias, we prompt
Claude to consider work as it existed in the target year and require sources that
place any cited robot in the appropriate time.11 Historical robot exposure is
constructed as described above using these judgments.

each period from 1977 to 2026, weighting tasks by estimated work time
requirements and Census employment. Large exposed occupations in 1977
included textile sewing machine operators and assemblers of electrical
equipment.12 The trend in this figure captures a novel, albeit expected, result

exposure. An important characteristic of these robots is that they can often
move around rather than needing to be fed inputs at a fixed location. While
workers that move materials around were E1 exposed in 1977 due to palletizers,
mobile warehouse robots now expose such workers at level E2.

B.3 Historical robot exposure and labor market impacts

We connect what robots could do to labor market impacts, tracking exposure
and job data in three overlapping periods of roughly 20 years. We regress
changes in log wages and employment on starting year robot exposure, pooling
data across these three periods from 1977 to 2024.

Since past robots mostly worked in structured environments like factories, we
measure historical job exposure by the time-weighted share of tasks exposed at
E1 or above. Historical exposure is related to labor market outcomes with
regressions of the form:

                             lnyoip = exposedTaskTimeop+ip+oip

Here, lnyoip is the change in occupation o's adjusted log wage or employment
in industry i, starting from period p, and exposedTaskTimeopis the share of
occupation o's tasks that robots could perform in the beginning of period p. Of

course, the US economy underwent large changes over this period not
attributable to robots, like trade with China and computerization. Regressions
therefore compare changes in employment and wages for jobs more or less
exposed to robots in the same industry, operationalized by adding industry by
period fixed effects ip.

The coefficient  gives the approximate percent change in wages or
employment over 20 years for an occupation with all tasks exposed to robots,
relative to an unexposed occupation in the same industry. We pool the three
periods and weight observations by initial employment, normalizing so that
each period counts equally. Standard errors are clustered by occupation.

Appendix Figure 5 presents results. The left panel plots occupations by robot
exposure and changes in log wages averaged over the three periods. Compared
to an unexposed occupation in the same industry, wages were 7.1% lower 20
years later for an occupation whose tasks could all be done by robots. This effect
is precisely estimated with a 95% confidence interval of 5.4% to 8.9%. For
example, welders, solderers, and metal cutters had three-quarters of their work
time exposed to robots on average. Over the next 20 years, their wages grew
about 3% less than for workers in other occupations in the same industries.15

Employment also fell for exposed occupations (right panel). Relative to an
unexposed occupation in the same industry, an occupation whose tasks can all
be done by robots is predicted to lose 34.2% of its jobs over about 20 years, with
a 95% confidence interval of 16.4% to 52.0%.

The auto industry, where robots are most common and are widely believed to
have eliminated jobs, shows the pattern.16 In 1991, machine operators were in
the top 5% of all workers by robot exposure, because robots such as automated
painters and welders could do the same work. About 100,000 such workers
held jobs in the motor vehicle sector in 1990. By 2012, about 50,000 did. The
sector shrank overall, but not all its workers lost jobs. Industrial machinery
repairers, whose exposure was one-quarter that of machine operators, saw a
50% rise in motor vehicle employment. Production supervisors or foremen,
who were nearly unexposed, saw a 10% employment rise.

findings in other countries are mixed. As the case of motor vehicles illustrates,

B.4 How quickly have robot capabilities advanced?

New robots will be able to do new tasks that they can't today. To measure the
historical rate of progress, we rate job tasks from 1977 using robots as of 2026.
Fixing tasks as of 1977 isolates how fast new robots became able to do work
they previously could not do.

Appendix Figure 8 plots exposure today for job tasks in 1977.24 Robots can now
do around 60% of the tasks they could not before.25 Robots that help herd, milk,
feed, and vaccinate cows can monitor health and administer medicine, so the
1977 task "Cares for livestock and poultry, observing general condition and
administering simple medications to animals and fowls" is now rated E2.26 On
construction sites, a bricklaying robot now "Spreads soft bed of mortar that
serves as base and binder for block, using trowel."27 And an autonomous mining
vehicle "Drives machine into pile of material blasted from working face" to
scoop ore.28 From 1977 to today, robots became able to perform about 2% more
of the physical work they previously could not per year.29

Robots also improved at tasks they could already do. In 1977, automated
photoelectric sorters could detect unripe produce on a conveyor belt and eject
it automatically. Because of this and other systems, "May examine and sort
items tallied" for talliers (clerical professionals who account for inventory) was
rated E1 in 1977, with just over half of task instances exposed.30 Today this task
is E2: a warehouse robot with computer vision and suction grippers can sort

C.2 Barriers to automation

Our exposure measure ranks jobs by what today's robots can do, but we aim to
understand how the economy will change in the future. To automate work,
robots must be capable but also overcome other barriers. Task descriptions in
Appendix C.1 point to regulation and human preferences, and robot costs
matter as well. Here we take a first look at barriers to automation for physical
tasks. We include exposed and unexposed tasks, since robots haven't
automated either at scale.

For each physical task, we ask Claude to identify barriers to automation today
that, on their own, would prevent robots from doing a significant share of this
work. Barriers include capability, cost, human preference, and regulation. For
capability, Claude picks the most important barrier among four robot skills:
manipulation, planning and reasoning, mobility and strength, and perception.

We instruct Claude to cite sources for its judgments and name specific
regulations. It also receives the output from our robot exposure classifier. For
tasks that robots today do not do, Claude judges whether a robot could be built
with today's technology. If not, capability is a barrier. If so, Claude judges what
would be a barrier if one existed, drawing on similar robots today. These
estimates are more forward-looking than our robot exposure measure, at the
cost of being less grounded in today's robots.36

In addition to cost, robot capabilities hold back automation for 68% of physical

robots can do them in some environment, but today's capabilities are
insufficient to automate these tasks at scale.

One likely cause is that robots and humans do tasks in different environments.
Consider painting. Factories have used robot arms to spray paint since the
1960s, and newer robots can paint complex patterns in three dimensions, as well
as navigate and paint inside buildings.37 Human construction and maintenance
painters may work on the side of a bridge to "Apply paint, stain, varnish, enamel,
or other finishes to equipment, buildings, bridges, or other structures, using
brushes, spray guns, or rollers." Robots could paint parts of bridges before
assembly or indoors at a construction site, so this task is E1. Automating it at
scale could be difficult unless robot painters become more capable.38

Advances in manipulation abilities specifically would be needed for robots to
do more work. Human hands manipulate objects very well compared to robots,
which especially struggle with deformable or delicate objects, using tools, and
feeling by touch.39 Just over half of physical tasks cannot be automated by
robots at scale with today's technology primarily because robots can't
manipulate objects well enough.

the metal back to its original shape. They hold a block called a dolly on the
other side of the metal, which matches its intended contour, to brace the blows.
Periodically, workers run a coarse file over the surface, scratching it lightly to
reveal where the metal is still dented. This combination of fine motor control
and touch is beyond robots today.

D.1 Estimating robot costs

Building on the results in Appendix C.2, we estimate how much it costs for
robots to perform physical tasks with today's technology. To ground our
estimates in real prices as much as possible, we estimate costs for tasks that
robots can perform today (E1-E3).

We prompt Claude to estimate total annual costs for robots to perform each
exposed task. Claude receives the list of robots and sources cited in task

exposure ratings and is instructed to search for sources on costs. Claude

annualized using hardware service lives and the cost of capital.

Appendix Figure 11 displays itemized costs for the task, "Cut, trim, bone, tie,
and grind meats, such as beef, pork, poultry, and fish, to prepare in cooking

In line with industry estimates, robot hardware itself is only around a third of
total costs.43 Accessory costs like tooling, integration, and maintenance make
up around 42% of costs. Workers are also required to oversee robots and handle
exceptions. Claude estimates that one technician could oversee robots that

oversight for robots to replace the yearly output of one butcher.

D.2 Robot versus labor costs

In Figures 7 and 8, we compare estimated robot and labor costs to gauge how
costs may limit robot automation. We say that jobs in an occupation are
exposed to cost-competitive robots if robots cost less than worker pay
multiplied by the fraction of time spent on exposed tasks. Specifically,

        exposedTotalTaskTimeAtCosto = employmentoexposedTaskTimeo
                1[robotCosto < totalCompensationoexposedTaskTimeo]

Here, exposedTaskTimeo is the share of occupation o's tasks that robots can do,
weighted by time. The variable robotCosto is how much it costs robots to perform
the exposed tasks for this occupation. Also, totalCompensationo is the
occupation's total compensation. Total compensation equals wages from the
OEWS (BLS, 2025) multiplied by the ratio between total compensation and
wages in the ECEC (BLS, 2026); these data are reported for 9 occupation groups.44

This is a stylized comparison between robots and workers that captures many
though not all possible costs. Costs for workers omit supervision, hiring, and
human resources needs, and robot costs omit corporate income tax effects,
though tax considerations seem to favor robots overall.45 Automation could also
push workers into remaining jobs, depressing wages. Our comparison also
assumes that wages can be split to price tasks. Granting that task cost
comparisons make some sense, potential omissions in cost estimates between
robots and workers seem roughly balanced.

Figure 8 shows how much work would be exposed to cost-competitive robots if
robot costs fell. For each cost decline, we recompute which workers are
exposed, sum time on exposed tasks, and weight by employment. At a decline
in robot costs d from 0 (current) to 1 (costless), we replace robotCosto with
(1-d)robotCosto in the formula for exposedTotalTaskTimeAtCosto. Because
robots may replace the highest-paid workers in an occupation first, we also
adjust this formula using data on wage quantiles.46 When robots are costless,
exposure reaches all tasks that today's robots can do.

Costs can also be used to measure job exposure based on what robots can do as

cost-weighted robot exposure index by multiplying the exposure index

                      exposureCostWeightedo = (totalCompensationo

When a job's robot and labor costs are equal, this equals the standard robot

and vice versa. This calculation uses median total compensation.

Jobs are ranked very similarly by these two robot exposure indices: their
employment weighted rank correlation is 0.95. So while robots are much more
expensive than labor overall, robot costs don't change the ranking of
occupational robot exposure.

E. Robot adoption scenarios

Our cost estimates, along with data on historical worker tasks and robot prices,
allow us to characterize the rate of technological advancement that would
make robots cost-competitive with a given share of workers. These scenarios
hold fixed today's job tasks and wages, projecting forward with past trends in
robot costs. Because we estimate costs per unit of yearly worker output, cost
declines can also represent capability gains, such as robots that work faster or
need less supervision. These scenarios offer a rough framework for robot
timelines, but set aside many important social, political, technical, and
economic forces.

We consider two scenarios. In business as usual, total robot deployment costs
fall 3% a year. This rate is roughly in line with price declines from 1990 to 2019.47
In rapid advances, upfront robot hardware costs decline by 12% per year and
other costs by 6% per year.48 This hardware rate aligns with quality-adjusted
price declines in industrial robots from 1990-2005, a period of rapid robot
innovation and adoption, as well as the faster end of market forecasts for
humanoids.49 In line with previous work, we model slower declines for
non-hardware costs because they involve inputs like labor and electricity.

Appendix Figure 12 projects work time exposed to cost-competitive robots

for 10% of human work today until the 2060s. Robot janitors, for example,
remain more expensive than their human counterparts until 2095. For some
tasks, robots are only around 40% more expensive, like "Clean building floors

administering first aid in emergencies.

For robot costs of newly exposed work, we scale up task labor costs by the ratio
between robot and labor costs today, separately by occupation group. Newly
exposed tasks begin about 5 times more expensive than labor, the ratio today
when averaging by exposed task time shares and employment.50

Adding new tasks does not sway these projections much. By around 2075,
robots are able to do 90% of physical tasks, but that barely affects business as
usual. Robots can already perform most physical work at some price point, so
cost effectiveness weighs more heavily on total exposure than new tasks.
Robots for new tasks also begin more expensive than labor, so they don't
noticeably increase cost-competitive exposure for several decades. Around
2060, broadened task exposure does matter in rapid advances. By then,
automation stalls absent broader capabilities as robots become cheaper than
labor on most physical work that robots can do today.

These scenarios suggest that robots may soon disrupt jobs, but also show how
much faster capabilities must improve for widespread automation. Jobs are most
at risk where robots can already do most tasks at similar costs. By 2040, business
as usual projects robots will be cost-competitive for 2.5 million jobs in

The dimensions, defined by what the work operates on:

- PHYSICAL -- acting on material things: manipulating, moving,
transforming, inspecting by handling, or navigating among physical
objects, materials, equipment, or bodies, using one's own body.
- COGNITIVE -- acting on information and ideas: taking in,
processing, producing, or deciding on information, whether it
arrives via documents, screens, instruments, or observation.
- INTERPERSONAL -- acting on people: informing, persuading, teaching,
serving, coordinating with, caring for, or otherwise changing the
state of other people, through any medium.

Boundary rules, for deciding which dimension a piece of content
belongs to:

1. OBJECT TEST. Classify by what typical instances operate ON, not
by what the task's verbs connote in isolation. The same wording can
be screen-only in one occupation and hands-on in another; your
instances, not the verbs, decide.
2. MEDIUM. Interaction with people through any medium (in person,
phone, video, text) is interpersonal. Physical co-presence by itself
does not create interpersonal content, and interacting does not by
itself create physical content.
3. PERCEPTION. Information taken in through documents, screens, or
instrument readouts is cognitive. Perception that requires
positioning one's body among objects or handling them (in-situ
inspection, palpation) also adds physical content.
4. BODIES. A person's body handled as a material object (lifting,
bathing, repositioning a patient) is physical content; attending to
that person's state and comfort while doing so is interpersonal
content. Both can be high -- many care tasks are. Do not force a
dominant dimension.

### Background

#### Task instances

Tasks are specific work activities that can be unique for each
occupation. Task statements concisely summarize work activities. A
task instance is an instantiation of a task as it is performed in
the economy and is defined by its essential work activities and its
output. Task instances and weights were written to be representative
and to span the distribution of task instances for a typical US
worker in this occupation. Weights sum to 100.

#### Robots

A robot is a programmed actuated mechanism with a degree of autonomy
to perform locomotion, manipulation or positioning. Autonomy is the
ability to perform intended tasks based on current state and
sensing, without human intervention.

A robot does a task instance if it completes the instance's    worker
essential work activities and produces output that is a close
substitute for, or better than, the output of a typical human
in this occupation.

#### The prior rating

A prior rating searched the web for robots that have done each
instance's work. It recorded the robots it found, what each has
done, and how much the setting had to be arranged for the robot: E0
means no robot has done the work anywhere; E1, only in a setting
built for robots; E2, in an ordinary workplace kept orderly; E3, in
unstructured places too. It also noted how structured the instance's
own usual setting is, on the same scale. These findings are provided
as evidence. Use them and search for more where you need it. Make
every judgment below yourself.

#### Barriers

A barrier is a reason that work is done by people rather than
robots. There are four kinds of reason.

Appendix to: "What work can robots do?"                                49
- CAPABILITY. Today's technology cannot do the work. No robot could
be built with today's technology that does what this work requires,
at the quality a worker delivers, under the conditions in which the
work is done. When this barrier is present, name the ability that
robots most lack:
- manipulation: handling and controlling objects: grasping, fine
motor control, force-sensitive handling, deformable or fragile
materials, tool use.
- perception: sensing and recognizing the state of objects,
materials, and surroundings under variable conditions, by sight,
touch, or hearing.
- mobility_strength: getting a robot body to the work and exerting
force there: moving over terrain, heights, confined spaces, reach,
load, balance, and endurance.
- planning_reasoning: deciding what to do as conditions vary:
diagnosing, planning, handling novel cases, and combining many
sub-tasks that differ from one occurrence to the next.
- REGULATION. The law does not allow a robot to do the work. A law,
regulation, licence, certification, liability rule, or collective
agreement requires a person to do it or forbids a robot from doing
it.
- PREFERENCE. People want a person to do the work. Those who buy,
receive, or supervise it value human presence, touch, trust,
consent, or accountability, so that a robot's work is not accepted
in place of a person's even when the result is the same.
- COST. It does not pay. A robot doing the work costs more than the
labor it replaces, once purchase, integration, changes to the
workplace, operation, and maintenance are counted. This includes
work that is too small a part of any job, too infrequent, or too
scattered for a robot to pay off.

A barrier is present if, on its own, it would keep robots from doing
a significant share of this work: deployment at scale across the
economy, not the replacement of every occurrence. Judge each barrier
as it applies today.

Whether a barrier is present is a counterfactual question: would
robots do more of this work if they were more capable, if they cost
less, if the people who buy and receive the work had different
preferences, or if the rules were different? Answer each of these
against today's conditions: today's technology, today's prices,
today's buyers, and today's rules.

When no robot does this work today, the capability question is
whether today's technology is lacking for building a robot that
could do it. That no robot does the work does not by itself mean

technology, with no robot built for it because it would not pay,

does this work. Where no such robot exists, judge them as if one
did: would it pay at today's prices, do today's rules allow it, and
would today's buyers accept it?
